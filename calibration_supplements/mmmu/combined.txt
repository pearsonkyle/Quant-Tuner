## Art

Western painting is often narrated as a sequence of problems and solutions. The
Renaissance problem was depth: how to make a flat plaster wall read as a window
onto a receding world. Linear perspective, codified by Brunelleschi and written
down by Alberti in De pictura (1435), solved it with a single vanishing point and
a horizon line tied to the viewer's eye. Masaccio's Holy Trinity (c. 1427) is the
canonical demonstration — the coffered barrel vault recedes to a point at the foot
of the cross, so a standing viewer feels the chapel bore into the wall. Once depth
was solved, the next problem became light. Caravaggio's tenebrism, a century and a
half later, abandoned even illumination for a single raking source, letting most
of the canvas fall into near-black so that gesture and face emerge like a spotlight
on a stage.

The shift from Renaissance to Baroque is less a change of subject than a change of
energy. A High Renaissance composition such as Raphael's School of Athens is
balanced, frontal, and stable: figures arrange themselves in a frieze under a
symmetrical arch, and the eye rests. A Baroque composition such as Rubens' Descent
from the Cross is diagonal, off-balance, and kinetic: the dead Christ slides down a
long diagonal of white cloth, and every supporting figure strains against gravity.
The Baroque wants you to feel motion and weight; the Renaissance wants you to
contemplate order.

Impressionism is best understood as a response to two pressures — photography and
optics. Once the camera could record a likeness cheaply, the painter's monopoly on
representation broke, and the question became what painting could do that a
photograph could not. The Impressionist answer was to paint perception rather than
objects: the broken, comma-like brushstrokes of Monet record the flicker of light
on water at a particular hour, not the timeless form of a haystack. The optical
theory of the period held that the eye mixes adjacent dabs of pure color at a
distance, so a field rendered in strokes of unmixed green and violet reads as more
luminous than the same field mixed to a flat olive on the palette.

In conservation and connoisseurship, attribution leans on material evidence as
much as on style. Pigment analysis can date a work: Prussian blue does not appear
before about 1704, titanium white not before the early 20th century, so either
pigment in a panel attributed to a 17th-century master is decisive evidence of
later intervention or forgery. Canvas weave, ground preparation, craquelure
patterns, and underdrawing revealed by infrared reflectography all enter the
argument. Style alone is the weakest leg of an attribution; it persuades the eye
but not the laboratory.

## Art Theory

Formalism holds that the meaning of a work resides in its formal relationships —
line, shape, color, mass, and their arrangement — rather than in subject matter,
biography, or social context. Clement Greenberg's mid-century formalism pushed this
toward medium-specificity: each art should pursue what is unique to its own medium,
so painting should foreground flatness, the one property no other art shares. On
this reading, the trajectory from Manet to abstract expressionism is a logical
self-purification, painting shedding the illusionistic depth it had borrowed from
sculpture and theater. The obvious objection is that medium-specificity is a
historically contingent value dressed up as a law, and that it cannot account for
work whose point is precisely to cross media.

Iconography and iconology, as formulated by Erwin Panofsky, give the complementary
account: a work is legible through layers. The pre-iconographic layer is what we
see (a man, a knife, a lamb). The iconographic layer identifies conventional
subjects and stories (this is Abraham and Isaac). The iconological layer reads the
work as a symptom of a culture's deepest assumptions — its "symbolic forms." A
Panofskyan reading of a Northern Renaissance interior treats the single candle, the
convex mirror, and the discarded shoes not as décor but as a coded theology of
presence and witness.

The "death of the author," in Barthes' essay, denies that a text's meaning is
fixed by its maker's intention; meaning is produced at the moment of reading, by a
reader situated in a web of prior texts. Applied to visual art, it licenses
readings the artist never intended and dissolves the authority of the artist's own
statements. The gain is interpretive freedom; the cost, critics argue, is that it
can detach a work from the conditions that actually produced it.

Q: A critic argues that a Rothko color-field painting should be judged solely on
the interaction of its stacked rectangles of color, ignoring Rothko's stated
intentions about tragedy and the sublime. This critic is most clearly working in
which tradition?
A. Iconology   B. Formalism   C. Social art history   D. Psychoanalytic criticism
Reasoning: Judging the work on internal formal relations while bracketing the
artist's intentions and external meaning is formalism. Iconology seeks deep
cultural meaning; social art history situates the work in class and economy;
psychoanalytic criticism reads unconscious content. Answer: B.

The institutional theory of art, associated with George Dickie, sidesteps the
question "what properties make something art?" and answers instead "what confers
the status of art?" On this account an object becomes art when an agent acting on
behalf of the artworld confers upon it the status of candidate for appreciation.
Duchamp's Fountain is the test case: a mass-produced urinal becomes art not by any
visible property but by being placed, by an artist, into the artworld's circuit of
galleries, critics, and catalogues. Critics object that this is circular — the
artworld is defined by the art it certifies — but its strength is descriptive: it
explains how readymades and conceptual works can be art without sharing any
perceptual feature with painting or sculpture.

## Design

Design is constrained problem-solving, and its theory is largely a theory of those
constraints. The Gestalt principles describe how the visual system groups marks
into wholes: proximity (near elements read as a group), similarity (alike elements
group across distance), closure (the eye completes implied contours), continuity
(the eye prefers smooth paths), and figure–ground (the scene splits into object and
backdrop). A logo that works as both a positive shape and the negative space around
it — the FedEx arrow hidden between the E and x — is a deliberate exploitation of
figure–ground.

Typography carries an enormous share of a design's tone and legibility. The first
distinction is serif versus sans-serif: serifs are the small terminal strokes that
guide the eye along a line of body text and lend a traditional, editorial feel;
sans-serifs read as modern, neutral, and clean on screens. The second is the type
scale — the ratio between successive sizes. A modular scale built on a ratio such
as 1.25 (the major third) yields sizes that feel harmonious because the steps are
proportional rather than arbitrary. Leading (line spacing) is typically set to
about 1.4–1.6× the font size for body text; too tight and lines crowd, too loose
and the paragraph loses cohesion. Measure (line length) wants roughly 45–75
characters; longer lines tire the eye on the return sweep.

Color in design is usually reasoned about in HSL — hue, saturation, lightness —
because it maps better to human intuition than RGB. A palette is often built from
relationships on the wheel: complementary (opposite hues, high contrast), analogous
(neighbors, harmonious and calm), or triadic (three evenly spaced hues, vibrant but
balanced). Accessibility imposes a hard constraint: text must meet a contrast ratio
of at least 4.5:1 against its background for normal body size under WCAG AA, which
frequently overrides aesthetic preference.

The double-diamond model frames the design process as two successive
divergence-convergence cycles: first discover (diverge to explore the problem) then
define (converge on a single problem statement), then develop (diverge to generate
solutions) then deliver (converge on one to ship). Its discipline is that you must
not begin solving until you have converged on the right problem; most failed
products solve a real solution to the wrong problem.

## Music

Western tonal music is organized around a tonic, the pitch that feels like home,
and the tension-and-release of departing from and returning to it. A major scale is
a specific pattern of whole (W) and half (H) steps: W–W–H–W–W–W–H. Starting on C
that gives C D E F G A B C with no sharps or flats; starting on G the pattern forces
an F#, which is why G major has one sharp. The natural minor scale uses the pattern
W–H–W–W–H–W–W and shares a key signature with its relative major a minor third
above (A minor relates to C major).

Triads are built by stacking thirds. A major triad is root, major third, perfect
fifth (C–E–G); a minor triad lowers the third (C–E♭–G); diminished stacks two minor
thirds (C–E♭–G♭); augmented stacks two major thirds (C–E–G#). Functional harmony
assigns roles: the tonic (I) is stable, the dominant (V) is maximally tense and
pulls back to I, and the subdominant (IV) is the gentle departure. The most
characteristic gesture in tonal music is the authentic cadence V→I, and the
dominant's pull is sharpened by adding a seventh (V7), whose tritone resolves
inward to the tonic third.

Roman-numeral analysis of a common progression in C major:
  C        Am       F        G7       C
  I        vi       IV       V7       I
  The vi–IV–V7–I tail is a textbook "circle-ish" approach to a strong cadence; the
  G7 (G–B–D–F) contains the tritone B–F, which resolves B→C and F→E into the tonic.

A 12-bar blues in E, the backbone of countless rock and jazz tunes:
  | E7 | E7 | E7 | E7 | A7 | A7 | E7 | E7 | B7 | A7 | E7 | B7 |
  I   I   I   I   IV  IV  I   I   V   IV  I   V(turnaround)
  The dominant-7 quality on every chord — not just on V — is what gives the blues
  its characteristic "wrong but right" sound against the blues scale melody.

Timbre, the quality that distinguishes a violin from a flute playing the same
pitch, is governed by the overtone series and the instrument's envelope (attack,
decay, sustain, release). A struck or plucked sound has a sharp attack and rapid
decay; a bowed or blown sound can sustain indefinitely. Synthesis exploits this:
subtractive synthesis starts with a harmonically rich waveform (a sawtooth) and
carves it with filters, while additive synthesis builds a timbre by summing pure
sine partials at chosen amplitudes.

[assistant] Try Am – F – C – G (i – VI – III – VII in A natural minor). Function:

The sonata-allegro form, the dominant large-scale structure of the Classical era,
is a drama of keys. The exposition states a first theme in the tonic and a second,
contrasting theme in the dominant (or relative major), establishing a tonal
conflict. The development fragments and recombines this material, modulating
restlessly through distant keys to maximize instability. The recapitulation
restates both themes — but now the second theme appears in the tonic, resolving the
key conflict. The whole form is thus a tonal narrative: home, away, struggle, and
a homecoming in which the once-foreign material is reconciled to the tonic.

Rhythm and meter give music its temporal grid. Simple meters divide the beat into
two (2/4, 3/4, 4/4); compound meters divide it into three (6/8, 9/8, 12/8), which
is why 6/8 feels like a lilting two-pulse rather than a march of six. Syncopation —
accenting the off-beats or the weak parts of the beat — creates the forward push
central to jazz, funk, and most popular music. A polyrhythm layers conflicting
divisions, such as three-against-two, where one voice plays triplets while another
plays straight eighths, producing a shimmering composite that neither voice states
alone.

## Art (continued)

Modernism in the visual arts is a chain of refusals. Manet refused the smooth
illusionistic finish of the Salon and left his brushwork visible, insisting that a
painting is paint on a flat surface before it is a window. Cézanne refused the
single fixed viewpoint, building apples and mountains from facets seen as if the eye
moved, which handed Cubism its starting premise. Picasso and Braque then refused
the unified picture plane entirely, shattering the motif into overlapping planes so
that a guitar is presented from several angles at once on a single shallow stage.
Each refusal narrows what painting claims to do and widens what it is allowed to be.

Non-Western traditions resist the European problem-and-solution narrative. Chinese
landscape painting (shanshui) never adopted single-point perspective because it was
not trying to fix a viewer at a window; the scroll is meant to be traveled through,
its shifting "moving focus" letting the eye wander up rivers and into mist. Empty
space (liubai, "leaving white") is not unfinished background but an active element —
the unpainted silk reads as cloud, water, or sky, and the balance of mark and void
carries the meaning. Japanese ukiyo-e woodblock prints, with their flat color
fields, bold outlines, and asymmetric cropping, in turn fed directly back into
European art when they reached Paris, shaping Degas's off-center compositions and
Van Gogh's saturated planes.

Sculpture poses problems painting does not: it occupies real space, casts real
shadows, and is seen from infinitely many angles. Classical Greek sculpture solved
the problem of lifelike stance with contrapposto, shifting the figure's weight onto
one leg so the hips and shoulders tilt in opposition and the body relaxes into an
S-curve. Bernini's Baroque marble pushes the medium toward the pictorial and the
theatrical, freezing a single instant of maximum drama — Daphne's fingers sprouting
into laurel leaves — and demanding a specific vantage point from which the
transformation reads. Modern sculpture, from Brancusi's polished ovoids to Calder's
mobiles, abandons the pedestal and the human figure to investigate pure form,
balance, and, in the mobile, time and motion themselves.

## Art Theory (continued)

The "gaze," a concept imported into art theory from film and psychoanalysis,
analyzes who is positioned to look and who is positioned to be looked at. The
classical European nude, in this reading, is constructed for an implied male
spectator: the figure is turned out, displayed, and made aware of being seen, so
that the picture's real subject is the act of looking itself. The critique does not
deny the beauty of the works; it asks what relations of power the conventions
naturalize, and why so many traditions reserve the active, surveying position for
one viewer and the passive, surveyed position for another.

Walter Benjamin's "aura" names what a work of art loses when it can be perfectly
reproduced. A unique painting in a specific place at a specific time has an aura — a
presence rooted in its singular history and ritual location. Photography and
printing strip that away: a poster of the Mona Lisa is everywhere and therefore
nowhere in particular. Benjamin reads this loss ambivalently. The aura was bound up
with cult and authority, so its decay is also a democratization — reproduction pries
art loose from ritual and makes it available to the masses, opening the door to
political rather than ceremonial uses of the image.

Q: A theorist argues that a museum's lighting, labels, and hushed galleries teach
visitors to treat objects as "art" before they have looked at a single one. This is
closest to:
A. Formalism   B. A critique of the institutional framing of art   C. Iconography
D. Medium-specificity
Reasoning: Attending to how the museum-as-institution confers and shapes the
category "art" is institutional critique, the practical sibling of the institutional
theory of art. Formalism and medium-specificity attend to internal form; iconography
decodes subject matter. Answer: B.

## Design (continued)

Interaction design adds time and response to the static concerns of graphic design.
Two ideas dominate. Affordance, borrowed from perceptual psychology, is the
relationship between an object and a user's capabilities — a flat plate on a door
affords pushing, a handle affords pulling, and a door that contradicts its
affordances ("Norman doors") forces a label to compensate for a design failure.
Feedback closes the loop: every action should produce a perceptible, timely
response, or the user cannot tell whether the system heard them. A button that
depresses, a spinner during a wait, a confirmation after a save — each is feedback
that keeps the user's mental model synchronized with the system's state.

Responsive layout reasons in terms of a grid and breakpoints. Content is poured into
a fluid grid of columns whose count and width adapt to the viewport, and at chosen
breakpoints the layout reflows — a three-column desktop grid collapsing to a single
stacked column on a phone. Mobile-first design inverts the historical default,
designing the constrained small-screen experience first and progressively enhancing
it for larger screens, on the theory that it is easier to add room than to remove
content gracefully.

A minimal responsive grid expressed in CSS-like tokens:
  container: max-width 1200px; gutter 24px; columns 12
  breakpoints: sm 640px / md 768px / lg 1024px / xl 1280px
  card span: 12 cols (xs) -> 6 cols (md) -> 4 cols (lg)
  spacing scale (px): 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64   (a 4px base unit)

## Music (continued)

Counterpoint is the art of combining independent melodic lines so that each is
satisfying alone yet they cohere vertically. Species counterpoint, the pedagogical
ladder codified by Fux, builds the skill in stages: first note-against-note, then
two-against-one, then suspensions, until the student can write florid lines that
remain consonant on the strong beats and treat dissonance as passing or prepared.
The fugue is counterpoint's high form: a subject is stated alone, answered in a
related key by a second voice while the first continues in counterpoint, and then
developed through episodes and entries until the voices reconverge.

The overtone series explains both consonance and the construction of instruments. A
vibrating string sounds not only its fundamental but a ladder of harmonics at
integer multiples of that frequency; the simplest ratios (2:1 octave, 3:2 fifth,
4:3 fourth) are the most consonant because their harmonics align. Equal temperament
is the compromise that lets keyboards play in all keys: it divides the octave into
twelve equal semitones, each a frequency ratio of the twelfth root of two (≈1.0595),
deliberately detuning the pure ratios slightly so that no key is unusable and
modulation anywhere is possible.

Equal-tempered frequencies in the A4=440 reference, one octave:
  A4 440.00 | A#4 466.16 | B4 493.88 | C5 523.25 | C#5 554.37 | D5 587.33
  D#5 622.25 | E5 659.26 | F5 698.46 | F#5 739.99 | G5 783.99 | G#5 830.61 | A5 880.00
  Each step multiplies the previous by 2^(1/12); twelve steps double the frequency.

## Art (movements and materials)

The story of an artwork is partly the story of its materials. Fresco — pigment in
water painted into wet lime plaster — fuses the image into the wall as the plaster
cures, which is why fresco is durable but unforgiving: the painter must finish each
patch (giornata, a "day's work") before the plaster sets, and mistakes require
chiseling out the section. Oil paint, by contrast, dries slowly, allowing blending,
glazing, and endless revision; its translucency lets light pass through thin colored
glazes and reflect off a white ground, producing the deep luminous shadows of
Northern Renaissance panels. Tempera, pigment bound in egg yolk, dries almost
instantly and cannot be blended wet, so its modeling is built from fine cross-hatched
strokes — the reason early Italian panels have their characteristic crisp, linear
surfaces.

Romanticism, Realism, and Symbolism form a 19th-century triangle of competing
answers to "what should art depict?" Romanticism (Turner, Friedrich, Géricault)
exalted emotion, the sublime, and nature's overwhelming scale, dwarfing the human
figure beneath storms and mountains. Realism (Courbet, Millet) rejected both
classical idealization and Romantic drama, insisting on the dignity of ordinary
labor depicted at life scale and without flattery. Symbolism (Redon, Moreau) turned
inward, treating the visible world as a screen for dreams, myth, and the irrational —
a reaction against Realism's materialism and a precursor to Surrealism.

Q: A large canvas shows tiny human figures on a shore beneath an immense, turbulent
sky that fills four-fifths of the picture. The composition's scale relationship most
directly expresses which value?
A. The Realist dignity of labor   B. The Romantic sublime   C. Cubist
simultaneity   D. Minimalist reduction
Reasoning: Dwarfing humans beneath an overwhelming natural force to evoke awe and
smallness is the Romantic sublime. Realism keeps the human at dignified life scale;
Cubism fragments viewpoint; Minimalism strips content. Answer: B.

## Art Theory (semiotics and reception)

Semiotics reads images as systems of signs. A sign has a signifier (the mark, the
shape, the color) and a signified (the concept it evokes); the link between them is
largely conventional, learned within a culture, not natural. A red octagon signifies
"stop" only because a community agreed it should. Applied to painting, semiotics
distinguishes the iconic sign (which resembles its referent, like a portrait), the
indexical sign (which is physically caused by its referent, like a cast shadow or a
brushstroke that records the hand's motion), and the symbolic sign (purely
conventional, like a halo signifying sanctity). Much of a picture's meaning lives in
the interplay of these registers.

Reception theory shifts attention from the work's production to its consumption,
arguing that meaning is completed by historically situated audiences. The same
altarpiece meant one thing to a 15th-century worshipper kneeling before it in
candlelight and means another to a 21st-century tourist photographing it in a
gallery. The "horizon of expectations" a viewer brings — their prior images, beliefs,
and conventions — shapes what they can see, so the history of a work is in part the
history of its changing audiences.

## Design (systems and accessibility)

A design system is the infrastructure that lets a large product stay coherent. It
codifies tokens (color, spacing, type, radius, shadow), components (buttons, inputs,
modals) with documented states, and usage guidelines, so that hundreds of screens
built by dozens of people still feel like one product. The payoff is consistency and
speed; the risk is rigidity, where the system's defaults discourage the bespoke
solution a hard problem actually needs.

Accessible design is not a coat of paint applied at the end; it is a constraint that
shapes the whole. Beyond color contrast, it requires that every interactive element
be reachable and operable by keyboard alone, that images carry text alternatives,
that form fields have programmatically associated labels, and that motion can be
reduced for users who are sensitive to it. The discipline of designing for the
keyboard and screen reader frequently improves the experience for everyone — captions
help in noisy rooms, high contrast helps in sunlight, and clear focus states help
fast mouse users too.

## Music (form, genre, and production)

Genre is partly a set of formal conventions and partly a set of timbral and
rhythmic signatures. The 32-bar AABA song form — two statements of an eight-bar A
section, a contrasting eight-bar bridge (B), and a final A — organized the Great
American Songbook and remains audible in countless standards. Verse–chorus form, the
backbone of pop and rock, instead alternates a narrative verse with a repeated,
hook-bearing chorus, often inserting a single contrasting bridge before a final
chorus to refresh the ear. The structural logic is the same in both: establish,
contrast, return.

Music production reasons about frequency, dynamics, and space. Equalization (EQ)
shapes the balance of frequencies — cutting low-mid "mud" around 250–400 Hz to clear
a vocal, or boosting "air" above 10 kHz for sheen. Compression reduces dynamic range
by attenuating peaks above a threshold by a set ratio, then making up the gain, so a
performance sits more evenly in the mix. Reverb and delay place sounds in a virtual
space, the pre-delay and decay time cueing the size of the imagined room. The
mastering stage then balances and limits the finished mix for consistent loudness
across playback systems.

A simple drum pattern in 4/4, sixteenth-note grid (x = hit, . = rest):
  Hi-hat: x x x x x x x x x x x x x x x x
  Snare:  . . . . x . . . . . . . x . . .
  Kick:   x . . . . . . . x . x . . . . .
  The snare on beats 2 and 4 is the backbeat; the kick on 1, the "and" of 2, and 3
  gives a common rock/pop groove. Shifting a kick to a sixteenth off-beat introduces
  syncopation and a funkier feel.

## Accounting

Accounting rests on the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity.
Every transaction preserves this identity, which is the deep reason double-entry
bookkeeping works — each entry touches at least two accounts so the books stay in
balance. Debits and credits are not "good" and "bad"; they are left and right.
Assets and expenses increase with debits; liabilities, equity, and revenue increase
with credits. A sale on account, for example, debits Accounts Receivable (an asset
goes up) and credits Revenue (equity goes up through retained earnings), and the
equation holds.

The three core financial statements answer three different questions and articulate
with one another. The income statement asks "was the period profitable?" and runs
Revenue − Expenses = Net Income over an interval. The balance sheet asks "what does
the entity own and owe?" at a single instant. The cash flow statement asks "where
did the cash actually go?", reconciling accrual net income to the change in cash by
adjusting for non-cash items and working-capital swings. Net income flows into
retained earnings on the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement's ending cash
ties to the balance sheet's cash line — the statements are a linked system, not
three independent reports.

Accrual accounting recognizes revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are
incurred, regardless of when cash moves; this is the core of the matching principle,
which pairs the costs of generating revenue with that revenue in the same period.
Depreciation embodies this: a machine bought for cash today is not expensed all at
once but allocated over its useful life, matching the cost to the periods that
benefit from it. The result is that profit and cash can diverge sharply — a
fast-growing firm can be profitable on the income statement yet starved for cash as
it funds receivables and inventory.

A simplified income statement (figures in $000s):
  Revenue                         4,200
  Cost of goods sold             (2,520)
  Gross profit                    1,680    (gross margin 40.0%)
  Operating expenses               (980)
  Operating income (EBIT)           700
  Interest expense                  (90)
  Pre-tax income                    610
  Income tax (21%)                 (128)
  Net income                        482    (net margin 11.5%)

## Economics

Economics begins with scarcity and the idea that every choice has an opportunity
cost — the value of the next-best alternative forgone. The supply-and-demand model
is the workhorse: the demand curve slopes down (lower price, more quantity
demanded), the supply curve slopes up (higher price, more quantity supplied), and
the market clears where they cross, at the equilibrium price and quantity. Shifts of
the whole curve (from changing income, input costs, tastes, or the number of buyers
and sellers) are distinct from movements along a curve (caused only by a change in
the good's own price) — conflating the two is the most common analytical error.

Elasticity measures responsiveness. Price elasticity of demand is the percentage
change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price; demand is
elastic (|E| > 1) when buyers are sensitive (luxuries, many substitutes) and
inelastic (|E| < 1) when they are not (necessities, addictive goods, no substitutes).
Elasticity drives revenue strategy: raising the price of an inelastic good raises
total revenue, while raising the price of an elastic good lowers it, because the
quantity lost outweighs the higher price.

Market structures sit on a spectrum from perfect competition to monopoly. Under
perfect competition, many firms sell an identical product, no one has pricing power,
and price equals marginal cost in the long run, yielding allocative efficiency. A
monopoly, facing the whole downward-sloping demand curve, restricts output and sets
price above marginal cost, capturing producer surplus and creating deadweight loss.
Between them lie monopolistic competition (many firms, differentiated products) and
oligopoly (a few interdependent firms whose strategic interaction is modeled with
game theory).

Macroeconomics studies aggregates: GDP, unemployment, and inflation. Fiscal policy
(government spending and taxation) and monetary policy (central-bank control of
interest rates and the money supply) are the two levers for stabilizing the business
cycle. A central bank fighting inflation raises its policy rate, which raises
borrowing costs, cools investment and consumption, and slows price growth — at the
risk of raising unemployment, the trade-off sketched by the short-run Phillips curve.

## Finance

Finance is built on the time value of money: a dollar today is worth more than a
dollar tomorrow because today's dollar can be invested to earn a return. The present
value of a future cash flow is PV = FV / (1 + r)^n, and the present value of a stream
of cash flows is the sum of each discounted to today. Net present value (NPV) sums
the discounted cash flows of a project net of its initial cost; the decision rule is
simple and powerful — accept any project with positive NPV, because it adds value
beyond the cost of capital.

Risk and return are linked. The Capital Asset Pricing Model expresses the required
return on an asset as the risk-free rate plus a risk premium proportional to the
asset's beta — its sensitivity to overall market moves: E[R] = Rf + β(E[Rm] − Rf).
Diversification reduces the firm-specific (idiosyncratic) portion of risk for free,
which is why CAPM rewards only the undiversifiable (systematic) risk captured by
beta. The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) blends the after-tax cost of debt
and the cost of equity by their market weights, and it is the standard discount rate
for a firm's projects of average risk.

Valuation of a business often uses discounted cash flow (DCF): project free cash
flows for an explicit horizon, estimate a terminal value capturing everything
beyond, discount both to the present at WACC, and net out debt to reach equity value.
The terminal value, frequently computed with the Gordon growth model TV = FCF×(1+g) /
(WACC − g), typically dominates the result, which is why small changes in the assumed
perpetual growth rate g or discount rate swing the valuation dramatically — DCF is
only as good as its long-run assumptions.

A two-stage discounting illustration (discount rate 10%):
  Year 1 FCF 120  -> PV 120 / 1.10^1   = 109.09
  Year 2 FCF 132  -> PV 132 / 1.10^2   = 109.09
  Year 3 FCF 145  -> PV 145 / 1.10^3   = 108.94
  Terminal (g=2%): TV = 145*1.02/(0.10-0.02) = 1,848.75 -> PV 1,848.75/1.10^3 = 1,388.99
  Enterprise value = 109.09 + 109.09 + 108.94 + 1,388.99 = 1,716.11

## Management

Management is the coordination of people and resources toward goals, and its theory
has swung between treating organizations as machines and as organisms. Scientific
management (Taylor) optimized tasks through time-and-motion study, maximizing
efficiency by standardizing the one best way to do each job. The human relations
movement (following the Hawthorne studies) countered that motivation, attention, and
social dynamics drive productivity as much as task design. Modern practice blends the
two: process discipline where work is repetitive and predictable, autonomy and
intrinsic motivation where work is creative and uncertain.

Strategy frameworks structure the question "where and how should we compete?"
Porter's Five Forces analyzes an industry's structural attractiveness through the
threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of suppliers and of buyers, the threat
of substitutes, and the intensity of rivalry. A SWOT analysis maps internal
strengths and weaknesses against external opportunities and threats. Porter's generic
strategies argue a firm must choose a clear position — cost leadership or
differentiation — and that being "stuck in the middle," neither cheapest nor most
distinctive, is the path to mediocre returns.

Organizational behavior studies motivation, teams, and leadership. Maslow's
hierarchy and Herzberg's two-factor theory distinguish hygiene factors (pay,
conditions) whose absence demotivates from motivators (achievement, growth) whose
presence energizes. Goal-setting theory finds that specific, challenging goals
outperform vague "do your best" exhortations, provided there is feedback and
commitment. Leadership styles — from transactional (exchange of reward for
performance) to transformational (inspiring a shared vision) — suit different
contexts, and contingency theories hold that the best style depends on the task,
the team's maturity, and the environment.

Operations management turns inputs into outputs reliably and cheaply. Lean thinking,
born at Toyota, attacks waste (muda) — overproduction, waiting, excess inventory,
defects — and pulls work through the system based on actual demand rather than
pushing it on a forecast. The theory of constraints argues that a system's
throughput is set by its single tightest bottleneck, so improvements anywhere else
are illusory until the constraint is elevated. Quality methods like Six Sigma reduce
process variation toward defect rates measured in parts per million.

ocean
Reasoning: Failing to commit to either cost leadership or differentiation, and

ticket lead time, not by any single step's utilization.

## Marketing

Marketing manages the relationship between an offering and a market, classically
organized as the 4 Ps: Product (what is offered and how it is differentiated), Price
(the value capture and positioning signal), Place (the distribution channels that
make it available), and Promotion (the communication that builds awareness and
demand). Modern service-oriented frameworks extend this to 7 Ps, adding People,
Process, and Physical evidence, recognizing that for services the experience and the
people delivering it are inseparable from the product.

Segmentation, targeting, and positioning (STP) is the strategic core. Segmentation
divides a heterogeneous market into groups with similar needs (by demography,
geography, behavior, or psychographics). Targeting selects which segments to serve
based on their size, growth, and fit with the firm's strengths. Positioning then
crafts a distinctive place in the target's mind, usually articulated as a
positioning statement: for [target] who [need], [brand] is the [category] that
[key benefit], because [reason to believe]. A muddled position — trying to be
everything to everyone — is the most common cause of weak brands.

The customer journey reframes marketing around the buyer's process rather than the
seller's funnel: awareness, consideration, purchase, retention, and advocacy. Metrics
follow the journey — customer acquisition cost (CAC) measures the price of winning a
customer, customer lifetime value (LTV) the total margin they generate, and the LTV/CAC
ratio (a rule of thumb wants it above ~3) gauges whether growth is economically sound.
A business that acquires customers for more than they are worth is buying revenue at a
loss, however fast it grows.

Digital marketing adds measurability and experimentation. A/B testing randomly
assigns users to variants and compares a metric (conversion rate, click-through) with
a test of statistical significance, so design choices are decided by data rather than
opinion. Attribution models assign credit for a conversion across the touchpoints
that preceded it — last-touch is simple but overcredits the final step, while
multi-touch models distribute credit more fairly across the journey.

## Accounting (managerial and ratios)

Where financial accounting reports to outsiders, managerial accounting informs
internal decisions, and its central tool is cost behavior. Costs split into fixed
(unchanged with volume, like rent) and variable (scaling with each unit, like
materials). The contribution margin — price minus variable cost per unit — is what
each sale contributes toward covering fixed costs and then profit. The break-even
volume is fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit: below it the firm
loses money, above it each additional unit drops its full contribution margin to the
bottom line, which is the source of operating leverage.

Ratio analysis distills statements into comparable signals. Liquidity ratios (current
ratio = current assets / current liabilities) gauge short-term solvency. Profitability
ratios (return on equity = net income / equity) gauge how hard shareholder capital
works. Leverage ratios (debt-to-equity) gauge financial risk. The DuPont decomposition
splits ROE into net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier, revealing whether
returns come from fat margins, efficient asset use, or simply borrowing — three very
different stories behind the same headline number.

Q: A product sells for $50 with $30 variable cost; fixed costs are $120,000. The

A. 2,400 units   B. 4,000 units   C. 6,000 units   D. 2,000 units
Reasoning: Contribution margin per unit = $50 − $30 = $20. Break-even = fixed costs /
CM = $120,000 / $20 = 6,000 units. Answer: C.

## Economics (macro, game theory, and externalities)

Game theory models strategic interaction where each player's best move depends on
the others'. The prisoner's dilemma is its emblem: two parties would both be better
off cooperating, but each has a dominant incentive to defect, so rational play yields
a worse outcome for both — a Nash equilibrium that is not Pareto-efficient. This
single structure explains arms races, price wars, overfishing, and the difficulty of
climate agreements: individually rational choices aggregate to a collectively bad
result, which is why enforceable cooperation (contracts, regulation, repeated
relationships) creates value.

Externalities are costs or benefits that fall on third parties not party to a
transaction. A factory that pollutes a river imposes a negative externality; its
private cost is below the social cost, so the market overproduces. The standard
remedies internalize the externality: a Pigouvian tax set equal to the marginal
external cost makes the polluter face the true social cost, while a cap-and-trade
system fixes the total quantity and lets a market price the right to emit. Public
goods — non-rival and non-excludable, like national defense or basic research — are
the mirror image: markets underprovide them because no one can be charged, which is
the textbook justification for public funding.

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, eroding purchasing power.
Demand-pull inflation arises when aggregate demand outruns capacity; cost-push
inflation arises when input costs (energy, wages) rise across the economy. Central
banks target low, stable inflation (often ~2%) because both extremes are costly:
high inflation distorts saving and contracts, while deflation raises the real burden
of debt and can trap an economy in postponed spending. The real interest rate —
nominal rate minus expected inflation — is what actually governs saving and
investment decisions.

Reasoning: Cap-and-trade sets a hard quantity cap on emissions and lets firms trade

Reasoning: Defection is each player's dominant strategy (it pays more regardless of

## Finance (fixed income, options, portfolios)

A bond is a promise of fixed cash flows, and its price moves inversely to interest
rates: when market yields rise, the fixed coupons are worth less, so the price falls.
Duration measures this sensitivity — the approximate percentage price change for a 1%
change in yield — and is longer for bonds with lower coupons and longer maturities.
Yield to maturity is the single discount rate that sets the present value of a bond's
cash flows equal to its market price; it is the bond's internal rate of return if
held to maturity and all coupons are reinvested at that rate.

Options give the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a
strike price. A call's value rises with the underlying price, volatility, and time to
expiry; the Black–Scholes model prices European options under assumptions of
lognormal returns and constant volatility. The key intuition is that an option is a
leveraged, asymmetric bet: the buyer's loss is capped at the premium while the upside
is large, which is why options are used both to speculate and to hedge — a put acts as
insurance on a long position.

Portfolio theory (Markowitz) shows that combining assets whose returns are not
perfectly correlated reduces risk for a given expected return. The efficient frontier
is the set of portfolios offering the maximum expected return for each level of risk;
adding a risk-free asset turns the optimal risky portfolio into the tangency
portfolio, and investors choose where to sit on the resulting capital market line by
their risk tolerance. The free lunch of diversification is the foundational result of
modern finance: idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away, so the market does not
reward it.

A small bond cash-flow schedule (face $1,000, 5% annual coupon, 3-year, YTM 6%):
  Year 1 coupon 50  -> PV 50 / 1.06^1   = 47.17
  Year 2 coupon 50  -> PV 50 / 1.06^2   = 44.50
  Year 3 coupon+face 1,050 -> PV 1,050 / 1.06^3 = 881.60
  Price = 47.17 + 44.50 + 881.60 = 973.27   (trades at a discount because coupon < YTM)

## Management (decision-making and change)

Decision-making under uncertainty is a core managerial skill, and good practice
separates the quality of a decision from the quality of its outcome — a sound bet can
lose, a reckless one can win, and judging only by results trains the wrong instincts.
Tools include expected-value reasoning (probability-weighted payoffs), decision trees
that lay out sequential choices and chance events, and explicit consideration of base
rates to counter the cognitive biases — anchoring, confirmation, sunk-cost — that
systematically distort judgment. The sunk-cost fallacy in particular destroys value:
money already spent is irrecoverable and should never influence a forward-looking
choice.

Change management addresses the human side of transformation, which is where most
strategies fail. Kotter's model sequences change as establishing urgency, building a
guiding coalition, forming a vision, communicating it relentlessly, removing
obstacles, generating short-term wins, consolidating gains, and anchoring the change
in the culture. The recurring lesson is that resistance is rarely irrational — it
reflects real costs and uncertainties for the people affected — so change succeeds
when leaders address those concerns directly rather than overpowering them.

Q: A manager argues the team must continue a failing two-year project because
"we've already invested $2M and can't waste it." This reasoning exhibits:
A. Opportunity-cost thinking   B. The sunk-cost fallacy   C. Expected-value
maximization   D. Base-rate neglect
Reasoning: The $2M is already spent and unrecoverable; letting it justify further
investment is the sunk-cost fallacy. The correct test is whether the remaining
spend has positive expected value going forward, independent of past outlays.
Answer: B.

## Marketing (brand, pricing, and lifecycle)

Brand equity is the premium a name commands beyond the functional product — the
reason a customer pays more for, or chooses faster, a branded good over an identical
generic. It is built from awareness, perceived quality, associations, and loyalty,
and it compounds: a strong brand lowers customer acquisition cost, supports premium
pricing, and creates resilience against competitors and missteps. Brands are managed
through consistency of identity and experience over time, which is why the
positioning, once chosen, must be defended against the constant temptation to chase
every adjacent opportunity.

Pricing strategy signals positioning and captures value. Cost-plus pricing marks up
unit cost by a target margin — simple but blind to what customers will pay.
Value-based pricing sets price by the worth delivered to the customer, capturing more
of the surplus the product creates. Penetration pricing enters low to win share and
build scale; price skimming enters high to capture early adopters' willingness to pay
before lowering it. Psychological pricing ($9.99 vs $10) and price anchoring (showing
a premium tier to make the middle look reasonable) exploit predictable quirks in how
buyers perceive value.

The product life cycle — introduction, growth, maturity, decline — implies different
marketing priorities at each stage: build awareness at introduction, build share and
differentiation during growth, defend share and optimize margin at maturity, and
harvest or reposition in decline. Mismatching tactics to stage is a common error,
such as spending heavily on awareness for a mature product whose buyers already know
it and instead need a reason to prefer it.

Reasoning: Presenting a high-priced option to reframe a lower option as reasonable is

to your cost.

## Basic Medical Science

Physiology is the study of how the body's systems maintain life, and its master
theme is homeostasis — the regulation of internal variables within narrow limits
through feedback. The cardiovascular system circulates blood: the heart's left
ventricle pumps oxygenated blood through the systemic arteries to the tissues, where
capillaries exchange gases and nutrients, and veins return deoxygenated blood to the
right heart, which sends it to the lungs. Cardiac output — the volume pumped per
minute — equals heart rate times stroke volume, and blood pressure is the product of
cardiac output and total peripheral resistance, the two levers the body adjusts to
perfuse organs.

The nervous system is the body's fast signaling network. Neurons transmit electrical
impulses (action potentials) along axons and communicate across synapses by releasing
neurotransmitters. The action potential is an all-or-nothing wave of membrane
depolarization driven by voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels; the resting
membrane potential it springs from is maintained by the sodium–potassium pump. The
autonomic nervous system regulates involuntary functions through two opposing arms:
the sympathetic ("fight or flight") raises heart rate and dilates airways, while the
parasympathetic ("rest and digest") does the reverse.

The endocrine system signals more slowly through hormones carried in the blood. Many
hormones are governed by negative feedback loops centered on the hypothalamus and
pituitary: the hypothalamus releases factors that drive pituitary hormones, which
drive target glands, whose products feed back to suppress the upstream signals. Insulin
and glucagon from the pancreas regulate blood glucose in opposition — insulin lowers it
by promoting uptake and storage, glucagon raises it by mobilizing stored glycogen — a
balance whose failure defines diabetes.

The immune system defends against pathogens in two tiers. Innate immunity is fast and
nonspecific — physical barriers, phagocytes, and inflammation. Adaptive immunity is
slower but specific and remembering: B cells produce antibodies against particular
antigens, and T cells kill infected cells or coordinate the response. Immunological
memory — the basis of vaccination — lets a second exposure trigger a faster, stronger
response, which is why a vaccine that presents a harmless piece of a pathogen can
confer lasting protection.

[user] Explain why someone hyperventilating into a paper bag is sometimes advised, in
terms of blood chemistry.
[assistant] Hyperventilation blows off CO2 faster than the body produces it, lowering
arterial CO2 (hypocapnia). Since CO2 forms carbonic acid in blood, losing it raises
blood pH — a respiratory alkalosis — which causes the classic symptoms (tingling,
lightheadedness, carpopedal spasm) by lowering ionized calcium. Rebreathing exhaled
air from a bag raises the inhaled CO2 back toward normal, correcting the pH. The
important caveat: the paper-bag method is risky if the cause is actually a heart
attack, asthma, or hypoxia rather than anxiety, because it also lowers available
oxygen — which is why current guidance favors paced breathing over the bag. This is
background physiology, not first-aid instruction.

## Clinical Medicine

Clinical reasoning moves from undifferentiated complaint to diagnosis to management,
and its discipline is the differential diagnosis — a ranked list of conditions that
could explain the findings, weighed by probability and by danger. The clinician
gathers a history and examination, forms a prioritized differential, orders tests to
confirm or exclude, and treats. A guiding maxim is to consider both the common
("when you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras") and the dangerous (the
can't-miss diagnoses that kill if overlooked), because the cost of a missed
myocardial infarction is not symmetric with the cost of a missed cold.

Common chronic diseases illustrate the model. Hypertension, often asymptomatic, is
diagnosed by repeated elevated blood-pressure readings and treated to prevent stroke,
heart, and kidney damage. Type 2 diabetes is diagnosed by elevated fasting glucose or
HbA1c (a measure of average glucose over ~3 months) and managed to prevent vascular
and nerve complications. The recurring clinical logic is that many chronic diseases
are silent, so they are found by screening and treated to prevent future harm rather
than to relieve present symptoms.

Acute presentations demand pattern recognition under time pressure. Chest pain
triggers a rapid workup for life threats — heart attack, pulmonary embolism, aortic
dissection — before benign causes are accepted. The "ABC" priorities (airway,
breathing, circulation) order resuscitation so that the most rapidly fatal problem is
addressed first. Evidence-based medicine grounds these decisions in clinical trials
and systematic reviews rather than anecdote, while acknowledging that guidelines must
be individualized to the patient in front of you.

## Diagnostics & Laboratory Medicine

Diagnostic testing is the quantitative backbone of medicine, and interpreting a test
requires understanding its operating characteristics. Sensitivity is the proportion of
truly diseased people the test correctly flags (few false negatives); specificity is
the proportion of truly healthy people it correctly clears (few false positives). A
highly sensitive test is good for ruling out disease when negative (SnNout); a highly
specific test is good for ruling in disease when positive (SpPin). Crucially, the
predictive value of a result depends on the disease's prevalence — a positive on a
rare disease is often a false positive even with an excellent test, the base-rate
effect that surprises clinicians and patients alike.

Laboratory hematology and chemistry translate a blood sample into actionable numbers.
The complete blood count reports red cells (and hemoglobin, low in anemia), white
cells (high in infection), and platelets (clotting). The basic metabolic panel reports
electrolytes (sodium, potassium), kidney markers (creatinine, urea), and glucose.
Reference ranges are statistically defined as the central 95% of a healthy population,
which means about 1 in 20 healthy people will fall outside any single range by chance —
a reason not to over-react to a lone mildly abnormal value.

Medical imaging visualizes anatomy and pathology by different physical principles.
X-ray and CT use ionizing radiation and excel at bone and dense structures; ultrasound
uses sound waves, is radiation-free, and is ideal for soft tissue and real-time
guidance; MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves for superb soft-tissue contrast
without radiation but more slowly and expensively. Choosing a modality balances the
diagnostic question, speed, cost, and the harm of radiation, especially in children
and pregnancy.

## Pharmacy

Pharmacology divides into pharmacokinetics — what the body does to a drug — and
pharmacodynamics — what the drug does to the body. Pharmacokinetics is summarized by
ADME: absorption (how the drug enters the bloodstream), distribution (how it spreads
into tissues), metabolism (chemical transformation, mostly in the liver by cytochrome
P450 enzymes), and excretion (removal, mostly by the kidneys). These determine the
dose and dosing interval needed to keep the drug concentration in the therapeutic
window — above the minimum effective level but below the toxic threshold.

Pharmacodynamics describes the drug–target interaction. An agonist binds a receptor
and activates it; an antagonist binds and blocks it without activating. Potency is the
dose needed for an effect; efficacy is the maximum effect achievable. The therapeutic
index — the ratio of the toxic dose to the effective dose — measures safety margin; a
drug like warfarin with a narrow therapeutic index requires careful monitoring because
the gap between helpful and harmful is small.

Half-life — the time for the plasma concentration to fall by half — governs dosing.
After about four to five half-lives a drug is essentially cleared, and with repeated
dosing it reaches steady state in the same number of half-lives. Drug interactions are
a major safety concern: one drug can inhibit or induce the enzymes that metabolize
another, raising or lowering its levels, and additive effects (two sedatives) can be
dangerous even when each alone is safe. Renal or hepatic impairment slows clearance,
requiring dose adjustment.

## Public Health

Public health protects and improves the health of populations rather than treating
individuals one at a time, and epidemiology is its core science — the study of how
disease distributes and what determines it. Incidence measures new cases over a
period; prevalence measures existing cases at a point in time. A chronic disease can
have low incidence yet high prevalence because cases accumulate, while a short, acute
illness can have high incidence yet low prevalence — distinguishing the two is
essential for planning services.

Infectious-disease control hinges on transmission dynamics. The basic reproduction
number R0 is the average number of secondary infections one case causes in a fully
susceptible population; an epidemic grows when the effective reproduction number
exceeds 1 and shrinks below it. Herd immunity is reached when a large enough fraction
of the population is immune that chains of transmission collapse; the threshold rises
with R0, which is why highly contagious diseases like measles require very high
vaccination coverage to prevent outbreaks.

Study designs trade rigor against feasibility. The randomized controlled trial is the
gold standard for causal inference because randomization balances confounders, but it
is costly and sometimes unethical. Observational designs — cohort studies (follow
exposed and unexposed forward) and case–control studies (compare diseased and healthy
backward) — are cheaper and feasible for rare diseases or harmful exposures but are
vulnerable to confounding and bias. The hierarchy of evidence, with systematic reviews
of trials at the top, ranks designs by how strongly they support causal claims.

Prevention is layered. Primary prevention stops disease before it starts (vaccination,
clean water, anti-smoking policy); secondary prevention detects it early when treatable
(screening); tertiary prevention limits the damage of established disease
(rehabilitation). The greatest historical gains in life expectancy came not from
clinical medicine but from public-health primary prevention — sanitation, nutrition,
and immunization — a reminder that population-level measures often outperform
individual treatment.

## Basic Medical Science (anatomy and metabolism)

Anatomy provides the structural map on which physiology runs. The body is organized
hierarchically — cells form tissues (epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous),
tissues form organs, and organs form systems. Anatomical position and directional
terms give a precise, unambiguous language: superior/inferior (up/down), anterior/
posterior (front/back), medial/lateral (toward/away from the midline), and proximal/
distal (near/far along a limb). This vocabulary lets a clinician describe a lesion's
location reproducibly, independent of how the patient is lying.

Metabolism is the sum of the body's chemical reactions, split into catabolism
(breaking molecules down to release energy) and anabolism (building molecules,
consuming energy). The central currency is ATP, produced mainly in mitochondria.
Glucose is catabolized through glycolysis to pyruvate, then through the citric acid
cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, which together extract far more ATP per glucose
in the presence of oxygen than glycolysis alone. When oxygen is scarce, cells fall
back on anaerobic glycolysis, producing lactate — the basis of muscle fatigue during
intense exercise.

## Clinical Medicine (organ systems and management)

Respiratory medicine illustrates how structure, function, and disease connect.
Asthma is reversible airway narrowing from inflammation and bronchospasm, presenting
with wheeze and breathlessness and treated with bronchodilators and inhaled
corticosteroids. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, usually from smoking, is
largely irreversible airflow limitation. The shared clinical thread is that
obstructive diseases impair exhalation, lowering the ratio of forced expiratory
volume in one second to forced vital capacity — the spirometric signature that
distinguishes them from restrictive diseases, where the lungs fill less but empty
proportionally.

Infectious disease management balances the benefit of antibiotics against the
collective harm of resistance. Antibiotics treat bacterial, not viral, infections,
so prescribing them for a viral cold is both useless and harmful — it breeds
resistant organisms without helping the patient. Antimicrobial stewardship matches
the narrowest effective agent to the identified or likely organism, for the shortest
effective course, precisely to slow the evolution of resistance that threatens to
return medicine to a pre-antibiotic era.

## Diagnostics & Laboratory Medicine (ECG and microbiology)

The electrocardiogram records the heart's electrical activity as a tracing whose
waveforms map to the cardiac cycle: the P wave is atrial depolarization, the QRS
complex is ventricular depolarization, and the T wave is ventricular repolarization.
Rate, rhythm, axis, and the morphology and intervals of these waves let a reader
detect arrhythmias, conduction blocks, and signs of ischemia. ST-segment elevation,
for instance, can indicate an acute myocardial infarction — one of the highest-stakes
pattern recognitions in all of diagnostics.

Clinical microbiology identifies the organism causing an infection so therapy can be
targeted. A Gram stain rapidly sorts bacteria into Gram-positive (purple, thick cell
wall) and Gram-negative (pink), narrowing the possibilities within minutes. Culture
grows the organism for definitive identification and for antibiotic susceptibility
testing, which reports which drugs will work — the data that converts empiric therapy
into targeted therapy and underpins stewardship.

preceded by a normal P wave, at a rate of about 75 beats per minute.

## Pharmacy (drug classes and dispensing)

Major drug classes are organized by mechanism. Beta-blockers slow the heart and lower
blood pressure by blocking sympathetic beta receptors; ACE inhibitors lower blood
pressure by blocking the conversion of angiotensin I to the vasoconstrictor
angiotensin II; statins lower cholesterol by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase, the
rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis. Knowing the mechanism predicts both
the therapeutic effect and the characteristic adverse effects — ACE inhibitors, for
example, commonly cause a dry cough by raising bradykinin.

Safe dispensing is a systems problem as much as a chemistry one. The "five rights" —
right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time — structure the checks
that prevent medication errors, the cause of substantial preventable harm. Look-alike,
sound-alike drug names and tenfold dosing errors (a misplaced decimal) are recurring
hazards, which is why doses are double-checked and why weight-based pediatric dosing
is verified independently.

## Public Health (screening and biostatistics)

Screening asymptomatic people for disease is only worthwhile under specific
conditions, classically the Wilson–Jungner criteria: the disease must be an important
health problem with a recognizable early stage, there must be an acceptable test and
an effective treatment that works better when started early, and the benefits must
outweigh the harms and costs. Screening that flags disease for which there is no
better-when-earlier treatment causes anxiety and overdiagnosis without saving lives —
which is why expanding a screening program is a careful evidence question, not an
automatic good.

Biostatistics quantifies the certainty of public-health claims. A relative risk or
odds ratio above 1 signals increased risk in the exposed group, but its confidence
interval matters as much as the point estimate: an interval that crosses 1 is
compatible with no effect. Statistical significance (a small p-value) is not the same
as clinical importance — with a large enough sample, a trivial effect can be
"significant," so effect size and absolute risk reduction, not just p-values, drive
sound policy.

Q: A screening program detects many early cancers that would never have caused

Reasoning: Detecting and treating disease that would never have harmed the patient is
overdiagnosis, a key harm of screening. Lead-time bias is the apparent survival gain

description here is overdiagnosis. Answer: B.

## History

History is not a list of dates but an argument about cause, change, and continuity
over time. The historian works from primary sources — documents, artifacts, and
testimony produced in the period under study — and secondary sources that interpret
them, always asking who produced a source, for whom, and why, because every source
carries the bias of its origin. Historiography, the history of historical writing,
reminds us that interpretations shift with each generation: the same revolution is
narrated as a triumph of liberty, a class struggle, or a contingent accident
depending on the historian's framework and moment.

Causation in history is layered. Long-term structural causes (economic systems,
demographic pressure, geography) set the stage; medium-term conjunctural causes
(political crises, ideological movements) build tension; and short-term triggers (a
specific event or decision) ignite change. The outbreak of the First World War
illustrates this: decades of imperial rivalry and alliance systems were the structure,
rising nationalism and an arms race the conjuncture, and the assassination at Sarajevo
the trigger. Reducing such an event to a single cause is the most common historical
error; explaining it requires weaving the levels together.

Periodization — dividing the past into eras like "medieval" or "early modern" — is a
tool, not a natural fact. It clarifies by grouping, but it distorts by imposing sharp
boundaries on gradual change and by privileging the perspective from which the scheme
was drawn. The "Renaissance" looks like a rebirth from a European vantage but ignores
flourishing elsewhere; the "fall of Rome" was, for many provincials, a slow
transformation rather than a single collapse. Good history holds its categories
lightly.

The major transformations of the modern world recur as themes. The Industrial
Revolution shifted economies from agrarian and artisanal to mechanized and factory-
based, reshaping where and how people lived and creating new classes and conflicts.
The Atlantic revolutions exported ideas of popular sovereignty and rights. Imperialism
bound distant societies into unequal global systems whose legacies persist.
Decolonization and the world wars redrew the political map and the moral imagination
of the twentieth century. In each, the historian's task is to explain not just what
changed but why it changed when and where it did.

## Literature

Literary study reads texts closely for how meaning is made, not just what is said.
Close reading attends to diction, imagery, syntax, sound, and structure — the choices
that distinguish literature from mere information. A metaphor asserts an identity
between unlike things ("the world is a stage"); a simile compares with "like" or "as";
symbolism lets a concrete object carry abstract meaning. Irony — saying one thing and
meaning another, or a gap between expectation and outcome — is among the most powerful
literary devices because it makes the reader an active interpreter rather than a
passive recipient.

Genre shapes expectation. Tragedy traces a protagonist's fall, often through a flaw
(hamartia) and a reversal (peripeteia) that yields recognition (anagnorisis); the
classical aim is catharsis, the purging of pity and fear. Comedy moves from disorder
to harmony, often ending in marriage or reconciliation. The novel, the dominant modern
form, gives interiority and social breadth, while lyric poetry compresses a single
charged moment of feeling or perception. Knowing a work's genre tells the reader which
conventions are in play and therefore where to look for meaning.

Narrative technique determines how a story reaches us. Point of view — first person
(intimate but limited and possibly unreliable), third-person limited, or omniscient —
controls what we know and trust. An unreliable narrator, whose account we learn to
doubt, turns reading into detective work. Structure matters too: a story told out of
chronological order, or framed as a story within a story, foregrounds the act of
telling itself and asks why this teller arranges events this way.

Literary theory supplies lenses. Formalism and New Criticism treat the text as a
self-contained verbal artifact. Marxist criticism reads literature as shaped by and
revealing class and economic relations. Feminist and postcolonial criticism ask whose
voices a text centers and whose it marginalizes. Reader-response theory locates meaning
in the encounter between text and reader. No single lens exhausts a rich text; each
reveals what the others obscure.

## Sociology

Sociology studies society as a structured system rather than a collection of
individuals, and its founding insight (the "sociological imagination") is that
personal troubles are often public issues — a single person's unemployment is a private
hardship, but mass unemployment is a structural fact requiring structural explanation.
The discipline distinguishes the macro level (institutions, classes, whole societies)
from the micro level (face-to-face interaction), and its great debate is how much
human behavior is shaped by social structure versus individual agency.

Three classical perspectives still organize the field. Functionalism sees society as a
system of interdependent parts, each contributing to stability, and asks what function
a given institution serves. Conflict theory, descended from Marx, sees society as an
arena of struggle over scarce resources and power, in which institutions serve
dominant groups. Symbolic interactionism works at the micro level, examining how shared
meanings are created and negotiated in everyday interaction. Each perspective is a
different question, not merely a different answer — they illuminate different facets of
the same social fact.

Socialization is the lifelong process by which people internalize the norms, values,
and roles of their culture, through family, school, peers, and media. It explains both
social reproduction — how inequalities persist across generations — and the apparent
naturalness of arrangements that are in fact learned. Concepts like role, status,
norm, and deviance let sociologists analyze how expectations govern behavior and how
societies define and respond to rule-breaking, which itself reveals their underlying
values.

Stratification — the structured ranking of groups by wealth, power, and prestige — is
a central concern. Social mobility measures movement between strata; societies that
celebrate opportunity often show less mobility than their ideology claims, because
advantages compound. Sociologists examine how class, race, and gender intersect to
shape life chances, and how institutions can entrench or reduce inequality — questions
that connect the discipline directly to policy.

## Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior, and its history is a sequence
of dominant paradigms. Behaviorism insisted psychology study only observable behavior,
explaining learning through classical conditioning (Pavlov: a neutral stimulus paired
with a meaningful one comes to evoke the response) and operant conditioning (Skinner:
behavior is shaped by its consequences — reinforcement increases it, punishment
decreases it). The cognitive revolution reopened the "black box" of the mind, modeling
attention, memory, and reasoning as information processing, and cognitive psychology
now dominates much of the field.

Memory is reconstructive, not a recording. Information passes from sensory memory to a
limited-capacity working memory and, if processed deeply, to long-term memory; retrieval
reconstructs the memory each time, which is why memories are malleable and eyewitness
testimony is fallible. The serial-position effect (better recall of the first and last
items in a list), the spacing effect (distributed practice beats cramming), and
encoding specificity (recall is better when context matches encoding) are robust
findings with direct implications for learning.

Cognitive biases are systematic departures from rational judgment, mapped especially by
Kahneman and Tversky. Confirmation bias leads us to seek evidence that fits our beliefs;
availability heuristic makes vivid, easily recalled events seem more probable; anchoring
ties estimates to an initial number. These are not random errors but predictable
products of mental shortcuts (heuristics) that are usually efficient but fail in
characteristic ways — a "fast" intuitive system and a "slow" deliberate system, in dual-
process terms.

Developmental and social psychology widen the lens. Piaget mapped stages of cognitive
development in children; attachment theory studies how early bonds shape later
relationships. Social psychology demonstrates the power of the situation: conformity
(Asch), obedience to authority (Milgram), and the fundamental attribution error — our
tendency to explain others' behavior by their character while excusing our own by
circumstance. The recurring lesson is that behavior is driven far more by context than
our intuitions credit.

## History (methods and themes)

Quantitative and social history broadened the discipline beyond kings and battles to
the lives of ordinary people, using parish registers, tax rolls, and census data to
reconstruct demography, family, and work. The Annales school in France shifted
attention to the longue durée — the slow rhythms of climate, geography, and mental
structures that change over centuries — arguing that the surface of political events
rides on these deeper currents. This widened the historian's evidence to include
material culture, oral testimony, and statistics, and made the experience of the
marginalized a legitimate, central subject.

Two persistent dangers shape historical method. Anachronism imposes the assumptions
of the present onto the past — judging a medieval figure by modern values, or reading
modern concepts into societies that lacked them. Presentism, its cousin, distorts the
past by treating it merely as a prelude to the present, as if everything led inevitably
to now. Good history practices empathy without endorsement: it reconstructs how people
in the past understood their own world on their own terms, while still subjecting their
actions to analysis.

Q: A historian uses tax records and parish baptism registers to study family size

A. Political history   B. Social and demographic history   C. Diplomatic history
D. Intellectual history
Reasoning: Using records of ordinary people to reconstruct demographic and family
patterns is social/demographic history, the kind expanded by the Annales school.
Political, diplomatic, and intellectual history focus on governance, relations between
states, and ideas respectively. Answer: B.

## Literature (poetics and movements)

Poetry organizes language through form as well as meaning. Meter is the pattern of
stressed and unstressed syllables; iambic pentameter — five iambs (unstressed-stressed)
per line — is the dominant meter of English verse, the medium of Shakespeare's plays
and sonnets. A sonnet is fourteen lines with a turn (volta) of thought; the Shakespearean
form rhymes three quatrains and a couplet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG), the Petrarchan an octave
and sestet. Free verse abandons regular meter and rhyme, organizing instead by image,
line break, and cadence — a modern liberation that places more weight on the poet's ear.

Literary movements respond to one another. Romanticism exalted emotion, nature, and the
individual imagination against Enlightenment rationalism. Realism then sought to depict
ordinary life truthfully, and Modernism, shattered by industrial modernity and war,
fragmented narrative and embraced difficulty, interior monologue, and allusion.
Postmodernism turned playful and self-aware, mixing high and low culture and doubting
grand narratives. Each movement is partly a rebellion against its predecessor's
conventions, which is why literary history reads as a conversation across time.

Q: A fourteen-line poem in iambic pentameter rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG is a:

Reasoning: Fourteen lines with three quatrains and a closing couplet in that rhyme
scheme define the Shakespearean (English) sonnet. The Petrarchan divides into octave
and sestet; a villanelle and an ode have different forms. Answer: B.

## Sociology (research methods and theory)

Sociological knowledge rests on research methods that balance depth against breadth.
Quantitative methods — surveys, experiments, and analysis of large datasets — measure
patterns across many cases and test hypotheses statistically, prizing
generalizability. Qualitative methods — ethnography, in-depth interviews, participant
observation — capture meaning and context in fewer cases, prizing depth and the
participants' own understanding. The choice is not about rigor versus its absence but
about the question: how widespread is a pattern (quantitative) versus what does it mean
to those living it (qualitative).

Sociologists guard against several pitfalls. Correlation is not causation, and
confounding variables can produce spurious associations. Sampling matters enormously:
a non-representative sample yields conclusions that don't generalize, the flaw behind
many famously wrong predictions. Reflexivity acknowledges that the researcher is part
of the social world studied and can influence it, so objectivity is pursued through
transparent method rather than assumed. Operationalization — turning an abstract
concept like "social capital" into something measurable — is where much of the craft,
and much of the controversy, lies.

Q: A researcher spends a year living in and observing a community to understand its
culture from the inside. This method is:
A. A survey   B. An experiment   C. Ethnography (participant observation)   D. A
meta-analysis
Reasoning: Immersive, long-term observation to understand a culture from participants'
perspectives is ethnography via participant observation — a qualitative method.
Surveys and experiments are quantitative; meta-analysis pools prior studies. Answer: C.

## Psychology (disorders, therapy, and method)

Clinical psychology studies, diagnoses, and treats mental disorders, classified in
manuals like the DSM by clusters of symptoms. The biopsychosocial model frames disorders
as arising from interacting biological (genes, neurochemistry), psychological (thoughts,
coping), and social (relationships, stress) factors, rather than any single cause —
which is why effective treatment often combines approaches. Major categories include
mood disorders (depression, bipolar), anxiety disorders, and psychotic disorders, each
with characteristic features and evidence-based treatments.

Therapeutic approaches mirror psychology's paradigms. Cognitive-behavioral therapy,
among the best-supported, targets the maladaptive thought patterns and behaviors that
maintain distress, on the premise that changing how one interprets and responds to
situations changes how one feels. Psychodynamic therapy explores unconscious conflicts
and early experience; humanistic therapy emphasizes empathy and self-actualization.
Biological treatments (medication) act on neurochemistry. The "common factors" research
suggests that the therapeutic alliance itself — a trusting, collaborative relationship —
accounts for much of therapy's benefit across methods.

Psychology's scientific credibility depends on method. Experiments establish causation
through random assignment and control groups; the placebo control and double-blinding
guard against expectation effects. The field has reckoned with a "replication crisis" —
many published findings failed to reproduce — prompting reforms like preregistration of
hypotheses, larger samples, and open data. The lesson is general to all science: a
single striking result is a hypothesis, not a fact, until independently replicated.

D. Pharmacotherapy
Reasoning: Targeting distorted thoughts and maladaptive behaviors to relieve distress
is cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT). Psychodynamic therapy explores unconscious
conflict; humanistic therapy centers empathy and growth; pharmacotherapy uses
medication. Answer: B.

publication bias toward surprising positive results. Preregistration helps by having
researchers commit, in advance and publicly, to their hypotheses, sample size, and
analysis plan before seeing the data. That separates genuine confirmatory tests from
exploratory fishing: if the analysis was specified beforehand, a significant result is
far more credible, and a null result still gets documented rather than buried. Combined
with larger samples and open data, it shifts the field from chasing eye-catching
one-off findings toward results that reliably replicate.

## History (global and comparative)

Comparative and global history resist the tendency to treat one society's path as the
template for all. World-systems analysis frames the modern era as a single
interdependent economy with a wealthy "core" and an exploited "periphery," explaining
underdevelopment not as backwardness but as a relationship produced by global trade.
Comparative history sets cases side by side — why did industrialization begin in
Britain and not in China, despite China's earlier technological lead? — to isolate the
factors that actually made the difference, guarding against explanations that merely
restate the outcome. The discipline increasingly studies connections (trade, disease,
migration, ideas) that cross the borders national histories take for granted.

The exchange of crops, animals, people, and pathogens across the Atlantic after 1492 —
the Columbian Exchange — reshaped the entire planet's demography and diet. New World
crops like the potato and maize fueled population growth across Europe, Africa, and
Asia; Old World diseases like smallpox devastated Indigenous American populations who
lacked immunity, a catastrophe that enabled European conquest as much as any weapon.
This is environmental history's central lesson: biological and ecological forces are
not a backdrop to human history but active agents within it.

Reasoning: Comparing two cases to isolate the factors that explain divergent outcomes

claim that ecology is an agent in human affairs, not merely its stage.

## Literature (drama and the essay)

Drama is literature written to be performed, and its meaning lives partly in elements
prose lacks: staging, gesture, silence, and the physical presence of actors before an
audience. Dramatic irony — where the audience knows what a character does not — generates
much of theater's tension, as when we watch a character walk knowingly toward a doom
they cannot see. The classical unities of time, place, and action constrained much early
drama; modern theater broke them, and Brecht's "alienation effect" deliberately reminded
audiences they were watching a constructed play, refusing the comfortable illusion in
order to provoke critical thought rather than passive emotion.

The essay is the great form of exploratory nonfiction, from Montaigne's intimate
self-examinations to the argumentative and lyric essays of today. Its hallmark is a mind
thinking on the page — testing a question rather than merely delivering a conclusion (the
word "essay" comes from the French for "to attempt"). A strong essay earns its claims
through evidence and reasoning while retaining a distinctive voice, occupying the ground
between the impersonality of the report and the invention of fiction.

## Biology

The cell is the fundamental unit of life, and the deepest division in biology is
between prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea), whose DNA floats free in the cytoplasm,
and eukaryotes (everything else), whose DNA is packaged in a membrane-bound nucleus
and whose cells contain organelles. Mitochondria, the eukaryotic powerhouses,
generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation and carry their own circular DNA — a
relic of their origin as free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral host, the
endosymbiotic theory. Chloroplasts in plants have a parallel origin and run the
light reactions of photosynthesis.

The central dogma describes the flow of genetic information: DNA is transcribed into
messenger RNA, which is translated by ribosomes into protein. DNA is a double helix
of complementary base pairs — adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine — held by
hydrogen bonds, and the sequence of bases is a code read in triplets (codons), each
specifying one amino acid. The genetic code is redundant (multiple codons per amino
acid) and nearly universal across life, which is itself strong evidence of common
descent and what makes genetic engineering across species possible.

Evolution by natural selection is the organizing theory of all biology. Heritable
variation arises by mutation and recombination; individuals better suited to their
environment leave more offspring; over generations the population's allele
frequencies shift. Selection is not the only force — genetic drift (random changes,
especially in small populations), gene flow (migration), and mutation all alter
frequencies — but selection is the only one that builds adaptation. Speciation occurs
when populations become reproductively isolated and diverge until they can no longer
interbreed.

Cellular respiration and photosynthesis are complementary energy transformations.
Photosynthesis captures light energy to build glucose from CO2 and water, releasing
O2: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light -> C6H12O6 + 6O2. Respiration runs the reverse, oxidizing
glucose to release energy as ATP: C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy. Together
they cycle carbon and energy through the biosphere, the products of one feeding the
other.

## Chemistry

Chemistry is governed by electrons. The periodic table organizes elements by atomic
number and recurring valence-electron configuration, which is why elements in the
same column (group) share chemical behavior — the alkali metals all have one easily
lost valence electron, the noble gases all have full shells and are inert. Periodic
trends follow from effective nuclear charge: atomic radius shrinks left-to-right
across a period and grows down a group, while ionization energy and electronegativity
do the opposite, rising toward fluorine in the upper right.

Bonding is the redistribution of electrons to reach stable configurations. Ionic
bonds transfer electrons from metal to nonmetal, forming charged ions held by
electrostatic attraction (NaCl). Covalent bonds share electron pairs between
nonmetals; when shared unequally (differing electronegativity) the bond is polar,
giving water its bent, polar geometry and the hydrogen bonding that underlies its
extraordinary properties. Metallic bonding pools valence electrons into a "sea" that
makes metals conductive and malleable. Molecular shape follows VSEPR theory —
electron pairs arrange to minimize repulsion, so four bonding pairs give a tetrahedron
(109.5°) and the lone pairs on water push its H–O–H angle to about 104.5°.

Stoichiometry is the bookkeeping of reactions, built on the mole — Avogadro's number
(6.022 × 10^23) of particles. A balanced equation gives the fixed molar ratios in
which species react, so the limiting reactant — the one that runs out first —
determines how much product forms. Thermodynamics then tells whether a reaction is
spontaneous: the Gibbs free energy change ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative for spontaneous
processes, balancing the enthalpy change (heat) against the entropy change (disorder)
scaled by temperature.

Acid–base chemistry centers on proton transfer. The pH scale measures hydrogen-ion
concentration logarithmically: pH = −log[H+], so each unit is a tenfold change, and a
neutral solution at 25 °C has pH 7. Strong acids dissociate completely; weak acids
establish an equilibrium described by their Ka, and a buffer — a weak acid with its
conjugate base — resists pH change, the principle behind blood's bicarbonate buffer.

Balancing a combustion reaction:
  unbalanced: C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O

  Check: C 3=3, H 8=8, O 10 = 6 (in 3 CO2) + 4 (in 4 H2O) = 10. Balanced.

## Geography

Geography studies the spatial organization of the Earth's physical and human systems.
Physical geography explains the patterns of climate, landforms, and ecosystems. Global
climate is driven by the unequal heating of the Earth — the equator receives more
direct solar energy than the poles — which sets up atmospheric circulation cells
(Hadley, Ferrel, Polar) that redistribute heat and create the planet's bands of wet
and dry climate: rainforests near the rising air of the equator, deserts near the
descending air around 30° latitude.

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of physical geography and geology. The
lithosphere is broken into plates that move on the ductile asthenosphere, and most of
the planet's dramatic features occur at their boundaries. Convergent boundaries build
mountains and volcanic arcs and spawn deep ocean trenches and great earthquakes;
divergent boundaries create new crust at mid-ocean ridges; transform boundaries, like
the San Andreas, grind past one another and store the strain that releases as
earthquakes. The distribution of volcanoes and earthquakes around the Pacific "Ring
of Fire" is the surface signature of these processes.

Human geography studies how populations, cultures, and economies distribute
themselves and reshape the land. Demographic transition theory describes how
societies move from high birth and death rates (pre-industrial), through a phase of
falling death rates and rapid population growth, to low birth and death rates
(post-industrial), reshaping age structures and economies. Urbanization concentrates
population in cities, where models of internal structure — concentric zones, sectors,
multiple nuclei — try to explain the spatial pattern of land use, and where issues of
sprawl, transport, and inequality play out spatially.

Maps are the discipline's core tool, and every map projection trades off distortion.
The Mercator projection preserves direction and shape locally (useful for navigation)
but grossly inflates the area of high-latitude regions, making Greenland appear as
large as Africa though Africa is fourteen times larger. Equal-area projections
preserve relative size at the cost of shape. There is no perfect flat map of a sphere;
the choice of projection is always a choice about which truth to keep and which to
sacrifice.

## Math

Mathematics is the study of structure, and calculus is its tool for studying change.
The derivative measures an instantaneous rate of change — the slope of a curve at a
point — defined as the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to
zero. The integral measures accumulation — the area under a curve — and the
fundamental theorem of calculus reveals that differentiation and integration are
inverse operations, tying the two halves of the subject together. With these tools
one can find maxima and minima (where the derivative is zero), compute areas and
volumes, and model any system whose state evolves continuously.

Linear algebra is the mathematics of vectors, matrices, and linear transformations,
and it is the language of modern data and computation. A matrix represents a linear
map; multiplying a vector by a matrix transforms it. Eigenvectors are the special
directions a transformation merely scales (by their eigenvalue) without rotating, and
they reveal a transformation's intrinsic axes — the principle behind dimensionality
reduction, vibration modes, and the stability of dynamical systems. A system of linear
equations Ax = b has a unique solution exactly when A is invertible (nonzero
determinant).

Probability quantifies uncertainty. The probability of independent events both
occurring is the product of their probabilities; for mutually exclusive events, the
probability of either is the sum. Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem let us
update beliefs given evidence: P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B). The law of large numbers
guarantees that sample averages converge to the true mean, and the central limit
theorem explains why sums of many independent effects are approximately normally
distributed — the reason the bell curve appears everywhere.

Proof is what distinguishes mathematics from the empirical sciences. A statement is
true not because it has been tested but because it follows by valid deduction from
axioms. Common proof techniques include direct proof, proof by contradiction (assume
the negation and derive an absurdity), and mathematical induction (prove a base case
and that each case implies the next). The irrationality of √2 and the infinitude of
the primes are classic proofs by contradiction.

  antiderivative F(x) = x^2 + 3x
  F(4) − F(0) = (16 + 12) − 0 = 28.

## Physics

Physics seeks the simplest laws that govern matter, energy, space, and time. Classical
mechanics begins with Newton's three laws: a body keeps its velocity unless acted on
by a net force (inertia); force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma); and every
action has an equal and opposite reaction. From these follow the conservation laws —
of momentum, energy, and angular momentum — which are often more powerful than the
forces themselves because they hold even when the detailed forces are complicated or
unknown. Energy is never created or destroyed, only transformed between kinetic,
potential, thermal, and other forms.

Thermodynamics governs heat and the direction of processes. The first law is energy
conservation including heat; the second law states that the entropy of an isolated
system never decreases, which defines the arrow of time and forbids perpetual-motion
machines. Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, never the reverse without work
input, and no engine can convert heat entirely into work — the Carnot efficiency sets
the ceiling, rising with the temperature difference between the hot and cold
reservoirs.

Electromagnetism unifies electricity and magnetism. Charges create electric fields and
feel forces in them (Coulomb's law); moving charges create magnetic fields; and
changing magnetic fields induce electric fields (Faraday's law), the principle of
every generator and transformer. Maxwell's equations bundle these together and predict
that oscillating fields propagate as electromagnetic waves at the speed of light —
revealing light itself to be an electromagnetic wave, and uniting optics with
electromagnetism.

Modern physics broke the classical picture twice. Relativity showed that space and
time are not absolute: the speed of light is the same for all observers, so measured
lengths contract and clocks run slow at high relative speeds, and mass and energy are
equivalent (E = mc^2). Quantum mechanics showed that at small scales energy comes in
discrete packets, particles have wave properties, and certain pairs of quantities
(position and momentum) cannot both be known precisely — the uncertainty principle.
Together these theories underpin everything from GPS (which corrects for relativistic
time dilation) to semiconductors and lasers (quantum devices).

Worked kinematics: a ball is thrown straight up at 20 m/s (g = 10 m/s^2).
  Time to peak: v = v0 − g t -> 0 = 20 − 10 t -> t = 2.0 s.
  Max height: h = v0 t − ½ g t^2 = 20(2) − ½(10)(2^2) = 40 − 20 = 20 m.
  Total flight time (return to start): 4.0 s by symmetry.

## Biology (systems and ecology)

At larger scales, biology becomes the study of systems and their regulation.
Homeostasis is the maintenance of stable internal conditions through negative
feedback: a sensor detects deviation, a control center compares it to a set point,
and effectors push the variable back — the way the body regulates temperature, blood
glucose, and pH. Positive feedback, by contrast, amplifies a change and is rarer,
used for processes that must run to completion, like blood clotting or childbirth.

Ecology studies interactions among organisms and their environment. Energy flows one
way through ecosystems — captured by producers, passed to consumers, lost as heat at
each step — which is why food chains are short and biomass forms a pyramid: only about
10% of energy transfers between trophic levels. Matter, in contrast, cycles: the
carbon, nitrogen, and water cycles route key elements through living and nonliving
reservoirs. Population growth is exponential when resources are unlimited but levels
off at the carrying capacity (logistic growth) as competition, predation, and disease
intensify.

D. Decomposers remove biomass from the top.
Reasoning: The ~10% ecological efficiency between trophic levels means each level has
roughly a tenth of the energy of the one below, limiting how much biomass higher
levels can sustain — hence the energy/biomass pyramid. Answer: B.

## Chemistry (organic, equilibrium, kinetics)

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon, whose four bonds let it form chains,
rings, and branched skeletons of unlimited variety. Functional groups — the reactive
sites attached to a hydrocarbon framework — dictate behavior: hydroxyl (–OH) makes
alcohols, carbonyl (C=O) makes aldehydes and ketones, carboxyl (–COOH) makes acids,
amino (–NH2) makes amines. Isomers share a molecular formula but differ in
arrangement; structural isomers differ in connectivity, while stereoisomers differ
only in spatial orientation, and chirality — a carbon bonded to four different groups
— produces mirror-image enantiomers that can behave very differently in biology, the
reason a drug's "handedness" can matter enormously.

Chemical equilibrium is a dynamic balance where forward and reverse reactions proceed
at equal rates, so concentrations stop changing. The equilibrium constant K expresses
the ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium; a large K favors products.
Le Chatelier's principle predicts the response to a disturbance: stress the system —
add reactant, remove product, change temperature or pressure — and the equilibrium
shifts to partially counteract the change. Adding reactant pushes the reaction toward
products; for an exothermic reaction, raising temperature shifts it back toward
reactants.

Reaction kinetics studies rates rather than positions of equilibrium. The rate
depends on concentration (through the rate law), temperature (rates roughly double per
10 °C, per the Arrhenius relation), and the activation energy — the barrier reactants
must surmount. A catalyst speeds a reaction by providing a lower-energy pathway
without being consumed, and crucially does not change the equilibrium position, only
how fast it is reached. Enzymes are nature's catalysts, accelerating biochemical
reactions by enormous factors with exquisite specificity.

Q: An exothermic reaction at equilibrium is heated. Le Chatelier's principle predicts

A. Toward products, increasing K.   B. Toward reactants, decreasing K.   C. No shift.
D. Toward products, decreasing K.
Reasoning: For an exothermic reaction, heat is effectively a product; adding heat

A. Increase the yield of product.   B. Shift the equilibrium toward products.

reactions equally, so it reaches the same equilibrium faster but does not change K or
the final yield. Answer: C.

## Math (sequences, geometry, and statistics)

Sequences and series formalize patterns of numbers. An arithmetic sequence adds a
constant difference each term; a geometric sequence multiplies by a constant ratio.
A geometric series with ratio |r| < 1 converges to a finite sum a/(1−r), which is why
infinitely many ever-smaller pieces can add to a finite whole — the resolution of
Zeno's paradox. Power series extend this idea, representing functions like e^x, sin x,
and cos x as infinite polynomials, the foundation of how calculators and computers
evaluate transcendental functions.

Geometry connects algebra to space. The Pythagorean theorem (a^2 + b^2 = c^2) governs
right triangles and generalizes to the distance formula in coordinate geometry.
Trigonometry relates angles to ratios of sides (sine, cosine, tangent) and, through
the unit circle, to periodic functions that model every oscillation in nature. Similar
figures preserve shape and scale all lengths by a common ratio (and areas by its
square, volumes by its cube) — the scaling law that explains why large animals need
proportionally thicker bones.

Statistics turns data into inference. Descriptive statistics summarize a dataset's
center (mean, median, mode) and spread (range, variance, standard deviation).
Inferential statistics generalizes from a sample to a population, quantifying
uncertainty with confidence intervals and testing hypotheses with p-values — the
probability of observing data as extreme as the sample if the null hypothesis were

## Physics (waves, optics, and circuits)

Waves transport energy without transporting matter. A wave's speed equals its
frequency times its wavelength (v = fλ); for a given medium the speed is roughly
fixed, so higher frequency means shorter wavelength. Waves reflect, refract (bend when
changing speed between media), diffract (spread around obstacles), and interfere —
adding constructively where crests meet crests and destructively where crests meet
troughs. Standing waves on a string fix the harmonic frequencies of musical
instruments; the Doppler effect shifts the perceived frequency of a moving source,
raising the pitch of an approaching siren and lowering it as it passes.

Geometric optics treats light as rays. Refraction at a lens or the eye focuses
parallel rays to a point, and Snell's law (n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2) quantifies the
bending; beyond a critical angle light undergoes total internal reflection, the
principle of optical fibers. A converging lens forms a real, inverted image of a
distant object at its focal length — the basis of cameras and the eye — while
correcting vision means adding a lens that shifts the focal point onto the retina.

Electric circuits obey Ohm's law (V = IR): voltage is the push, current the flow, and
resistance the opposition. Power dissipated is P = IV = I^2R. In a series circuit the
same current flows through every element and resistances add; in a parallel circuit
the voltage is shared and the reciprocals of resistances add, so parallel paths lower
total resistance. Kirchhoff's laws — charge is conserved at junctions, and voltage
sums to zero around any loop — let one solve any network, however complex.

Worked optics: an object 30 cm from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Using
1/f = 1/do + 1/di: 1/10 = 1/30 + 1/di -> 1/di = 1/10 − 1/30 = 2/30 -> di = 15 cm.
The image is real (positive di), inverted, and reduced (magnification = −di/do =
−15/30 = −0.5).

Worked circuit: a 12 V battery across two resistors, 4 Ω and 2 Ω, in series.
  Total R = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω; current I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A.
  Voltage across the 4 Ω: V = IR = 2 × 4 = 8 V; across the 2 Ω: 4 V (sums to 12 V).
  Power from the battery: P = IV = 2 × 12 = 24 W.

## Geography (climate, hydrology, and resources)

The hydrologic cycle moves water through the Earth system: evaporation lifts water to
the atmosphere, condensation forms clouds, precipitation returns it, and runoff and
infiltration route it through rivers and aquifers back to the sea. Watersheds organize
the landscape into drainage basins, and the residence time of water varies enormously
— days in the atmosphere, thousands of years in deep groundwater and ice sheets. Human
demand increasingly outpaces recharge in many aquifers, making groundwater depletion a
defining resource issue.

Climate classification (the Köppen system) groups the world's climates by temperature
and precipitation patterns that align closely with vegetation: tropical, dry,
temperate, continental, and polar. Climate differs from weather in timescale — weather
is the atmosphere's state now, climate is its statistical pattern over decades. The
greenhouse effect, by which atmospheric gases trap outgoing infrared radiation, keeps
the planet habitable; the concern of climate change is the rapid enhancement of that
effect by added CO2 and methane, shifting the statistical baseline of extremes.

Reasoning: Large seasonal temperature swings (hot summers, cold winters) with
year-round precipitation and mixed forest are characteristic of a continental climate,
found in mid-latitude continental interiors. Answer: B.

## Agriculture

Agricultural science applies biology, chemistry, and engineering to the production of
food and fiber. Soil is the foundation, and its fertility depends on texture (the mix
of sand, silt, and clay), structure, organic matter, pH, and the availability of
nutrients. The three macronutrients are nitrogen (for leafy growth), phosphorus (for
roots and flowering), and potassium (for overall vigor and water regulation), the
N-P-K printed on every fertilizer bag. Nitrogen is the usual limiting nutrient, and
its management — through fertilizer, legume rotation, and timing — is central to yield
and to environmental impact, since excess nitrogen leaches into waterways and volatilizes
as greenhouse gas.

Crop production balances genetics, environment, and management. Plant breeding and, more
recently, genetic engineering have raised yields dramatically — the Green Revolution's
semi-dwarf, fertilizer-responsive grains averted famine for hundreds of millions.
Integrated pest management combines biological, cultural, and chemical controls to
suppress pests while slowing the evolution of resistance and protecting beneficial
insects. Precision agriculture uses GPS, sensors, and variable-rate application to match
inputs to the spatial variability of a field, cutting waste and cost.

Sustainability is now a defining constraint. Practices like crop rotation, cover
cropping, reduced tillage, and integrated nutrient management maintain soil health and
reduce erosion and runoff. Irrigation efficiency matters acutely where water is scarce:
drip irrigation delivers water directly to roots with far less evaporative loss than
flood or sprinkler systems. The overarching challenge is to raise productivity to feed a
growing population while shrinking agriculture's footprint on soil, water, and climate.

A. Add phosphorus.   B. Add nitrogen.   C. Lower the soil pH.   D. Increase irrigation.
Reasoning: Yellowing of older leaves (chlorosis) is a classic nitrogen-deficiency
symptom because nitrogen is mobile and the plant moves it from old leaves to new growth;
with the test confirming low N, adding nitrogen is the fix. Answer: B.

## Architecture & Engineering

Architecture unites art and engineering: a building must be beautiful and habitable,
but it must first stand up. The fundamental structural problem is carrying loads —
dead loads (the structure's own weight), live loads (occupants and furniture), and
environmental loads (wind, snow, earthquakes) — safely down to the foundation and into
the ground. Structural elements work in characteristic ways: columns carry compression,
ties and cables carry tension, and beams carry bending, which combines compression on
one face with tension on the other. The history of architecture is largely the history
of new ways to span space and resist these forces.

The great structural innovations each solved a spanning problem. The arch converts the
load of a span into compression carried along its curve to the supports, letting masonry
(strong in compression, weak in tension) span far wider than a flat stone lintel could;
the dome extends this idea into three dimensions. Steel and reinforced concrete in the
modern era allowed the frame to carry the loads, freeing the wall from its structural
role and enabling the glass curtain wall and the skyscraper. Reinforced concrete is a
composite: concrete handles compression while embedded steel reinforcing bars handle the
tension concrete cannot.

Sustainable and human-centered design now shapes the field. Passive design — orientation,
shading, thermal mass, and natural ventilation — reduces energy demand before any
mechanical system is added. Building codes encode hard-won safety lessons: egress widths,
fire ratings, and seismic detailing exist because earlier failures killed people.
Accessibility standards ensure the built environment serves people of all abilities, a
requirement that, like good structural design, is invisible when done well and dangerous
when ignored.

Figure: a semicircular stone bridge spanning a river, built of wedge-shaped blocks
(voussoirs) with a central keystone at the top.
Q: In this arch, the wedge-shaped stones primarily carry the load through:
A. Tension   B. Compression directed along the curve to the abutments   C. Bending
D. Shear only
Reasoning: An arch transforms vertical load into compression that follows the curve
down to the supports (abutments), which is why masonry — strong in compression, weak in
tension — can span far as an arch but not as a flat beam. Answer: B.

Q: Reinforced concrete embeds steel bars in concrete because:
A. Steel makes concrete cheaper.   B. Concrete is weak in tension, and steel carries

Reasoning: Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension; placing steel where
tension occurs (e.g., the bottom of a beam) lets the composite resist bending. The two

## Computer Science

Computer science studies computation: what can be computed, how efficiently, and how to
organize the computation reliably. Algorithms and data structures are the foundation.
The efficiency of an algorithm is described in Big-O notation, which characterizes how
its running time or space grows with input size n in the worst case — O(1) constant,
O(log n) logarithmic, O(n) linear, O(n log n), O(n^2) quadratic, and so on. Choosing the
right data structure (array, linked list, hash table, tree, graph) often matters more
than micro-optimizing code, because it changes the asymptotic complexity of the
operations you perform most.

Computability and complexity bound what is possible. Some problems are undecidable —
the halting problem (whether an arbitrary program halts) cannot be solved by any
algorithm. Among decidable problems, the class P contains those solvable in polynomial
time, and NP those whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time; whether P equals
NP is the field's great open question, and the NP-complete problems (like the traveling
salesman decision problem) are the hardest in NP — a polynomial algorithm for any one
would solve them all.

Systems and theory meet in practice. Operating systems manage concurrency, memory, and
I/O, juggling many processes on limited hardware and guarding against races and
deadlocks. Databases provide durable, consistent storage, and the relational model with
ACID transactions trades some scalability for strong guarantees. Networking layers
abstractions so that an application can send data across the world without knowing the
physical path. Throughout, abstraction is the central intellectual tool: hiding
complexity behind clean interfaces so that systems can be built from comprehensible
parts.

Worked complexity: searching a sorted array of n elements.
  Linear scan:     O(n)        — check each element
  Binary search:   O(log n)    — halve the search space each step
  For n = 1,000,000, linear is up to ~1,000,000 comparisons; binary is ~20. The gain
  comes from the data being sorted, which is the precondition binary search exploits.

## Electronics

Electronics is the engineering of circuits that process signals and power using active
devices. The foundational passive components are the resistor (opposes current, V=IR),
the capacitor (stores energy in an electric field, opposes changes in voltage), and the
inductor (stores energy in a magnetic field, opposes changes in current). In
alternating-current circuits, capacitors and inductors present a frequency-dependent
opposition called reactance, which combines with resistance into impedance — the reason
an RC circuit acts as a filter, passing some frequencies and blocking others.

The active device that defines modern electronics is the transistor, which acts as a
switch or amplifier: a small signal at one terminal controls a large current between two
others. Billions of MOSFET transistors on a single chip implement digital logic, where
voltages are interpreted as binary 0s and 1s. Logic gates (AND, OR, NOT, NAND) built
from transistors compose into the adders, registers, and control units of a processor.
The diode, which conducts in only one direction, rectifies AC to DC and protects
circuits; the light-emitting diode turns current into light.

Signals are either analog (continuous) or digital (discrete), and converting between
them is central. An analog-to-digital converter samples a continuous signal at intervals
and quantizes each sample to a number; the Nyquist theorem requires sampling at more
than twice the highest frequency present to reconstruct the signal faithfully, which is
why CD audio samples at 44.1 kHz to capture sound up to ~20 kHz. Digital signals
resist noise because a slightly corrupted voltage still reads as the correct 0 or 1,
the robustness that drove the digital revolution.

Q: To digitally capture an audio signal containing frequencies up to 15 kHz without
aliasing, the minimum sampling rate is:
A. 7.5 kHz   B. 15 kHz   C. 30 kHz   D. 60 kHz
Reasoning: The Nyquist theorem requires sampling at more than twice the highest
frequency: 2 × 15 kHz = 30 kHz minimum. Below that, higher frequencies fold back as
aliasing artifacts. Answer: C.

Q: A transistor used in digital logic is best understood as:
A. A voltage source   B. An electronically controlled switch   C. A passive resistor
D. An energy-storage element
Reasoning: In digital circuits the transistor functions as a switch whose conduction is
controlled by a gate voltage, enabling the 0/1 behavior of logic. (In analog circuits
it can act as an amplifier.) Answer: B.

## Energy & Power

Power engineering generates, transmits, and converts energy, and it is governed by
conservation and efficiency. Most electricity is still generated by spinning a turbine
coupled to a generator — whether the turbine is driven by steam from burning fuel or
nuclear fission, by falling water, or by wind. The generator exploits electromagnetic
induction: a changing magnetic flux through a coil induces a voltage. Efficiency is
bounded by thermodynamics; a thermal power plant's conversion of heat to work is capped
by the Carnot limit set by its hot and cold temperatures, which is why waste heat is
unavoidable and why higher operating temperatures raise efficiency.

The grid transmits power at high voltage to minimize losses. Since resistive loss in a
line is I^2R, transmitting a given power at high voltage means lower current and far
lower loss; transformers step the voltage up for long-distance transmission and back
down for safe use. Alternating current won the early "war of currents" largely because
transformers make AC voltage easy to change. Balancing supply and demand in real time,
across a grid that cannot easily store electricity, is the central operational
challenge.

The energy transition reshapes the field. Renewable sources — solar photovoltaics, wind,
hydro — produce electricity with no fuel and low emissions but are variable, so they
raise the problem of storage and grid flexibility. Batteries, pumped hydro, and demand
response help match intermittent supply to demand. Comparing sources fairly requires
the levelized cost of energy, which spreads lifetime capital and operating costs over
lifetime output, and a full accounting of externalities like emissions that markets
often ignore.

Q: Electric power is transmitted at very high voltage primarily to:
A. Make it safer to touch.   B. Reduce current and therefore I^2R resistive losses
over long distances.   C. Increase the frequency.   D. Store energy more easily.
Reasoning: For a fixed power P = VI, raising voltage lowers current, and since line
loss is I^2R, lower current sharply cuts transmission losses. High voltage is less safe
to touch, not more. Answer: B.

## Materials

Materials science links the structure of matter at the atomic and microscopic scale to
the properties engineers care about. The four classic material families are metals
(metallic bonding gives strength, ductility, and conductivity), ceramics (ionic/covalent
bonding gives hardness and heat resistance but brittleness), polymers (long molecular
chains give low density and flexibility), and composites (combinations exploiting the
best of each, like carbon-fiber-reinforced plastic). The central paradigm is the
processing–structure–property–performance chain: how you make a material sets its
internal structure, which sets its properties, which set how it performs in service.

Mechanical properties describe response to load. Stress is force per unit area; strain
is fractional deformation. In the elastic region a material returns to its original
shape when unloaded, and the stiffness is the Young's modulus, the slope of the
stress–strain curve. Beyond the yield strength, deformation becomes permanent (plastic);
the ultimate tensile strength is the peak the material can bear before necking and
fracture. Toughness is the energy absorbed before fracture — a material can be strong but
brittle (high strength, low toughness, like glass) or tough (absorbing energy, like many
metals).

Microstructure controls these properties and can be engineered. In metals, grain size
matters: finer grains generally mean higher strength (the Hall–Petch relationship),
because grain boundaries impede the dislocation motion that underlies plastic flow.
Heat treatment manipulates microstructure — quenching steel traps a hard phase, and
tempering then trades some hardness for toughness. Alloying, work hardening, and
precipitation hardening are other levers, all working by controlling how easily
dislocations move.

Figure: a stress–strain curve rising steeply and linearly from the origin, then bending
over at a point, continuing to a peak, and finally dropping to a fracture point.

A. Plastic deformation   B. Elastic deformation, whose slope is the Young's modulus
C. Fracture   D. Necking

A. Lower strength   B. Higher strength (Hall-Petch)   C. No change in strength

Reasoning: The Hall-Petch relationship says strength increases as grain size decreases,
because more grain boundaries obstruct dislocation movement, making plastic deformation
harder. Answer: B.

## Mechanical Engineering

Mechanical engineering designs machines and systems that produce, transmit, and use
mechanical power, resting on statics, dynamics, thermodynamics, and fluid mechanics.
Statics analyzes bodies in equilibrium, where forces and moments balance; it sizes
beams, trusses, and supports so structures don't move or fail. Dynamics adds motion,
relating forces to acceleration (Newton's second law) and analyzing vibration, which
must be understood and controlled because every structure has natural frequencies at
which a small periodic force can build catastrophic resonance.

Thermodynamics and heat transfer govern engines and energy systems. The thermodynamic
cycles — Otto for gasoline engines, Diesel, Rankine for steam plants, Brayton for gas
turbines — are sequences of compression, heat addition, expansion, and heat rejection
whose efficiency depends on the temperature extremes and the compression ratio. Heat
transfer occurs by conduction (through solids), convection (by moving fluids), and
radiation (electromagnetic), and managing it — cooling an engine, insulating a building,
designing a heat exchanger — is a recurring engineering task.

Fluid mechanics studies liquids and gases in motion, governed by conservation of mass
and momentum. Bernoulli's principle relates pressure and velocity in a flow — faster
flow means lower pressure — explaining lift on a wing and flow through a venturi. The
Reynolds number predicts whether flow is smooth (laminar) or chaotic (turbulent), which
determines drag and mixing. Across all these areas, the engineer's method is to model
the physics, apply safety factors to cover uncertainty, and validate with testing,
because real materials and loads never exactly match the idealized analysis.

Worked statics: a 600 N weight hangs from the middle of a 4 m beam simply supported at
both ends. By symmetry each support carries half: 300 N. The maximum bending moment at
the center is M = (F × L)/4 = (600 × 4)/4 = 600 N·m.

## Computer Science (systems and concurrency)

Concurrency lets a program make progress on multiple tasks at once, and it is a source
of both performance and a notorious class of bugs. When threads share mutable state,
unsynchronized access produces race conditions, where the result depends on unpredictable
timing. The classic remedies are mutual exclusion (a lock so only one thread enters a
critical section at a time) and immutability (sharing only data that never changes).
Locks introduce their own hazard, deadlock, where two threads each hold a resource the
other needs and both wait forever — preventable by always acquiring locks in a consistent
global order.

Caching pervades systems because there is an enormous gap between fast, small storage
(CPU registers and caches) and slow, large storage (disk and network). The principle of
locality — programs tend to reuse recent data (temporal) and nearby data (spatial) —
makes caches effective. A cache needs an eviction policy (LRU evicts the least recently
used entry) and an invalidation strategy, and "there are only two hard things in computer
science: cache invalidation and naming things" is a joke precisely because keeping a cache
consistent with its source of truth is genuinely difficult.

Q: Two threads each lock resource A then resource B, but a third locks B then A. The
system occasionally freezes with threads waiting on each other. The fault and a fix are:
A. A race condition; add more threads.   B. A deadlock; enforce a consistent global lock
ordering.   C. A cache miss; add memory.   D. A memory leak; restart.
Reasoning: Threads waiting circularly on locks each holds is deadlock; acquiring locks
in one consistent order across all threads breaks the circular-wait condition and
prevents it. Answer: B.

A binary search, the canonical O(log n) algorithm, in pseudocode:
  function search(sorted, target):
      lo = 0; hi = len(sorted) - 1
      while lo <= hi:
          mid = (lo + hi) // 2
          if sorted[mid] == target: return mid
          if sorted[mid] <  target: lo = mid + 1
          else:                      hi = mid - 1
      return -1
  Each iteration halves the candidate range, so it finishes in about log2(n) steps.

## Electronics (digital logic and embedded)

Digital logic builds computation from Boolean algebra, where variables are 0 or 1 and the
operations are AND, OR, and NOT. Any logic function can be expressed in terms of these,
and in fact the NAND gate alone is functionally complete — every other gate can be built
from NANDs, which is why NAND is a workhorse of chip design. Combinational logic produces
outputs that depend only on current inputs (adders, multiplexers), while sequential logic
adds memory through flip-flops clocked by a shared signal, enabling counters, registers,
and state machines — the basis of every processor.

Embedded systems put a small computer inside a device to sense and control the physical
world, under tight constraints of power, memory, and real-time response. A microcontroller
reads sensors through analog-to-digital converters, runs control logic, and drives
actuators, often using pulse-width modulation — switching a signal on and off rapidly so
the average power sets motor speed or LED brightness without wasting energy in a resistor.
Real-time constraints mean correctness includes timing: a result that is right but late
can be as useless as a wrong one.

Q: Pulse-width modulation controls the power delivered to a motor by:
A. Varying the supply voltage continuously with a resistor.   B. Rapidly switching the
supply on and off and varying the fraction of on-time (duty cycle).   C. Changing the
motor's resistance.   D. Reversing polarity.
Reasoning: PWM holds the voltage fixed but varies the duty cycle — the fraction of each
period the signal is on — so the average delivered power scales with duty cycle, far more
efficiently than dissipating the difference across a resistor. Answer: B.

## Energy & Power (storage and renewables)

Energy storage is the linchpin of a high-renewables grid, because solar and wind are
variable and electricity is otherwise consumed the instant it is generated. Storage
technologies trade energy capacity, power, efficiency, and cost: lithium-ion batteries
offer high round-trip efficiency and fast response for hours-scale shifting; pumped hydro
stores enormous energy by pumping water uphill and recovering it through turbines, the
dominant grid-scale store today; emerging options target the harder problem of seasonal
storage. Round-trip efficiency — energy out divided by energy in — is a key figure of
merit, since every conversion loses some energy.

A solar photovoltaic cell converts light directly to electricity via the photovoltaic
effect: photons promote electrons across a semiconductor junction, driving a current. A
panel's output depends on irradiance and temperature (efficiency actually falls as cells
heat up), and real-world yield is captured by the capacity factor — average output divided
by rated peak — which for solar is modest because the sun isn't always overhead. Comparing
a solar farm to a gas plant on rated capacity alone is misleading; capacity factor and
dispatchability matter as much as nameplate power.

Q: A 100 MW solar farm generates 220 GWh in a year. Its capacity factor is approximately
(a year is ~8,760 hours):
A. 25%   B. 50%   C. 75%   D. 90%
Reasoning: Maximum possible output = 100 MW × 8,760 h = 876,000 MWh = 876 GWh. Capacity
factor = 220 / 876 ≈ 0.25, i.e. ~25%, typical for solar. Answer: A.

## Materials (selection and failure)

Materials selection matches a material's property profile to a design's requirements, and
engineers often reason with figures of merit that combine properties — specific strength
(strength per density) for aerospace where weight is critical, or specific stiffness for
structures that must not flex. Ashby charts plot one property against another (say,
modulus versus density) so that whole classes of material can be compared at a glance and
the best candidate for a given objective identified. The cheapest material that meets all
constraints, not the strongest, is usually the right engineering answer.

Failure analysis asks why a part broke, because most failures are not simple overload.
Fatigue is failure under repeated cyclic loading far below the static strength — a crack
initiates at a stress concentration and grows a little with each cycle until the remaining
section fails suddenly; it causes a large share of mechanical failures and is why sharp
internal corners are avoided. Creep is slow deformation under sustained load at high
temperature, critical in turbine blades. Corrosion degrades materials chemically, and
brittle fracture can propagate catastrophically with little warning, especially in cold
conditions.

Q: An axle that carried its rated load for years suddenly snaps, and the fracture surface
shows concentric "beach marks" radiating from a small surface notch. The failure mode is:
A. Brittle overload   B. Fatigue   C. Creep   D. Corrosion
Reasoning: Beach marks emanating from a stress concentration, after many load cycles
below the static strength, are the signature of fatigue — progressive crack growth per
cycle until sudden final fracture. Answer: B.

## Agriculture (livestock and systems)

Animal agriculture applies the same systems thinking to livestock: nutrition, genetics,
health, and welfare interact to determine productivity. Ruminants like cattle digest
fibrous plants humans cannot eat by fermenting them in a specialized stomach with
microbial help, converting grass into milk and meat — but that same fermentation produces
methane, a potent greenhouse gas, making feed efficiency both an economic and an
environmental lever. Feed conversion ratio — feed input per unit of product — is the core
efficiency metric, and improving it reduces both cost and footprint.

Q: Cattle can derive nutrition from grass that humans cannot digest because:
A. They have stronger teeth.   B. Microbial fermentation in the rumen breaks down
cellulose.   C. They eat larger quantities.   D. Grass contains no cellulose.
Reasoning: Ruminants host microbes in the rumen that ferment and break down cellulose
into usable nutrients — the symbiosis that lets them convert otherwise-indigestible plant
fiber into energy. Answer: B.
