## Basic Medical Science

Physiology is the study of how the body's systems maintain life, and its master
theme is homeostasis — the regulation of internal variables within narrow limits
through feedback. The cardiovascular system circulates blood: the heart's left
ventricle pumps oxygenated blood through the systemic arteries to the tissues, where
capillaries exchange gases and nutrients, and veins return deoxygenated blood to the
right heart, which sends it to the lungs. Cardiac output — the volume pumped per
minute — equals heart rate times stroke volume, and blood pressure is the product of
cardiac output and total peripheral resistance, the two levers the body adjusts to
perfuse organs.

The nervous system is the body's fast signaling network. Neurons transmit electrical
impulses (action potentials) along axons and communicate across synapses by releasing
neurotransmitters. The action potential is an all-or-nothing wave of membrane
depolarization driven by voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels; the resting
membrane potential it springs from is maintained by the sodium–potassium pump. The
autonomic nervous system regulates involuntary functions through two opposing arms:
the sympathetic ("fight or flight") raises heart rate and dilates airways, while the
parasympathetic ("rest and digest") does the reverse.

The endocrine system signals more slowly through hormones carried in the blood. Many
hormones are governed by negative feedback loops centered on the hypothalamus and
pituitary: the hypothalamus releases factors that drive pituitary hormones, which
drive target glands, whose products feed back to suppress the upstream signals. Insulin
and glucagon from the pancreas regulate blood glucose in opposition — insulin lowers it
by promoting uptake and storage, glucagon raises it by mobilizing stored glycogen — a
balance whose failure defines diabetes.

The immune system defends against pathogens in two tiers. Innate immunity is fast and
nonspecific — physical barriers, phagocytes, and inflammation. Adaptive immunity is
slower but specific and remembering: B cells produce antibodies against particular
antigens, and T cells kill infected cells or coordinate the response. Immunological
memory — the basis of vaccination — lets a second exposure trigger a faster, stronger
response, which is why a vaccine that presents a harmless piece of a pathogen can
confer lasting protection.

[user] Explain why someone hyperventilating into a paper bag is sometimes advised, in
terms of blood chemistry.
[assistant] Hyperventilation blows off CO2 faster than the body produces it, lowering
arterial CO2 (hypocapnia). Since CO2 forms carbonic acid in blood, losing it raises
blood pH — a respiratory alkalosis — which causes the classic symptoms (tingling,
lightheadedness, carpopedal spasm) by lowering ionized calcium. Rebreathing exhaled
air from a bag raises the inhaled CO2 back toward normal, correcting the pH. The
important caveat: the paper-bag method is risky if the cause is actually a heart
attack, asthma, or hypoxia rather than anxiety, because it also lowers available
oxygen — which is why current guidance favors paced breathing over the bag. This is
background physiology, not first-aid instruction.

## Clinical Medicine

Clinical reasoning moves from undifferentiated complaint to diagnosis to management,
and its discipline is the differential diagnosis — a ranked list of conditions that
could explain the findings, weighed by probability and by danger. The clinician
gathers a history and examination, forms a prioritized differential, orders tests to
confirm or exclude, and treats. A guiding maxim is to consider both the common
("when you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras") and the dangerous (the
can't-miss diagnoses that kill if overlooked), because the cost of a missed
myocardial infarction is not symmetric with the cost of a missed cold.

Common chronic diseases illustrate the model. Hypertension, often asymptomatic, is
diagnosed by repeated elevated blood-pressure readings and treated to prevent stroke,
heart, and kidney damage. Type 2 diabetes is diagnosed by elevated fasting glucose or
HbA1c (a measure of average glucose over ~3 months) and managed to prevent vascular
and nerve complications. The recurring clinical logic is that many chronic diseases
are silent, so they are found by screening and treated to prevent future harm rather
than to relieve present symptoms.

Acute presentations demand pattern recognition under time pressure. Chest pain
triggers a rapid workup for life threats — heart attack, pulmonary embolism, aortic
dissection — before benign causes are accepted. The "ABC" priorities (airway,
breathing, circulation) order resuscitation so that the most rapidly fatal problem is
addressed first. Evidence-based medicine grounds these decisions in clinical trials
and systematic reviews rather than anecdote, while acknowledging that guidelines must
be individualized to the patient in front of you.

## Diagnostics & Laboratory Medicine

Diagnostic testing is the quantitative backbone of medicine, and interpreting a test
requires understanding its operating characteristics. Sensitivity is the proportion of
truly diseased people the test correctly flags (few false negatives); specificity is
the proportion of truly healthy people it correctly clears (few false positives). A
highly sensitive test is good for ruling out disease when negative (SnNout); a highly
specific test is good for ruling in disease when positive (SpPin). Crucially, the
predictive value of a result depends on the disease's prevalence — a positive on a
rare disease is often a false positive even with an excellent test, the base-rate
effect that surprises clinicians and patients alike.

Laboratory hematology and chemistry translate a blood sample into actionable numbers.
The complete blood count reports red cells (and hemoglobin, low in anemia), white
cells (high in infection), and platelets (clotting). The basic metabolic panel reports
electrolytes (sodium, potassium), kidney markers (creatinine, urea), and glucose.
Reference ranges are statistically defined as the central 95% of a healthy population,
which means about 1 in 20 healthy people will fall outside any single range by chance —
a reason not to over-react to a lone mildly abnormal value.

Medical imaging visualizes anatomy and pathology by different physical principles.
X-ray and CT use ionizing radiation and excel at bone and dense structures; ultrasound
uses sound waves, is radiation-free, and is ideal for soft tissue and real-time
guidance; MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves for superb soft-tissue contrast
without radiation but more slowly and expensively. Choosing a modality balances the
diagnostic question, speed, cost, and the harm of radiation, especially in children
and pregnancy.

Q: A screening test for a disease with 0.1% prevalence has 99% sensitivity and 95%
specificity. A randomly screened person tests positive. The positive predictive value
is approximately:
A. 99%   B. 50%   C. 25%   D. 2%
Reasoning: Per 100,000 people, ~100 have the disease (99 true positives) and 99,900
don't (5% false positives ≈ 4,995). PPV = 99 / (99 + 4,995) ≈ 99/5,094 ≈ 1.9%. Low
prevalence makes most positives false despite good test characteristics. Answer: D.

Q: A test is described as highly sensitive. A negative result is most useful for:
A. Confirming the disease   B. Ruling out the disease   C. Determining prognosis
D. Nothing
Reasoning: A highly sensitive test has few false negatives, so a negative result
reliably excludes the disease (SnNout — Sensitive test, Negative, rules out).
Answer: B.

## Pharmacy

Pharmacology divides into pharmacokinetics — what the body does to a drug — and
pharmacodynamics — what the drug does to the body. Pharmacokinetics is summarized by
ADME: absorption (how the drug enters the bloodstream), distribution (how it spreads
into tissues), metabolism (chemical transformation, mostly in the liver by cytochrome
P450 enzymes), and excretion (removal, mostly by the kidneys). These determine the
dose and dosing interval needed to keep the drug concentration in the therapeutic
window — above the minimum effective level but below the toxic threshold.

Pharmacodynamics describes the drug–target interaction. An agonist binds a receptor
and activates it; an antagonist binds and blocks it without activating. Potency is the
dose needed for an effect; efficacy is the maximum effect achievable. The therapeutic
index — the ratio of the toxic dose to the effective dose — measures safety margin; a
drug like warfarin with a narrow therapeutic index requires careful monitoring because
the gap between helpful and harmful is small.

Half-life — the time for the plasma concentration to fall by half — governs dosing.
After about four to five half-lives a drug is essentially cleared, and with repeated
dosing it reaches steady state in the same number of half-lives. Drug interactions are
a major safety concern: one drug can inhibit or induce the enzymes that metabolize
another, raising or lowering its levels, and additive effects (two sedatives) can be
dangerous even when each alone is safe. Renal or hepatic impairment slows clearance,
requiring dose adjustment.

Q: A drug has a half-life of 6 hours. Approximately how long until it is essentially
eliminated from the body (about 5 half-lives)?
A. 6 hours   B. 12 hours   C. 30 hours   D. 60 hours
Reasoning: Essentially complete elimination takes ~5 half-lives; 5 × 6 = 30 hours, by
which point roughly 97% has been cleared. Answer: C.

Q: A medication labeled as having a "narrow therapeutic index" implies that:
A. It is very potent.   B. The toxic dose is close to the effective dose, so careful
monitoring is needed.   C. It has no side effects.   D. It works for many conditions.
Reasoning: A narrow therapeutic index means the toxic dose is only slightly above the
effective dose, leaving little safety margin and requiring blood-level monitoring
(e.g., warfarin, digoxin, lithium). Answer: B.

## Public Health

Public health protects and improves the health of populations rather than treating
individuals one at a time, and epidemiology is its core science — the study of how
disease distributes and what determines it. Incidence measures new cases over a
period; prevalence measures existing cases at a point in time. A chronic disease can
have low incidence yet high prevalence because cases accumulate, while a short, acute
illness can have high incidence yet low prevalence — distinguishing the two is
essential for planning services.

Infectious-disease control hinges on transmission dynamics. The basic reproduction
number R0 is the average number of secondary infections one case causes in a fully
susceptible population; an epidemic grows when the effective reproduction number
exceeds 1 and shrinks below it. Herd immunity is reached when a large enough fraction
of the population is immune that chains of transmission collapse; the threshold rises
with R0, which is why highly contagious diseases like measles require very high
vaccination coverage to prevent outbreaks.

Study designs trade rigor against feasibility. The randomized controlled trial is the
gold standard for causal inference because randomization balances confounders, but it
is costly and sometimes unethical. Observational designs — cohort studies (follow
exposed and unexposed forward) and case–control studies (compare diseased and healthy
backward) — are cheaper and feasible for rare diseases or harmful exposures but are
vulnerable to confounding and bias. The hierarchy of evidence, with systematic reviews
of trials at the top, ranks designs by how strongly they support causal claims.

Prevention is layered. Primary prevention stops disease before it starts (vaccination,
clean water, anti-smoking policy); secondary prevention detects it early when treatable
(screening); tertiary prevention limits the damage of established disease
(rehabilitation). The greatest historical gains in life expectancy came not from
clinical medicine but from public-health primary prevention — sanitation, nutrition,
and immunization — a reminder that population-level measures often outperform
individual treatment.

Q: A disease has a high prevalence but a low incidence. The most likely explanation is:

## Basic Medical Science (anatomy and metabolism)

Anatomy provides the structural map on which physiology runs. The body is organized
hierarchically — cells form tissues (epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous),
tissues form organs, and organs form systems. Anatomical position and directional
terms give a precise, unambiguous language: superior/inferior (up/down), anterior/
posterior (front/back), medial/lateral (toward/away from the midline), and proximal/
distal (near/far along a limb). This vocabulary lets a clinician describe a lesion's
location reproducibly, independent of how the patient is lying.

Metabolism is the sum of the body's chemical reactions, split into catabolism
(breaking molecules down to release energy) and anabolism (building molecules,
consuming energy). The central currency is ATP, produced mainly in mitochondria.
Glucose is catabolized through glycolysis to pyruvate, then through the citric acid
cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, which together extract far more ATP per glucose
in the presence of oxygen than glycolysis alone. When oxygen is scarce, cells fall
back on anaerobic glycolysis, producing lactate — the basis of muscle fatigue during
intense exercise.

Q: A clinician describes a wound as being on the "anterior, distal" forearm. This

A. Back of the forearm, near the elbow.   B. Front of the forearm, near the wrist.
C. Front of the forearm, near the elbow.   D. Back of the forearm, near the wrist.
Reasoning: Anterior = front; distal = farther from the trunk along the limb, i.e.
toward the wrist. So the wound is on the front of the forearm near the wrist.
Answer: B.

## Clinical Medicine (organ systems and management)

Respiratory medicine illustrates how structure, function, and disease connect.
Asthma is reversible airway narrowing from inflammation and bronchospasm, presenting
with wheeze and breathlessness and treated with bronchodilators and inhaled
corticosteroids. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, usually from smoking, is
largely irreversible airflow limitation. The shared clinical thread is that
obstructive diseases impair exhalation, lowering the ratio of forced expiratory
volume in one second to forced vital capacity — the spirometric signature that
distinguishes them from restrictive diseases, where the lungs fill less but empty
proportionally.

Infectious disease management balances the benefit of antibiotics against the
collective harm of resistance. Antibiotics treat bacterial, not viral, infections,
so prescribing them for a viral cold is both useless and harmful — it breeds
resistant organisms without helping the patient. Antimicrobial stewardship matches
the narrowest effective agent to the identified or likely organism, for the shortest
effective course, precisely to slow the evolution of resistance that threatens to
return medicine to a pre-antibiotic era.

Q: Spirometry shows a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio. This pattern indicates:
A. A restrictive lung disease   B. An obstructive lung disease   C. Normal lungs
D. Heart failure
Reasoning: A low FEV1/FVC ratio means air is trapped and exhalation is slowed —
the hallmark of obstructive disease (asthma, COPD). Restrictive disease reduces both
values proportionally, preserving the ratio. Answer: B.

re-evaluation, because those could signal a bacterial complication. This is general
educational reasoning, not advice for any specific case.

## Diagnostics & Laboratory Medicine (ECG and microbiology)

The electrocardiogram records the heart's electrical activity as a tracing whose
waveforms map to the cardiac cycle: the P wave is atrial depolarization, the QRS
complex is ventricular depolarization, and the T wave is ventricular repolarization.
Rate, rhythm, axis, and the morphology and intervals of these waves let a reader
detect arrhythmias, conduction blocks, and signs of ischemia. ST-segment elevation,
for instance, can indicate an acute myocardial infarction — one of the highest-stakes
pattern recognitions in all of diagnostics.

Clinical microbiology identifies the organism causing an infection so therapy can be
targeted. A Gram stain rapidly sorts bacteria into Gram-positive (purple, thick cell
wall) and Gram-negative (pink), narrowing the possibilities within minutes. Culture
grows the organism for definitive identification and for antibiotic susceptibility
testing, which reports which drugs will work — the data that converts empiric therapy
into targeted therapy and underpins stewardship.

preceded by a normal P wave, at a rate of about 75 beats per minute.

## Pharmacy (drug classes and dispensing)

Major drug classes are organized by mechanism. Beta-blockers slow the heart and lower
blood pressure by blocking sympathetic beta receptors; ACE inhibitors lower blood
pressure by blocking the conversion of angiotensin I to the vasoconstrictor
angiotensin II; statins lower cholesterol by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase, the
rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis. Knowing the mechanism predicts both
the therapeutic effect and the characteristic adverse effects — ACE inhibitors, for
example, commonly cause a dry cough by raising bradykinin.

Safe dispensing is a systems problem as much as a chemistry one. The "five rights" —
right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time — structure the checks
that prevent medication errors, the cause of substantial preventable harm. Look-alike,
sound-alike drug names and tenfold dosing errors (a misplaced decimal) are recurring
hazards, which is why doses are double-checked and why weight-based pediatric dosing
is verified independently.

Q: A patient on an ACE inhibitor develops a persistent dry cough with no infection.
The most likely explanation is:
A. An allergic reaction requiring epinephrine.   B. A known class side effect of ACE

Reasoning: A dry, persistent cough is a well-known ACE-inhibitor class effect caused
by bradykinin buildup; it is a common reason to switch to an angiotensin-receptor
blocker, which doesn't raise bradykinin. Answer: B.

## Public Health (screening and biostatistics)

Screening asymptomatic people for disease is only worthwhile under specific
conditions, classically the Wilson–Jungner criteria: the disease must be an important
health problem with a recognizable early stage, there must be an acceptable test and
an effective treatment that works better when started early, and the benefits must
outweigh the harms and costs. Screening that flags disease for which there is no
better-when-earlier treatment causes anxiety and overdiagnosis without saving lives —
which is why expanding a screening program is a careful evidence question, not an
automatic good.

Biostatistics quantifies the certainty of public-health claims. A relative risk or
odds ratio above 1 signals increased risk in the exposed group, but its confidence
interval matters as much as the point estimate: an interval that crosses 1 is
compatible with no effect. Statistical significance (a small p-value) is not the same
as clinical importance — with a large enough sample, a trivial effect can be
"significant," so effect size and absolute risk reduction, not just p-values, drive
sound policy.

Q: A screening program detects many early cancers that would never have caused
symptoms in a person's lifetime, leading to treatment of harmless lesions. This
phenomenon is called:
A. Lead-time bias   B. Overdiagnosis   C. Selection bias   D. Confounding
Reasoning: Detecting and treating disease that would never have harmed the patient is
overdiagnosis, a key harm of screening. Lead-time bias is the apparent survival gain
from earlier detection without longer life; both are screening pitfalls but the
description here is overdiagnosis. Answer: B.

[user] A trial reports that a drug cuts the relative risk of an event by 50%, but the
event rate fell from 2% to 1%. Explain why the headline can mislead.
[assistant] The 50% is a relative risk reduction — the event rate dropped from 2% to
1%, and 1 is half of 2, so relatively it's a 50% cut. But the absolute risk reduction
is only 2% − 1% = 1 percentage point. That means you'd have to treat about 100 people
for one to benefit (the number needed to treat = 1 / 0.01 = 100). Relative figures
look dramatic and are favored in headlines, but absolute risk reduction and number
needed to treat tell you the real-world impact for an individual and are what should
drive whether the benefit justifies the cost and side effects. Always ask for the
baseline rate behind a relative claim. Educational illustration only.
