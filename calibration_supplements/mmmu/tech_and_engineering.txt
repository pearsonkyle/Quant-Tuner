## Agriculture

Agricultural science applies biology, chemistry, and engineering to the production of
food and fiber. Soil is the foundation, and its fertility depends on texture (the mix
of sand, silt, and clay), structure, organic matter, pH, and the availability of
nutrients. The three macronutrients are nitrogen (for leafy growth), phosphorus (for
roots and flowering), and potassium (for overall vigor and water regulation), the
N-P-K printed on every fertilizer bag. Nitrogen is the usual limiting nutrient, and
its management — through fertilizer, legume rotation, and timing — is central to yield
and to environmental impact, since excess nitrogen leaches into waterways and volatilizes
as greenhouse gas.

Crop production balances genetics, environment, and management. Plant breeding and, more
recently, genetic engineering have raised yields dramatically — the Green Revolution's
semi-dwarf, fertilizer-responsive grains averted famine for hundreds of millions.
Integrated pest management combines biological, cultural, and chemical controls to
suppress pests while slowing the evolution of resistance and protecting beneficial
insects. Precision agriculture uses GPS, sensors, and variable-rate application to match
inputs to the spatial variability of a field, cutting waste and cost.

Sustainability is now a defining constraint. Practices like crop rotation, cover
cropping, reduced tillage, and integrated nutrient management maintain soil health and
reduce erosion and runoff. Irrigation efficiency matters acutely where water is scarce:
drip irrigation delivers water directly to roots with far less evaporative loss than
flood or sprinkler systems. The overarching challenge is to raise productivity to feed a
growing population while shrinking agriculture's footprint on soil, water, and climate.

## Architecture & Engineering

Architecture unites art and engineering: a building must be beautiful and habitable,
but it must first stand up. The fundamental structural problem is carrying loads —
dead loads (the structure's own weight), live loads (occupants and furniture), and
environmental loads (wind, snow, earthquakes) — safely down to the foundation and into
the ground. Structural elements work in characteristic ways: columns carry compression,
ties and cables carry tension, and beams carry bending, which combines compression on
one face with tension on the other. The history of architecture is largely the history
of new ways to span space and resist these forces.

The great structural innovations each solved a spanning problem. The arch converts the
load of a span into compression carried along its curve to the supports, letting masonry
(strong in compression, weak in tension) span far wider than a flat stone lintel could;
the dome extends this idea into three dimensions. Steel and reinforced concrete in the
modern era allowed the frame to carry the loads, freeing the wall from its structural
role and enabling the glass curtain wall and the skyscraper. Reinforced concrete is a
composite: concrete handles compression while embedded steel reinforcing bars handle the
tension concrete cannot.

Sustainable and human-centered design now shapes the field. Passive design — orientation,
shading, thermal mass, and natural ventilation — reduces energy demand before any
mechanical system is added. Building codes encode hard-won safety lessons: egress widths,
fire ratings, and seismic detailing exist because earlier failures killed people.
Accessibility standards ensure the built environment serves people of all abilities, a
requirement that, like good structural design, is invisible when done well and dangerous
when ignored.

## Computer Science

Computer science studies computation: what can be computed, how efficiently, and how to
organize the computation reliably. Algorithms and data structures are the foundation.
The efficiency of an algorithm is described in Big-O notation, which characterizes how
its running time or space grows with input size n in the worst case — O(1) constant,
O(log n) logarithmic, O(n) linear, O(n log n), O(n^2) quadratic, and so on. Choosing the
right data structure (array, linked list, hash table, tree, graph) often matters more
than micro-optimizing code, because it changes the asymptotic complexity of the
operations you perform most.

Computability and complexity bound what is possible. Some problems are undecidable —
the halting problem (whether an arbitrary program halts) cannot be solved by any
algorithm. Among decidable problems, the class P contains those solvable in polynomial
time, and NP those whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time; whether P equals
NP is the field's great open question, and the NP-complete problems (like the traveling
salesman decision problem) are the hardest in NP — a polynomial algorithm for any one
would solve them all.

Systems and theory meet in practice. Operating systems manage concurrency, memory, and
I/O, juggling many processes on limited hardware and guarding against races and
deadlocks. Databases provide durable, consistent storage, and the relational model with
ACID transactions trades some scalability for strong guarantees. Networking layers
abstractions so that an application can send data across the world without knowing the
physical path. Throughout, abstraction is the central intellectual tool: hiding
complexity behind clean interfaces so that systems can be built from comprehensible
parts.

Worked complexity: searching a sorted array of n elements.
  Linear scan:     O(n)        — check each element
  Binary search:   O(log n)    — halve the search space each step
  For n = 1,000,000, linear is up to ~1,000,000 comparisons; binary is ~20. The gain
  comes from the data being sorted, which is the precondition binary search exploits.

## Electronics

Electronics is the engineering of circuits that process signals and power using active
devices. The foundational passive components are the resistor (opposes current, V=IR),
the capacitor (stores energy in an electric field, opposes changes in voltage), and the
inductor (stores energy in a magnetic field, opposes changes in current). In
alternating-current circuits, capacitors and inductors present a frequency-dependent
opposition called reactance, which combines with resistance into impedance — the reason
an RC circuit acts as a filter, passing some frequencies and blocking others.

The active device that defines modern electronics is the transistor, which acts as a
switch or amplifier: a small signal at one terminal controls a large current between two
others. Billions of MOSFET transistors on a single chip implement digital logic, where
voltages are interpreted as binary 0s and 1s. Logic gates (AND, OR, NOT, NAND) built
from transistors compose into the adders, registers, and control units of a processor.
The diode, which conducts in only one direction, rectifies AC to DC and protects
circuits; the light-emitting diode turns current into light.

Signals are either analog (continuous) or digital (discrete), and converting between
them is central. An analog-to-digital converter samples a continuous signal at intervals
and quantizes each sample to a number; the Nyquist theorem requires sampling at more
than twice the highest frequency present to reconstruct the signal faithfully, which is
why CD audio samples at 44.1 kHz to capture sound up to ~20 kHz. Digital signals
resist noise because a slightly corrupted voltage still reads as the correct 0 or 1,
the robustness that drove the digital revolution.

Q: To digitally capture an audio signal containing frequencies up to 15 kHz without
aliasing, the minimum sampling rate is:
A. 7.5 kHz   B. 15 kHz   C. 30 kHz   D. 60 kHz
Reasoning: The Nyquist theorem requires sampling at more than twice the highest
frequency: 2 × 15 kHz = 30 kHz minimum. Below that, higher frequencies fold back as
aliasing artifacts. Answer: C.

Q: A transistor used in digital logic is best understood as:
A. A voltage source   B. An electronically controlled switch   C. A passive resistor
D. An energy-storage element
Reasoning: In digital circuits the transistor functions as a switch whose conduction is
controlled by a gate voltage, enabling the 0/1 behavior of logic. (In analog circuits
it can act as an amplifier.) Answer: B.

## Energy & Power

Power engineering generates, transmits, and converts energy, and it is governed by
conservation and efficiency. Most electricity is still generated by spinning a turbine
coupled to a generator — whether the turbine is driven by steam from burning fuel or
nuclear fission, by falling water, or by wind. The generator exploits electromagnetic
induction: a changing magnetic flux through a coil induces a voltage. Efficiency is
bounded by thermodynamics; a thermal power plant's conversion of heat to work is capped
by the Carnot limit set by its hot and cold temperatures, which is why waste heat is
unavoidable and why higher operating temperatures raise efficiency.

The grid transmits power at high voltage to minimize losses. Since resistive loss in a
line is I^2R, transmitting a given power at high voltage means lower current and far
lower loss; transformers step the voltage up for long-distance transmission and back
down for safe use. Alternating current won the early "war of currents" largely because
transformers make AC voltage easy to change. Balancing supply and demand in real time,
across a grid that cannot easily store electricity, is the central operational
challenge.

The energy transition reshapes the field. Renewable sources — solar photovoltaics, wind,
hydro — produce electricity with no fuel and low emissions but are variable, so they
raise the problem of storage and grid flexibility. Batteries, pumped hydro, and demand
response help match intermittent supply to demand. Comparing sources fairly requires
the levelized cost of energy, which spreads lifetime capital and operating costs over
lifetime output, and a full accounting of externalities like emissions that markets
often ignore.

Q: Electric power is transmitted at very high voltage primarily to:
A. Make it safer to touch.   B. Reduce current and therefore I^2R resistive losses
over long distances.   C. Increase the frequency.   D. Store energy more easily.
Reasoning: For a fixed power P = VI, raising voltage lowers current, and since line
loss is I^2R, lower current sharply cuts transmission losses. High voltage is less safe
to touch, not more. Answer: B.

## Materials

Materials science links the structure of matter at the atomic and microscopic scale to
the properties engineers care about. The four classic material families are metals
(metallic bonding gives strength, ductility, and conductivity), ceramics (ionic/covalent
bonding gives hardness and heat resistance but brittleness), polymers (long molecular
chains give low density and flexibility), and composites (combinations exploiting the
best of each, like carbon-fiber-reinforced plastic). The central paradigm is the
processing–structure–property–performance chain: how you make a material sets its
internal structure, which sets its properties, which set how it performs in service.

Mechanical properties describe response to load. Stress is force per unit area; strain
is fractional deformation. In the elastic region a material returns to its original
shape when unloaded, and the stiffness is the Young's modulus, the slope of the
stress–strain curve. Beyond the yield strength, deformation becomes permanent (plastic);
the ultimate tensile strength is the peak the material can bear before necking and
fracture. Toughness is the energy absorbed before fracture — a material can be strong but
brittle (high strength, low toughness, like glass) or tough (absorbing energy, like many
metals).

Microstructure controls these properties and can be engineered. In metals, grain size
matters: finer grains generally mean higher strength (the Hall–Petch relationship),
because grain boundaries impede the dislocation motion that underlies plastic flow.
Heat treatment manipulates microstructure — quenching steel traps a hard phase, and
tempering then trades some hardness for toughness. Alloying, work hardening, and
precipitation hardening are other levers, all working by controlling how easily
dislocations move.

Figure: a stress–strain curve rising steeply and linearly from the origin, then bending
over at a point, continuing to a peak, and finally dropping to a fracture point.

A. Plastic deformation   B. Elastic deformation, whose slope is the Young's modulus

because more grain boundaries obstruct dislocation movement, making plastic deformation
harder. Answer: B.

## Mechanical Engineering

Mechanical engineering designs machines and systems that produce, transmit, and use
mechanical power, resting on statics, dynamics, thermodynamics, and fluid mechanics.
Statics analyzes bodies in equilibrium, where forces and moments balance; it sizes
beams, trusses, and supports so structures don't move or fail. Dynamics adds motion,
relating forces to acceleration (Newton's second law) and analyzing vibration, which
must be understood and controlled because every structure has natural frequencies at
which a small periodic force can build catastrophic resonance.

Thermodynamics and heat transfer govern engines and energy systems. The thermodynamic
cycles — Otto for gasoline engines, Diesel, Rankine for steam plants, Brayton for gas
turbines — are sequences of compression, heat addition, expansion, and heat rejection
whose efficiency depends on the temperature extremes and the compression ratio. Heat
transfer occurs by conduction (through solids), convection (by moving fluids), and
radiation (electromagnetic), and managing it — cooling an engine, insulating a building,
designing a heat exchanger — is a recurring engineering task.

Fluid mechanics studies liquids and gases in motion, governed by conservation of mass
and momentum. Bernoulli's principle relates pressure and velocity in a flow — faster
flow means lower pressure — explaining lift on a wing and flow through a venturi. The
Reynolds number predicts whether flow is smooth (laminar) or chaotic (turbulent), which
determines drag and mixing. Across all these areas, the engineer's method is to model
the physics, apply safety factors to cover uncertainty, and validate with testing,
because real materials and loads never exactly match the idealized analysis.

Worked statics: a 600 N weight hangs from the middle of a 4 m beam simply supported at
both ends. By symmetry each support carries half: 300 N. The maximum bending moment at
the center is M = (F × L)/4 = (600 × 4)/4 = 600 N·m.

## Computer Science (systems and concurrency)

Concurrency lets a program make progress on multiple tasks at once, and it is a source
of both performance and a notorious class of bugs. When threads share mutable state,
unsynchronized access produces race conditions, where the result depends on unpredictable
timing. The classic remedies are mutual exclusion (a lock so only one thread enters a
critical section at a time) and immutability (sharing only data that never changes).
Locks introduce their own hazard, deadlock, where two threads each hold a resource the
other needs and both wait forever — preventable by always acquiring locks in a consistent
global order.

Caching pervades systems because there is an enormous gap between fast, small storage
(CPU registers and caches) and slow, large storage (disk and network). The principle of
locality — programs tend to reuse recent data (temporal) and nearby data (spatial) —
makes caches effective. A cache needs an eviction policy (LRU evicts the least recently
used entry) and an invalidation strategy, and "there are only two hard things in computer
science: cache invalidation and naming things" is a joke precisely because keeping a cache
consistent with its source of truth is genuinely difficult.

Q: Two threads each lock resource A then resource B, but a third locks B then A. The
system occasionally freezes with threads waiting on each other. The fault and a fix are:
A. A race condition; add more threads.   B. A deadlock; enforce a consistent global lock
ordering.   C. A cache miss; add memory.   D. A memory leak; restart.
Reasoning: Threads waiting circularly on locks each holds is deadlock; acquiring locks
in one consistent order across all threads breaks the circular-wait condition and
prevents it. Answer: B.

A binary search, the canonical O(log n) algorithm, in pseudocode:
  function search(sorted, target):
      lo = 0; hi = len(sorted) - 1
      while lo <= hi:
          mid = (lo + hi) // 2
          if sorted[mid] == target: return mid
          if sorted[mid] <  target: lo = mid + 1
          else:                      hi = mid - 1
      return -1
  Each iteration halves the candidate range, so it finishes in about log2(n) steps.

## Electronics (digital logic and embedded)

Digital logic builds computation from Boolean algebra, where variables are 0 or 1 and the
operations are AND, OR, and NOT. Any logic function can be expressed in terms of these,
and in fact the NAND gate alone is functionally complete — every other gate can be built
from NANDs, which is why NAND is a workhorse of chip design. Combinational logic produces
outputs that depend only on current inputs (adders, multiplexers), while sequential logic
adds memory through flip-flops clocked by a shared signal, enabling counters, registers,
and state machines — the basis of every processor.

Embedded systems put a small computer inside a device to sense and control the physical
world, under tight constraints of power, memory, and real-time response. A microcontroller
reads sensors through analog-to-digital converters, runs control logic, and drives
actuators, often using pulse-width modulation — switching a signal on and off rapidly so
the average power sets motor speed or LED brightness without wasting energy in a resistor.
Real-time constraints mean correctness includes timing: a result that is right but late
can be as useless as a wrong one.

Q: Pulse-width modulation controls the power delivered to a motor by:
A. Varying the supply voltage continuously with a resistor.   B. Rapidly switching the
supply on and off and varying the fraction of on-time (duty cycle).   C. Changing the
motor's resistance.   D. Reversing polarity.
Reasoning: PWM holds the voltage fixed but varies the duty cycle — the fraction of each
period the signal is on — so the average delivered power scales with duty cycle, far more
efficiently than dissipating the difference across a resistor. Answer: B.

## Energy & Power (storage and renewables)

Energy storage is the linchpin of a high-renewables grid, because solar and wind are
variable and electricity is otherwise consumed the instant it is generated. Storage
technologies trade energy capacity, power, efficiency, and cost: lithium-ion batteries
offer high round-trip efficiency and fast response for hours-scale shifting; pumped hydro
stores enormous energy by pumping water uphill and recovering it through turbines, the
dominant grid-scale store today; emerging options target the harder problem of seasonal
storage. Round-trip efficiency — energy out divided by energy in — is a key figure of
merit, since every conversion loses some energy.

A solar photovoltaic cell converts light directly to electricity via the photovoltaic
effect: photons promote electrons across a semiconductor junction, driving a current. A
panel's output depends on irradiance and temperature (efficiency actually falls as cells
heat up), and real-world yield is captured by the capacity factor — average output divided
by rated peak — which for solar is modest because the sun isn't always overhead. Comparing
a solar farm to a gas plant on rated capacity alone is misleading; capacity factor and
dispatchability matter as much as nameplate power.

Q: A 100 MW solar farm generates 220 GWh in a year. Its capacity factor is approximately
(a year is ~8,760 hours):
A. 25%   B. 50%   C. 75%   D. 90%
Reasoning: Maximum possible output = 100 MW × 8,760 h = 876,000 MWh = 876 GWh. Capacity
factor = 220 / 876 ≈ 0.25, i.e. ~25%, typical for solar. Answer: A.

## Materials (selection and failure)

Materials selection matches a material's property profile to a design's requirements, and
engineers often reason with figures of merit that combine properties — specific strength
(strength per density) for aerospace where weight is critical, or specific stiffness for
structures that must not flex. Ashby charts plot one property against another (say,
modulus versus density) so that whole classes of material can be compared at a glance and
the best candidate for a given objective identified. The cheapest material that meets all
constraints, not the strongest, is usually the right engineering answer.

Failure analysis asks why a part broke, because most failures are not simple overload.
Fatigue is failure under repeated cyclic loading far below the static strength — a crack
initiates at a stress concentration and grows a little with each cycle until the remaining
section fails suddenly; it causes a large share of mechanical failures and is why sharp
internal corners are avoided. Creep is slow deformation under sustained load at high
temperature, critical in turbine blades. Corrosion degrades materials chemically, and
brittle fracture can propagate catastrophically with little warning, especially in cold
conditions.

Q: An axle that carried its rated load for years suddenly snaps, and the fracture surface
shows concentric "beach marks" radiating from a small surface notch. The failure mode is:
A. Brittle overload   B. Fatigue   C. Creep   D. Corrosion
Reasoning: Beach marks emanating from a stress concentration, after many load cycles
below the static strength, are the signature of fatigue — progressive crack growth per
cycle until sudden final fracture. Answer: B.

## Agriculture (livestock and systems)

Animal agriculture applies the same systems thinking to livestock: nutrition, genetics,
health, and welfare interact to determine productivity. Ruminants like cattle digest
fibrous plants humans cannot eat by fermenting them in a specialized stomach with
microbial help, converting grass into milk and meat — but that same fermentation produces
methane, a potent greenhouse gas, making feed efficiency both an economic and an
environmental lever. Feed conversion ratio — feed input per unit of product — is the core
efficiency metric, and improving it reduces both cost and footprint.

Q: Cattle can derive nutrition from grass that humans cannot digest because:
A. They have stronger teeth.   B. Microbial fermentation in the rumen breaks down
cellulose.   C. They eat larger quantities.   D. Grass contains no cellulose.
Reasoning: Ruminants host microbes in the rumen that ferment and break down cellulose
into usable nutrients — the symbiosis that lets them convert otherwise-indigestible plant
fiber into energy. Answer: B.
