## History

History is not a list of dates but an argument about cause, change, and continuity
over time. The historian works from primary sources — documents, artifacts, and
testimony produced in the period under study — and secondary sources that interpret
them, always asking who produced a source, for whom, and why, because every source
carries the bias of its origin. Historiography, the history of historical writing,
reminds us that interpretations shift with each generation: the same revolution is
narrated as a triumph of liberty, a class struggle, or a contingent accident
depending on the historian's framework and moment.

Causation in history is layered. Long-term structural causes (economic systems,
demographic pressure, geography) set the stage; medium-term conjunctural causes
(political crises, ideological movements) build tension; and short-term triggers (a
specific event or decision) ignite change. The outbreak of the First World War
illustrates this: decades of imperial rivalry and alliance systems were the structure,
rising nationalism and an arms race the conjuncture, and the assassination at Sarajevo
the trigger. Reducing such an event to a single cause is the most common historical
error; explaining it requires weaving the levels together.

Periodization — dividing the past into eras like "medieval" or "early modern" — is a
tool, not a natural fact. It clarifies by grouping, but it distorts by imposing sharp
boundaries on gradual change and by privileging the perspective from which the scheme
was drawn. The "Renaissance" looks like a rebirth from a European vantage but ignores
flourishing elsewhere; the "fall of Rome" was, for many provincials, a slow
transformation rather than a single collapse. Good history holds its categories
lightly.

The major transformations of the modern world recur as themes. The Industrial
Revolution shifted economies from agrarian and artisanal to mechanized and factory-
based, reshaping where and how people lived and creating new classes and conflicts.
The Atlantic revolutions exported ideas of popular sovereignty and rights. Imperialism
bound distant societies into unequal global systems whose legacies persist.
Decolonization and the world wars redrew the political map and the moral imagination
of the twentieth century. In each, the historian's task is to explain not just what
changed but why it changed when and where it did.

## Literature

Literary study reads texts closely for how meaning is made, not just what is said.
Close reading attends to diction, imagery, syntax, sound, and structure — the choices
that distinguish literature from mere information. A metaphor asserts an identity
between unlike things ("the world is a stage"); a simile compares with "like" or "as";
symbolism lets a concrete object carry abstract meaning. Irony — saying one thing and
meaning another, or a gap between expectation and outcome — is among the most powerful
literary devices because it makes the reader an active interpreter rather than a
passive recipient.

Genre shapes expectation. Tragedy traces a protagonist's fall, often through a flaw
(hamartia) and a reversal (peripeteia) that yields recognition (anagnorisis); the
classical aim is catharsis, the purging of pity and fear. Comedy moves from disorder
to harmony, often ending in marriage or reconciliation. The novel, the dominant modern
form, gives interiority and social breadth, while lyric poetry compresses a single
charged moment of feeling or perception. Knowing a work's genre tells the reader which
conventions are in play and therefore where to look for meaning.

Narrative technique determines how a story reaches us. Point of view — first person
(intimate but limited and possibly unreliable), third-person limited, or omniscient —
controls what we know and trust. An unreliable narrator, whose account we learn to
doubt, turns reading into detective work. Structure matters too: a story told out of
chronological order, or framed as a story within a story, foregrounds the act of
telling itself and asks why this teller arranges events this way.

Literary theory supplies lenses. Formalism and New Criticism treat the text as a
self-contained verbal artifact. Marxist criticism reads literature as shaped by and
revealing class and economic relations. Feminist and postcolonial criticism ask whose
voices a text centers and whose it marginalizes. Reader-response theory locates meaning
in the encounter between text and reader. No single lens exhausts a rich text; each
reveals what the others obscure.

## Sociology

Sociology studies society as a structured system rather than a collection of
individuals, and its founding insight (the "sociological imagination") is that
personal troubles are often public issues — a single person's unemployment is a private
hardship, but mass unemployment is a structural fact requiring structural explanation.
The discipline distinguishes the macro level (institutions, classes, whole societies)
from the micro level (face-to-face interaction), and its great debate is how much
human behavior is shaped by social structure versus individual agency.

Three classical perspectives still organize the field. Functionalism sees society as a
system of interdependent parts, each contributing to stability, and asks what function
a given institution serves. Conflict theory, descended from Marx, sees society as an
arena of struggle over scarce resources and power, in which institutions serve
dominant groups. Symbolic interactionism works at the micro level, examining how shared
meanings are created and negotiated in everyday interaction. Each perspective is a
different question, not merely a different answer — they illuminate different facets of
the same social fact.

Socialization is the lifelong process by which people internalize the norms, values,
and roles of their culture, through family, school, peers, and media. It explains both
social reproduction — how inequalities persist across generations — and the apparent
naturalness of arrangements that are in fact learned. Concepts like role, status,
norm, and deviance let sociologists analyze how expectations govern behavior and how
societies define and respond to rule-breaking, which itself reveals their underlying
values.

Stratification — the structured ranking of groups by wealth, power, and prestige — is
a central concern. Social mobility measures movement between strata; societies that
celebrate opportunity often show less mobility than their ideology claims, because
advantages compound. Sociologists examine how class, race, and gender intersect to
shape life chances, and how institutions can entrench or reduce inequality — questions
that connect the discipline directly to policy.

## Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior, and its history is a sequence
of dominant paradigms. Behaviorism insisted psychology study only observable behavior,
explaining learning through classical conditioning (Pavlov: a neutral stimulus paired
with a meaningful one comes to evoke the response) and operant conditioning (Skinner:
behavior is shaped by its consequences — reinforcement increases it, punishment
decreases it). The cognitive revolution reopened the "black box" of the mind, modeling
attention, memory, and reasoning as information processing, and cognitive psychology
now dominates much of the field.

Memory is reconstructive, not a recording. Information passes from sensory memory to a
limited-capacity working memory and, if processed deeply, to long-term memory; retrieval
reconstructs the memory each time, which is why memories are malleable and eyewitness
testimony is fallible. The serial-position effect (better recall of the first and last
items in a list), the spacing effect (distributed practice beats cramming), and
encoding specificity (recall is better when context matches encoding) are robust
findings with direct implications for learning.

Cognitive biases are systematic departures from rational judgment, mapped especially by
Kahneman and Tversky. Confirmation bias leads us to seek evidence that fits our beliefs;
availability heuristic makes vivid, easily recalled events seem more probable; anchoring
ties estimates to an initial number. These are not random errors but predictable
products of mental shortcuts (heuristics) that are usually efficient but fail in
characteristic ways — a "fast" intuitive system and a "slow" deliberate system, in dual-
process terms.

Developmental and social psychology widen the lens. Piaget mapped stages of cognitive
development in children; attachment theory studies how early bonds shape later
relationships. Social psychology demonstrates the power of the situation: conformity
(Asch), obedience to authority (Milgram), and the fundamental attribution error — our
tendency to explain others' behavior by their character while excusing our own by
circumstance. The recurring lesson is that behavior is driven far more by context than
our intuitions credit.

## History (methods and themes)

Quantitative and social history broadened the discipline beyond kings and battles to
the lives of ordinary people, using parish registers, tax rolls, and census data to
reconstruct demography, family, and work. The Annales school in France shifted
attention to the longue durée — the slow rhythms of climate, geography, and mental
structures that change over centuries — arguing that the surface of political events
rides on these deeper currents. This widened the historian's evidence to include
material culture, oral testimony, and statistics, and made the experience of the
marginalized a legitimate, central subject.

Two persistent dangers shape historical method. Anachronism imposes the assumptions
of the present onto the past — judging a medieval figure by modern values, or reading
modern concepts into societies that lacked them. Presentism, its cousin, distorts the
past by treating it merely as a prelude to the present, as if everything led inevitably
to now. Good history practices empathy without endorsement: it reconstructs how people
in the past understood their own world on their own terms, while still subjecting their
actions to analysis.

## Literature (poetics and movements)

Poetry organizes language through form as well as meaning. Meter is the pattern of
stressed and unstressed syllables; iambic pentameter — five iambs (unstressed-stressed)
per line — is the dominant meter of English verse, the medium of Shakespeare's plays
and sonnets. A sonnet is fourteen lines with a turn (volta) of thought; the Shakespearean
form rhymes three quatrains and a couplet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG), the Petrarchan an octave
and sestet. Free verse abandons regular meter and rhyme, organizing instead by image,
line break, and cadence — a modern liberation that places more weight on the poet's ear.

Literary movements respond to one another. Romanticism exalted emotion, nature, and the
individual imagination against Enlightenment rationalism. Realism then sought to depict
ordinary life truthfully, and Modernism, shattered by industrial modernity and war,
fragmented narrative and embraced difficulty, interior monologue, and allusion.
Postmodernism turned playful and self-aware, mixing high and low culture and doubting
grand narratives. Each movement is partly a rebellion against its predecessor's
conventions, which is why literary history reads as a conversation across time.

## Sociology (research methods and theory)

Sociological knowledge rests on research methods that balance depth against breadth.
Quantitative methods — surveys, experiments, and analysis of large datasets — measure
patterns across many cases and test hypotheses statistically, prizing
generalizability. Qualitative methods — ethnography, in-depth interviews, participant
observation — capture meaning and context in fewer cases, prizing depth and the
participants' own understanding. The choice is not about rigor versus its absence but
about the question: how widespread is a pattern (quantitative) versus what does it mean
to those living it (qualitative).

Sociologists guard against several pitfalls. Correlation is not causation, and
confounding variables can produce spurious associations. Sampling matters enormously:
a non-representative sample yields conclusions that don't generalize, the flaw behind
many famously wrong predictions. Reflexivity acknowledges that the researcher is part
of the social world studied and can influence it, so objectivity is pursued through
transparent method rather than assumed. Operationalization — turning an abstract
concept like "social capital" into something measurable — is where much of the craft,
and much of the controversy, lies.

Q: A researcher spends a year living in and observing a community to understand its

## Psychology (disorders, therapy, and method)

Clinical psychology studies, diagnoses, and treats mental disorders, classified in
manuals like the DSM by clusters of symptoms. The biopsychosocial model frames disorders
as arising from interacting biological (genes, neurochemistry), psychological (thoughts,
coping), and social (relationships, stress) factors, rather than any single cause —
which is why effective treatment often combines approaches. Major categories include
mood disorders (depression, bipolar), anxiety disorders, and psychotic disorders, each
with characteristic features and evidence-based treatments.

Therapeutic approaches mirror psychology's paradigms. Cognitive-behavioral therapy,
among the best-supported, targets the maladaptive thought patterns and behaviors that
maintain distress, on the premise that changing how one interprets and responds to
situations changes how one feels. Psychodynamic therapy explores unconscious conflicts
and early experience; humanistic therapy emphasizes empathy and self-actualization.
Biological treatments (medication) act on neurochemistry. The "common factors" research
suggests that the therapeutic alliance itself — a trusting, collaborative relationship —
accounts for much of therapy's benefit across methods.

Psychology's scientific credibility depends on method. Experiments establish causation
through random assignment and control groups; the placebo control and double-blinding
guard against expectation effects. The field has reckoned with a "replication crisis" —
many published findings failed to reproduce — prompting reforms like preregistration of
hypotheses, larger samples, and open data. The lesson is general to all science: a
single striking result is a hypothesis, not a fact, until independently replicated.

[assistant] The replication crisis is the discovery that a large share of published
psychology (and other) findings don't hold up when other labs repeat the studies. A
big driver was "researcher degrees of freedom" — analyzing data many ways and reporting

far more credible, and a null result still gets documented rather than buried. Combined
with larger samples and open data, it shifts the field from chasing eye-catching
one-off findings toward results that reliably replicate.

## History (global and comparative)

Comparative and global history resist the tendency to treat one society's path as the
template for all. World-systems analysis frames the modern era as a single
interdependent economy with a wealthy "core" and an exploited "periphery," explaining
underdevelopment not as backwardness but as a relationship produced by global trade.
Comparative history sets cases side by side — why did industrialization begin in
Britain and not in China, despite China's earlier technological lead? — to isolate the
factors that actually made the difference, guarding against explanations that merely
restate the outcome. The discipline increasingly studies connections (trade, disease,
migration, ideas) that cross the borders national histories take for granted.

The exchange of crops, animals, people, and pathogens across the Atlantic after 1492 —
the Columbian Exchange — reshaped the entire planet's demography and diet. New World
crops like the potato and maize fueled population growth across Europe, Africa, and
Asia; Old World diseases like smallpox devastated Indigenous American populations who
lacked immunity, a catastrophe that enabled European conquest as much as any weapon.
This is environmental history's central lesson: biological and ecological forces are
not a backdrop to human history but active agents within it.

on top of those biological events. It's the clearest case for environmental history's
claim that ecology is an agent in human affairs, not merely its stage.

## Literature (drama and the essay)

Drama is literature written to be performed, and its meaning lives partly in elements
prose lacks: staging, gesture, silence, and the physical presence of actors before an
audience. Dramatic irony — where the audience knows what a character does not — generates
much of theater's tension, as when we watch a character walk knowingly toward a doom
they cannot see. The classical unities of time, place, and action constrained much early
drama; modern theater broke them, and Brecht's "alienation effect" deliberately reminded
audiences they were watching a constructed play, refusing the comfortable illusion in
order to provoke critical thought rather than passive emotion.

The essay is the great form of exploratory nonfiction, from Montaigne's intimate
self-examinations to the argumentative and lyric essays of today. Its hallmark is a mind
thinking on the page — testing a question rather than merely delivering a conclusion (the
word "essay" comes from the French for "to attempt"). A strong essay earns its claims
through evidence and reasoning while retaining a distinctive voice, occupying the ground
between the impersonality of the report and the invention of fiction.
