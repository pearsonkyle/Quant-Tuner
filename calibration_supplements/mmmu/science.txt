## Biology

The cell is the fundamental unit of life, and the deepest division in biology is
between prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea), whose DNA floats free in the cytoplasm,
and eukaryotes (everything else), whose DNA is packaged in a membrane-bound nucleus
and whose cells contain organelles. Mitochondria, the eukaryotic powerhouses,
generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation and carry their own circular DNA — a
relic of their origin as free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral host, the
endosymbiotic theory. Chloroplasts in plants have a parallel origin and run the
light reactions of photosynthesis.

The central dogma describes the flow of genetic information: DNA is transcribed into
messenger RNA, which is translated by ribosomes into protein. DNA is a double helix
of complementary base pairs — adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine — held by
hydrogen bonds, and the sequence of bases is a code read in triplets (codons), each
specifying one amino acid. The genetic code is redundant (multiple codons per amino
acid) and nearly universal across life, which is itself strong evidence of common
descent and what makes genetic engineering across species possible.

Evolution by natural selection is the organizing theory of all biology. Heritable
variation arises by mutation and recombination; individuals better suited to their
environment leave more offspring; over generations the population's allele
frequencies shift. Selection is not the only force — genetic drift (random changes,
especially in small populations), gene flow (migration), and mutation all alter
frequencies — but selection is the only one that builds adaptation. Speciation occurs
when populations become reproductively isolated and diverge until they can no longer
interbreed.

Cellular respiration and photosynthesis are complementary energy transformations.
Photosynthesis captures light energy to build glucose from CO2 and water, releasing
O2: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light -> C6H12O6 + 6O2. Respiration runs the reverse, oxidizing
glucose to release energy as ATP: C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy. Together
they cycle carbon and energy through the biosphere, the products of one feeding the
other.

## Chemistry

Chemistry is governed by electrons. The periodic table organizes elements by atomic
number and recurring valence-electron configuration, which is why elements in the
same column (group) share chemical behavior — the alkali metals all have one easily
lost valence electron, the noble gases all have full shells and are inert. Periodic
trends follow from effective nuclear charge: atomic radius shrinks left-to-right
across a period and grows down a group, while ionization energy and electronegativity
do the opposite, rising toward fluorine in the upper right.

Bonding is the redistribution of electrons to reach stable configurations. Ionic
bonds transfer electrons from metal to nonmetal, forming charged ions held by
electrostatic attraction (NaCl). Covalent bonds share electron pairs between
nonmetals; when shared unequally (differing electronegativity) the bond is polar,
giving water its bent, polar geometry and the hydrogen bonding that underlies its
extraordinary properties. Metallic bonding pools valence electrons into a "sea" that
makes metals conductive and malleable. Molecular shape follows VSEPR theory —
electron pairs arrange to minimize repulsion, so four bonding pairs give a tetrahedron
(109.5°) and the lone pairs on water push its H–O–H angle to about 104.5°.

Stoichiometry is the bookkeeping of reactions, built on the mole — Avogadro's number
(6.022 × 10^23) of particles. A balanced equation gives the fixed molar ratios in
which species react, so the limiting reactant — the one that runs out first —
determines how much product forms. Thermodynamics then tells whether a reaction is
spontaneous: the Gibbs free energy change ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative for spontaneous
processes, balancing the enthalpy change (heat) against the entropy change (disorder)
scaled by temperature.

Acid–base chemistry centers on proton transfer. The pH scale measures hydrogen-ion
concentration logarithmically: pH = −log[H+], so each unit is a tenfold change, and a
neutral solution at 25 °C has pH 7. Strong acids dissociate completely; weak acids
establish an equilibrium described by their Ka, and a buffer — a weak acid with its
conjugate base — resists pH change, the principle behind blood's bicarbonate buffer.

## Geography

Geography studies the spatial organization of the Earth's physical and human systems.
Physical geography explains the patterns of climate, landforms, and ecosystems. Global
climate is driven by the unequal heating of the Earth — the equator receives more
direct solar energy than the poles — which sets up atmospheric circulation cells
(Hadley, Ferrel, Polar) that redistribute heat and create the planet's bands of wet
and dry climate: rainforests near the rising air of the equator, deserts near the
descending air around 30° latitude.

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of physical geography and geology. The
lithosphere is broken into plates that move on the ductile asthenosphere, and most of
the planet's dramatic features occur at their boundaries. Convergent boundaries build
mountains and volcanic arcs and spawn deep ocean trenches and great earthquakes;
divergent boundaries create new crust at mid-ocean ridges; transform boundaries, like
the San Andreas, grind past one another and store the strain that releases as
earthquakes. The distribution of volcanoes and earthquakes around the Pacific "Ring
of Fire" is the surface signature of these processes.

Human geography studies how populations, cultures, and economies distribute
themselves and reshape the land. Demographic transition theory describes how
societies move from high birth and death rates (pre-industrial), through a phase of
falling death rates and rapid population growth, to low birth and death rates
(post-industrial), reshaping age structures and economies. Urbanization concentrates
population in cities, where models of internal structure — concentric zones, sectors,
multiple nuclei — try to explain the spatial pattern of land use, and where issues of
sprawl, transport, and inequality play out spatially.

Maps are the discipline's core tool, and every map projection trades off distortion.
The Mercator projection preserves direction and shape locally (useful for navigation)
but grossly inflates the area of high-latitude regions, making Greenland appear as
large as Africa though Africa is fourteen times larger. Equal-area projections
preserve relative size at the cost of shape. There is no perfect flat map of a sphere;
the choice of projection is always a choice about which truth to keep and which to
sacrifice.

## Math

Mathematics is the study of structure, and calculus is its tool for studying change.
The derivative measures an instantaneous rate of change — the slope of a curve at a
point — defined as the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to
zero. The integral measures accumulation — the area under a curve — and the
fundamental theorem of calculus reveals that differentiation and integration are
inverse operations, tying the two halves of the subject together. With these tools
one can find maxima and minima (where the derivative is zero), compute areas and
volumes, and model any system whose state evolves continuously.

Linear algebra is the mathematics of vectors, matrices, and linear transformations,
and it is the language of modern data and computation. A matrix represents a linear
map; multiplying a vector by a matrix transforms it. Eigenvectors are the special
directions a transformation merely scales (by their eigenvalue) without rotating, and
they reveal a transformation's intrinsic axes — the principle behind dimensionality
reduction, vibration modes, and the stability of dynamical systems. A system of linear
equations Ax = b has a unique solution exactly when A is invertible (nonzero
determinant).

Probability quantifies uncertainty. The probability of independent events both
occurring is the product of their probabilities; for mutually exclusive events, the
probability of either is the sum. Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem let us
update beliefs given evidence: P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B). The law of large numbers
guarantees that sample averages converge to the true mean, and the central limit
theorem explains why sums of many independent effects are approximately normally
distributed — the reason the bell curve appears everywhere.

Proof is what distinguishes mathematics from the empirical sciences. A statement is
true not because it has been tested but because it follows by valid deduction from
axioms. Common proof techniques include direct proof, proof by contradiction (assume
the negation and derive an absurdity), and mathematical induction (prove a base case
and that each case implies the next). The irrationality of √2 and the infinitude of
the primes are classic proofs by contradiction.

## Physics

Physics seeks the simplest laws that govern matter, energy, space, and time. Classical
mechanics begins with Newton's three laws: a body keeps its velocity unless acted on
by a net force (inertia); force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma); and every
action has an equal and opposite reaction. From these follow the conservation laws —
of momentum, energy, and angular momentum — which are often more powerful than the
forces themselves because they hold even when the detailed forces are complicated or
unknown. Energy is never created or destroyed, only transformed between kinetic,
potential, thermal, and other forms.

Thermodynamics governs heat and the direction of processes. The first law is energy
conservation including heat; the second law states that the entropy of an isolated
system never decreases, which defines the arrow of time and forbids perpetual-motion
machines. Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, never the reverse without work
input, and no engine can convert heat entirely into work — the Carnot efficiency sets
the ceiling, rising with the temperature difference between the hot and cold
reservoirs.

Electromagnetism unifies electricity and magnetism. Charges create electric fields and
feel forces in them (Coulomb's law); moving charges create magnetic fields; and
changing magnetic fields induce electric fields (Faraday's law), the principle of
every generator and transformer. Maxwell's equations bundle these together and predict
that oscillating fields propagate as electromagnetic waves at the speed of light —
revealing light itself to be an electromagnetic wave, and uniting optics with
electromagnetism.

Modern physics broke the classical picture twice. Relativity showed that space and
time are not absolute: the speed of light is the same for all observers, so measured
lengths contract and clocks run slow at high relative speeds, and mass and energy are
equivalent (E = mc^2). Quantum mechanics showed that at small scales energy comes in
discrete packets, particles have wave properties, and certain pairs of quantities
(position and momentum) cannot both be known precisely — the uncertainty principle.
Together these theories underpin everything from GPS (which corrects for relativistic
time dilation) to semiconductors and lasers (quantum devices).

Worked kinematics: a ball is thrown straight up at 20 m/s (g = 10 m/s^2).
  Time to peak: v = v0 − g t -> 0 = 20 − 10 t -> t = 2.0 s.
  Max height: h = v0 t − ½ g t^2 = 20(2) − ½(10)(2^2) = 40 − 20 = 20 m.
  Total flight time (return to start): 4.0 s by symmetry.

## Biology (systems and ecology)

At larger scales, biology becomes the study of systems and their regulation.
Homeostasis is the maintenance of stable internal conditions through negative
feedback: a sensor detects deviation, a control center compares it to a set point,
and effectors push the variable back — the way the body regulates temperature, blood
glucose, and pH. Positive feedback, by contrast, amplifies a change and is rarer,
used for processes that must run to completion, like blood clotting or childbirth.

Ecology studies interactions among organisms and their environment. Energy flows one
way through ecosystems — captured by producers, passed to consumers, lost as heat at
each step — which is why food chains are short and biomass forms a pyramid: only about
10% of energy transfers between trophic levels. Matter, in contrast, cycles: the
carbon, nitrogen, and water cycles route key elements through living and nonliving
reservoirs. Population growth is exponential when resources are unlimited but levels
off at the carrying capacity (logistic growth) as competition, predation, and disease
intensify.

Q: In a food chain, why is biomass at the top (top predators) so much smaller than at

D. Decomposers remove biomass from the top.
Reasoning: The ~10% ecological efficiency between trophic levels means each level has
roughly a tenth of the energy of the one below, limiting how much biomass higher
levels can sustain — hence the energy/biomass pyramid. Answer: B.

## Chemistry (organic, equilibrium, kinetics)

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon, whose four bonds let it form chains,
rings, and branched skeletons of unlimited variety. Functional groups — the reactive
sites attached to a hydrocarbon framework — dictate behavior: hydroxyl (–OH) makes
alcohols, carbonyl (C=O) makes aldehydes and ketones, carboxyl (–COOH) makes acids,
amino (–NH2) makes amines. Isomers share a molecular formula but differ in
arrangement; structural isomers differ in connectivity, while stereoisomers differ
only in spatial orientation, and chirality — a carbon bonded to four different groups
— produces mirror-image enantiomers that can behave very differently in biology, the
reason a drug's "handedness" can matter enormously.

Chemical equilibrium is a dynamic balance where forward and reverse reactions proceed
at equal rates, so concentrations stop changing. The equilibrium constant K expresses
the ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium; a large K favors products.
Le Chatelier's principle predicts the response to a disturbance: stress the system —
add reactant, remove product, change temperature or pressure — and the equilibrium
shifts to partially counteract the change. Adding reactant pushes the reaction toward
products; for an exothermic reaction, raising temperature shifts it back toward
reactants.

Reaction kinetics studies rates rather than positions of equilibrium. The rate
depends on concentration (through the rate law), temperature (rates roughly double per
10 °C, per the Arrhenius relation), and the activation energy — the barrier reactants
must surmount. A catalyst speeds a reaction by providing a lower-energy pathway
without being consumed, and crucially does not change the equilibrium position, only
how fast it is reached. Enzymes are nature's catalysts, accelerating biochemical
reactions by enormous factors with exquisite specificity.

Q: An exothermic reaction at equilibrium is heated. Le Chatelier's principle predicts
the equilibrium shifts:
A. Toward products, increasing K.   B. Toward reactants, decreasing K.   C. No shift.
D. Toward products, decreasing K.
Reasoning: For an exothermic reaction, heat is effectively a product; adding heat
shifts the equilibrium toward reactants to absorb it, and K decreases. Answer: B.

Q: Adding a catalyst to a reaction at equilibrium will:
A. Increase the yield of product.   B. Shift the equilibrium toward products.
C. Speed the approach to equilibrium without changing the equilibrium position.
D. Increase K.
Reasoning: A catalyst lowers the activation energy for both forward and reverse
reactions equally, so it reaches the same equilibrium faster but does not change K or
the final yield. Answer: C.

## Math (sequences, geometry, and statistics)

Sequences and series formalize patterns of numbers. An arithmetic sequence adds a
constant difference each term; a geometric sequence multiplies by a constant ratio.
A geometric series with ratio |r| < 1 converges to a finite sum a/(1−r), which is why
infinitely many ever-smaller pieces can add to a finite whole — the resolution of
Zeno's paradox. Power series extend this idea, representing functions like e^x, sin x,
and cos x as infinite polynomials, the foundation of how calculators and computers
evaluate transcendental functions.

Geometry connects algebra to space. The Pythagorean theorem (a^2 + b^2 = c^2) governs
right triangles and generalizes to the distance formula in coordinate geometry.
Trigonometry relates angles to ratios of sides (sine, cosine, tangent) and, through
the unit circle, to periodic functions that model every oscillation in nature. Similar
figures preserve shape and scale all lengths by a common ratio (and areas by its
square, volumes by its cube) — the scaling law that explains why large animals need
proportionally thicker bones.

Statistics turns data into inference. Descriptive statistics summarize a dataset's
center (mean, median, mode) and spread (range, variance, standard deviation).
Inferential statistics generalizes from a sample to a population, quantifying
uncertainty with confidence intervals and testing hypotheses with p-values — the
probability of observing data as extreme as the sample if the null hypothesis were
true. Correlation measures linear association but does not imply causation; only a
controlled, randomized experiment licenses a causal claim, because randomization
balances confounders across groups.

## Physics (waves, optics, and circuits)

Waves transport energy without transporting matter. A wave's speed equals its
frequency times its wavelength (v = fλ); for a given medium the speed is roughly
fixed, so higher frequency means shorter wavelength. Waves reflect, refract (bend when
changing speed between media), diffract (spread around obstacles), and interfere —
adding constructively where crests meet crests and destructively where crests meet
troughs. Standing waves on a string fix the harmonic frequencies of musical
instruments; the Doppler effect shifts the perceived frequency of a moving source,
raising the pitch of an approaching siren and lowering it as it passes.

Geometric optics treats light as rays. Refraction at a lens or the eye focuses
parallel rays to a point, and Snell's law (n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2) quantifies the
bending; beyond a critical angle light undergoes total internal reflection, the
principle of optical fibers. A converging lens forms a real, inverted image of a
distant object at its focal length — the basis of cameras and the eye — while
correcting vision means adding a lens that shifts the focal point onto the retina.

Electric circuits obey Ohm's law (V = IR): voltage is the push, current the flow, and
resistance the opposition. Power dissipated is P = IV = I^2R. In a series circuit the
same current flows through every element and resistances add; in a parallel circuit
the voltage is shared and the reciprocals of resistances add, so parallel paths lower
total resistance. Kirchhoff's laws — charge is conserved at junctions, and voltage
sums to zero around any loop — let one solve any network, however complex.

Worked optics: an object 30 cm from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Using
1/f = 1/do + 1/di: 1/10 = 1/30 + 1/di -> 1/di = 1/10 − 1/30 = 2/30 -> di = 15 cm.
The image is real (positive di), inverted, and reduced (magnification = −di/do =
−15/30 = −0.5).

Worked circuit: a 12 V battery across two resistors, 4 Ω and 2 Ω, in series.
  Total R = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω; current I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A.
  Voltage across the 4 Ω: V = IR = 2 × 4 = 8 V; across the 2 Ω: 4 V (sums to 12 V).
  Power from the battery: P = IV = 2 × 12 = 24 W.

Q: An ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches and lower as it recedes.

A. Refraction   B. The Doppler effect   C. Total internal reflection   D. Diffraction
Reasoning: The change in perceived frequency due to relative motion of source and
observer is the Doppler effect — approaching compresses the wavefronts (higher pitch),
receding stretches them (lower pitch). Answer: B.

## Geography (climate, hydrology, and resources)

The hydrologic cycle moves water through the Earth system: evaporation lifts water to
the atmosphere, condensation forms clouds, precipitation returns it, and runoff and
infiltration route it through rivers and aquifers back to the sea. Watersheds organize
the landscape into drainage basins, and the residence time of water varies enormously
— days in the atmosphere, thousands of years in deep groundwater and ice sheets. Human
demand increasingly outpaces recharge in many aquifers, making groundwater depletion a
defining resource issue.

Climate classification (the Köppen system) groups the world's climates by temperature
and precipitation patterns that align closely with vegetation: tropical, dry,
temperate, continental, and polar. Climate differs from weather in timescale — weather
is the atmosphere's state now, climate is its statistical pattern over decades. The
greenhouse effect, by which atmospheric gases trap outgoing infrared radiation, keeps
the planet habitable; the concern of climate change is the rapid enhancement of that
effect by added CO2 and methane, shifting the statistical baseline of extremes.
