## Phonetics and Phonology

Linguistics studies language as a natural phenomenon, describing what speakers actually do rather
than prescribing what they should. This descriptive commitment is the field's foundational stance
and it distinguishes linguistics from the tradition of usage guides: a construction that native
speakers produce systematically is part of the language by definition, whatever its social
standing.

Phonetics studies speech sounds as physical events. Articulatory phonetics describes how sounds
are produced — where in the vocal tract the constriction occurs, how the airstream is obstructed,
and whether the vocal folds vibrate. These three parameters classify consonants completely, so a
sound is identified as, for instance, a voiced alveolar stop, and the classification is
cross-linguistic rather than tied to any writing system.

Phonology studies how sounds function within a particular language's system, and the central
concept is the distinction between a phoneme and its realizations. A phoneme is a sound category
that distinguishes meaning; an allophone is a variant occurring in a specific context without
distinguishing meaning. The aspirated and unaspirated realizations of a stop are separate phonemes
in some languages, so substituting one for the other changes the word, and are allophones of one
phoneme in others, so the substitution produces an accent rather than a different word.

Q: A language learner substitutes one sound for another and native speakers hear an accent rather
than a different word. This indicates that the two sounds are:
A. Separate phonemes in that language
B. Allophones of a single phoneme, so the substitution does not cross a meaning-distinguishing
   boundary
C. Not present in the language
D. Difficult to produce
Reasoning: A phonemic distinction changes the word; an allophonic difference does not. Hearing an
accent rather than a different word means the substitution stayed within one phoneme category.
This is why learners find some distinctions in a new language nearly inaudible — their native
phonology assigns both to one category, so the contrast was never attended to. Answer: B.

## Morphology and Syntax

Morphology studies word structure. A morpheme is the smallest unit carrying meaning or grammatical
function, and words decompose into them: a stem carrying the core meaning plus affixes modifying
it. Inflectional morphology marks grammatical categories — tense, number, case, agreement — without
changing the word's class. Derivational morphology creates new words, frequently changing the
class.

Languages differ enormously in how much they use morphology, and the variation is a major
typological axis. Isolating languages have close to one morpheme per word and express grammatical
relations by word order and separate particles. Agglutinative languages stack many morphemes, each
with one clear function, producing words that translate as whole English clauses. Fusional
languages combine several functions into a single affix that cannot be decomposed. Polysynthetic
languages incorporate what other languages express as separate words into a single verb.

Syntax studies how words combine into phrases and sentences, and the central observation is that
sentences have hierarchical structure rather than being flat sequences. Evidence for the hierarchy
comes from constituency tests: units that can be moved as a whole, replaced by a single word, or
serve as a fragment answer are constituents, and the groupings those tests reveal are the same ones
that ambiguity and agreement patterns require.

Structural ambiguity is the clearest evidence. A sentence with one word sequence and two meanings
has two possible structures, and the meanings correspond to the structures — a modifier attaching
at one point or another. This establishes that the structure is real rather than an analytical
convenience, since the meaning difference has no other source.

Morphology and syntax:

Recursion deserves emphasis because it explains how a finite system produces unlimited sentences. A
noun phrase can contain a clause which contains a noun phrase, and there is no principled limit to
the nesting, so the set of possible sentences is infinite while the grammar is finite. This is why
a language cannot be a list and must be a generative system, and it is the property that any
account of linguistic knowledge must explain.

## Semantics and Pragmatics

Semantics studies meaning as encoded in linguistic form, independent of context. Lexical semantics
concerns word meaning and the relations among words — synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy, and the
polysemy by which one word carries related senses. Compositional semantics concerns how the
meanings of parts combine into the meaning of the whole, which is what permits understanding a
sentence never encountered before.

The principle of compositionality states that a complex expression's meaning is determined by its
parts and the way they are combined. It is what makes language learnable and productive, and its
apparent counterexamples are informative: idioms have meanings not derivable from their parts,
which is exactly why they must be learned as units.

Pragmatics studies meaning in context, and the central observation is that speakers routinely
convey more than they say. Grice's cooperative principle holds that participants assume a
conversation is a cooperative activity governed by expectations — that contributions are
appropriately informative, truthful, relevant, and clear. Implicature arises when a speaker
apparently violates one of these while remaining cooperative, so the hearer infers additional
meaning to restore the assumption.

The mechanism is worth stating concretely. Saying that some of the students passed implicates that
not all did, because a cooperative speaker who knew all had passed would have said so — the
stronger statement was available and was not made. This is an inference from the speaker's choice
rather than from the sentence's meaning, which is why it can be cancelled: adding that in fact all
of them did is not a contradiction.

Presupposition deserves attention because it makes content difficult to challenge. Asserted
content invites agreement or disagreement; presupposed content is taken for granted and slips past
without evaluation. The negation test distinguishes them — a presupposition survives negation,
since both a sentence and its denial presuppose the same background — and the practical
consequence is that a question or statement embedding a presupposition smuggles it in. This is why
loaded questions work and why the rhetorically effective move is to reject the presupposition
explicitly rather than to answer the question.

## Language Change, Variation, and Acquisition

Language change is continuous, systematic, and inevitable. It occurs at every level: sounds shift,
words change meaning, grammatical constructions arise and disappear, and the changes are regular
enough that they can be traced backward to reconstruct unattested ancestor languages. The
comparative method compares related languages, identifies systematic correspondences, and infers
the forms from which they descend — and its predictions have been confirmed when previously unknown
languages were discovered.

Sound change is the most regular. A change applies to a sound in a specified environment across the
whole vocabulary rather than word by word, which is why the correspondences between related
languages are systematic rather than sporadic. This regularity is what makes reconstruction
possible and what made historical linguistics the first branch of the field to achieve
methodological rigour.

Variation within a language is patterned rather than random, and sociolinguistics studies its
correlation with social factors — region, class, age, gender, ethnicity, and the formality of the
situation. The finding that matters most for how language is discussed is that non-standard
varieties are rule-governed systems rather than degraded versions of a standard. A variety with
different agreement patterns, different negation, or different tense marking has its own consistent
grammar, and speakers of it make errors in it just as speakers of any variety do.

Language acquisition is remarkable for its speed and uniformity. Children acquire a complete
grammar within a few years, from input that is finite and contains no explicit instruction about
grammar, and they converge on the same system as their community. The stages are consistent across
languages and cultures, and children produce forms they have never heard — regularizing irregular
verbs, for instance — which demonstrates that they are inferring rules rather than imitating.

Grammaticalization deserves mention because it explains where grammatical machinery comes from. A
content word with lexical meaning gradually becomes a grammatical marker: a verb meaning to go
becomes a future tense marker, a noun meaning back becomes a preposition meaning behind, a
demonstrative becomes a definite article. The process is unidirectional — grammatical markers do
not become content words — and it is observable across unrelated languages, which is why languages
that have never been in contact develop similar grammatical categories by similar routes.

## Typology and Writing Systems

Linguistic typology classifies languages by their structural properties rather than by their
descent, and its finding is that the variation is bounded. Languages differ enormously and not
arbitrarily: certain properties cluster, and certain logically possible systems do not occur.
Implicational universals capture this — if a language has a given property it also has another —
and they constrain what a possible human language is.

Word order correlations are the clearest instance. A language placing the verb before the object
tends to place prepositions before their nouns, auxiliaries before verbs, and nouns before relative
clauses; a language placing the object before the verb tends toward the mirror image. This is
consistent across unrelated languages and families, which suggests a processing or cognitive
explanation rather than a historical one.

Writing systems are technologies for representing language and are independent of the languages
themselves — a language can be written in several systems and a system can serve several languages.
They differ in what unit they represent. Alphabets represent consonants and vowels. Abjads
represent consonants with vowels optional or absent. Abugidas represent consonant-vowel syllables
with the vowel marked as a modification. Syllabaries represent syllables as units. And logographic
systems represent morphemes.

  word order correlations  head-initial and head-final patterns cluster

The distinction between writing and language deserves the closing emphasis because conflating them
distorts most popular reasoning about language. Writing is a comparatively recent technology
representing language imperfectly, spoken language is acquired without instruction while writing
requires years of teaching, and the properties of a writing system tell you little about the
language it encodes. Arguments about correct usage frequently appeal to spelling or to written
convention, which is appealing to the properties of the technology rather than to the language, and
the mismatch between English spelling and English pronunciation is a fact about a writing system
that was standardized before several sound changes completed rather than a fact about the language
at all.
