## Normative Frameworks

Ethics asks what one ought to do, and the major frameworks answer by identifying different
features of an action as morally decisive. They are best treated as complementary lenses that
surface different considerations rather than as competing theories one of which is correct, since
each captures something the others handle poorly and each produces conclusions its adherents find
uncomfortable.

Consequentialism holds that the rightness of an action depends on its outcomes, with utilitarianism
identifying the relevant outcome as aggregate wellbeing. Its strengths are that it takes
consequences seriously, that it treats everyone's interests as counting equally, and that it gives
a determinate answer where other frameworks are silent. Its difficulties are that it permits
serious harm to individuals when the aggregate improves, that it requires predicting consequences
that are frequently unknowable, and that aggregating wellbeing across people assumes a comparison
that may not be well defined.

Deontology holds that some actions are right or wrong independent of their consequences, typically
grounded in duties or in rights. Its strengths are that it protects individuals against being used
as means to collective ends, that it does not require predicting the future, and that it captures
the intuition that some things must not be done regardless of the benefit. Its difficulties are
that duties conflict with no principled resolution, and that it can require adherence to a rule
when the consequences of doing so are catastrophic.

Virtue ethics asks what a person of good character would do, shifting attention from the act to
the agent. Its strengths are that it accounts for the role of character, motivation, and practical
wisdom, and that it addresses how to become the kind of person who acts well rather than only what
to do in a given case. Its difficulty is that it provides less guidance in specific cases, since
determining what a virtuous person would do frequently requires already knowing the answer.

Q: A framework recommends an action that seems clearly wrong on reflection. The appropriate
response is:
A. Accept the recommendation, since the framework is systematic
B. Treat the conflict as evidence about either the framework's application or the judgment, and
   adjust whichever survives scrutiny less well — the method of reflective equilibrium
C. Abandon the framework
D. Ignore the judgment
Reasoning: Neither principles nor particular judgments have automatic priority. A strong
considered judgment conflicting with a principle is evidence against the principle or its
application, and a principle with wide support is evidence against the judgment. Working back and
forth until they cohere is the standard method, and it treats both as revisable. Answer: B.

## Metaethics and the Limits of Argument

Metaethics asks what moral claims mean and whether they can be true, and the positions matter
practically because they determine what a moral disagreement is. Moral realism holds that moral
claims describe objective facts and can be true or false. Anti-realist positions hold variously
that they express attitudes, that they are systematically false, or that their truth is relative to
a framework.

The is-ought gap is the observation that a normative conclusion cannot be derived from purely
descriptive premises. Establishing what is the case does not by itself establish what ought to be
done, and an argument moving from one to the other has a suppressed normative premise. This is
worth recognizing because arguments of that form are common — from what is natural, from what is
statistically typical, from what has historically been done — and identifying the missing premise
is what permits it to be examined.

The naturalistic fallacy is the related error of inferring goodness from naturalness, and it
deserves specific mention because it recurs in discussions of technology, medicine, and social
arrangements. That something occurs in nature, or has evolved, or is traditional, establishes
nothing about whether it is good — disease is natural, and so is the impulse to treat it.

Metaethics:
  moral realism        moral claims describe objective facts
  anti-realism         they express attitudes, or are relative, or are
                       systematically false
  moral intuitions     the data any theory must account for; strong widely shared
                       intuitions carry evidential weight
  is-ought gap         normative conclusions need a normative premise
  naturalistic fallacy  inferring good from natural; naturalness establishes
                       nothing
  moral relativism     the descriptive claim that moralities differ is
                       uncontroversial; the normative claim that all are equally
                       valid does not follow and is rarely held consistently
  moral uncertainty    acting under uncertainty about which framework is correct;
                       an active area with practical implications for

  the demandingness objection  a theory requiring constant maximal sacrifice is

  disagreement         persistent moral disagreement is compatible with realism,
                       as persistent scientific disagreement is

Moral uncertainty deserves attention because it is the practical situation. Someone who finds
consequentialist reasoning compelling in some cases and deontological constraints compelling in
others is not confused; they are uncertain which framework is correct, which is a reasonable state
given that the question has been debated for millennia without resolution. Acting under that
uncertainty argues for choices that are acceptable across the frameworks one takes seriously rather
than optimal under one — which is why an action strongly condemned by any major framework warrants
caution even from someone who does not hold that framework.

## Applied Reasoning and Professional Ethics

Applied ethics addresses specific domains, and the recurring structure is that the difficult cases
involve genuine conflicts between considerations that all matter rather than a clear right answer
that someone is failing to see. Recognizing this changes the task from determining who is correct
to identifying what is at stake on each side and whether an option exists that respects both.

The distinction between an ethical dilemma and a temptation is worth keeping clear. A dilemma is a
genuine conflict between two obligations, where satisfying one means failing the other and no
option is fully right. A temptation is a conflict between what one should do and what one wants,
which is not a dilemma however difficult it feels. Most situations described as ethical dilemmas
are temptations, and the distinction matters because they require different responses — a dilemma
requires judgment about which obligation takes precedence, and a temptation requires resolve.

Professional ethics binds practitioners to standards beyond ordinary morality, and the
justification is asymmetry: a professional has knowledge and power that the person relying on them
lacks and cannot readily evaluate. The client cannot assess whether the engineering is sound, the
diagnosis correct, or the advice self-serving, so the profession's standards substitute for the
scrutiny the client cannot apply.

Engineering ethics has a specific feature that distinguishes it: the primary obligation is to
public safety, and it takes precedence over obligations to the employer and the client. This is
stated in essentially every engineering code of ethics and it is what makes the codes
consequential, since it establishes that an engineer may be professionally required to act against
their employer's instruction.

Conflict of interest deserves clarification because it is frequently treated as an accusation. It
is a situation in which a person's obligations or interests could compromise their judgment, and
being in one is not wrongdoing — it is a circumstance that arises routinely through ordinary
professional life. The wrongdoing is failing to disclose it or acting within it when recusal was
required. Treating the existence of a conflict as an accusation discourages disclosure, which
produces exactly the undisclosed conflicts the rule exists to prevent.

## Technology Ethics

Technology ethics has become substantively important because technical decisions increasingly
have consequences at scale, and the person making them frequently has more effective authority
over the outcome than anyone with formal responsibility. A choice about a default setting, a
ranking function, a data retention period, or a threshold affects millions of people and is made
by someone with no mandate to make a policy decision.

The recurring pattern is that a system optimizing a measurable proxy diverges from the intended
goal, and the divergence is discovered after deployment at scale. Engagement optimization produces
content that engages and does not satisfy. Efficiency optimization in a decision system reproduces
the patterns in its training data, which encode historical decisions. Personalization produces
narrowing. In each case the system did what it was built to do and the specification was
incomplete.

Privacy has a structure worth understanding beyond the intuitive one. It is not only about
secrecy but about contextual integrity — information appropriate in one context is a violation in
another, so the question is whether a flow of information matches the norms of the context it came
from. Medical information shared with a doctor and then with an insurer violates this without any
of it being secret. This framing explains why aggregation is a privacy harm even when every
component was individually public.

The proxy divergence pattern deserves emphasis because recognizing it prospectively is the most
useful contribution a technically informed person can make. Any system optimizing a measurable
quantity will find the cheapest way to increase it, and if the measurement diverges from the intent
anywhere, the system will locate and exploit that divergence. Asking, before deployment, what the
cheapest way to improve the metric would be, and whether that is what is wanted, surfaces most of
these failures at the design stage rather than at scale.

Your training data is historical hiring outcomes, which means the model learns to reproduce past
hiring decisions rather than to predict job performance. If past decisions reflected bias, the
model learns the bias and applies it consistently and at scale, with the appearance of objectivity.
The question to ask is what the labels actually represent — "was hired" and "performed well" are
different targets, and only the second is what you want. If you only have the first, be explicit
that you are automating the historical process rather than improving it.

Excluding protected attributes from the features does not remove their influence and does remove
your ability to measure it. Other features correlate with them — postal code, education history,
gaps in employment, activities — so the model reconstructs the attribute and its effect persists
invisibly. You need the attributes available for measurement even if not for prediction, which
sits awkwardly with regulations restricting their collection and is a tension to resolve
deliberately rather than by avoidance.

Human review at the final stage is less protective than it appears. Automation bias means
reviewers defer to the system's ranking, particularly under time pressure and particularly when
the system is usually right. A reviewer seeing candidates pre-sorted rarely overturns the ordering.
If the review is meant to be a safeguard, it needs to be structured so that the reviewer forms an
independent judgment before seeing the score.

There is no recourse specified for affected individuals. Someone screened out has no way to know
why, to contest it, or to correct an error in the data. This is a substantive fairness question and
in many jurisdictions a legal requirement, since employment decisions are regulated and automated
decision-making with significant effects carries specific obligations.

Two process recommendations. Measure the outcome disparity across groups on a held-out set before
deployment and periodically after, since you cannot address what you do not measure. And write
down what the system is and is not intended to do, what it was trained on, and its known
limitations — a model card — because the people who use it in two years will not be the people who
built it, and they will otherwise assume it does more than it does.

## Research Ethics and Institutional Design

Research ethics developed in response to documented abuses, and the resulting requirements are
specific and consequential rather than procedural. Informed consent requires that participants
understand what they are agreeing to, agree voluntarily without coercion, and have the capacity to
consent. Risks must be minimized and justified by the expected benefit. Vulnerable populations —
those with limited capacity, those in dependent relationships, those with limited alternatives —
require additional protection because the voluntariness condition is harder to satisfy.

Research integrity concerns the conduct of the work itself. Fabrication and falsification of data
and plagiarism are the recognized categories of misconduct, and the far more common problems are
questionable practices that fall short of them: selective reporting, undisclosed analytic
flexibility, hypothesizing after results are known, and salami-slicing results across publications.
These are not misconduct by the formal definition and they distort the literature substantially,
which is why the reforms have focused on preregistration and disclosure rather than on enforcement.

Institutional design is what determines whether ethical behaviour is the default or requires
individual heroism, and this is the practical lever. A system where raising a concern is costly
produces few concerns raised. A system where the incentives reward the outcome regardless of how it
was achieved produces the outcome achieved however necessary. Designing so that the ethical action
is the easy one, and that raising a problem is safe and expected, accomplishes more than exhorting
people to behave well.

The final test deserves the closing emphasis because it is the one that generalizes. Formal
frameworks require interpretation, professional codes cover anticipated situations, and neither
resolves a novel case under time pressure. Asking whether one would be comfortable with the
decision being public, and whether one could explain it to the person most affected without
embarrassment, catches most of what matters. It is not a theory and it is a reliable heuristic,
and its usefulness comes from the fact that the reasoning that cannot survive being stated plainly
to the affected party is usually the reasoning that should not have been acted on.
