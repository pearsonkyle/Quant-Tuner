## The Accounting Equation and Double Entry

Accounting records economic activity so that a firm's position and performance can be assessed,
and the entire system rests on one identity: assets equal liabilities plus equity. Everything a
firm controls was funded either by borrowing or by owners, so the two sides balance by
construction rather than by coincidence.

Double-entry bookkeeping is the mechanism enforcing that identity. Every transaction is recorded
in at least two accounts with equal debits and credits, so the equation holds after each entry.
The system's value is not merely arithmetic checking but that it records both what happened and
where the resources came from — a purchase records the asset acquired and the cash spent or the
obligation incurred, so the complete picture is captured rather than only one side.

The debit and credit convention is a persistent source of confusion because the words have no
intuitive meaning in this context. Debit is the left side and credit is the right, and whether
either increases or decreases an account depends on the account type. Assets and expenses increase
with debits; liabilities, equity, and revenue increase with credits. The rule follows from the
equation's structure — assets on the left increase on the left — and it must be memorized rather
than reasoned about each time.

Q: A firm purchases equipment for cash. The immediate effect on the accounting equation is:
A. Assets increase and liabilities increase
B. One asset increases and another decreases, so total assets and the equation are unchanged
C. Assets increase and equity increases
D. Expenses increase
Reasoning: Equipment is acquired and cash is reduced by the same amount, so the composition of
assets changes and the total does not. No liability or equity is affected. The purchase is not an
expense either, since the equipment is an asset consumed over time through depreciation rather
than in the period of purchase. Answer: B.

## The Three Statements

Three statements together describe a firm, and each answers a different question. The income
statement reports performance over a period — revenue earned and expenses incurred, giving
profit. The balance sheet reports position at a moment — what is owned and owed. The cash flow
statement reports movement of cash over the period, reconciling the accrual-based profit to the
change in cash.

The statements articulate with each other, and the connections are what make them a system.
Net income from the income statement flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet. The cash
flow statement begins with net income and adjusts for non-cash items and working capital changes
to arrive at cash flow, and its ending cash balance appears on the balance sheet. A set of
statements that does not articulate contains an error.

The cash flow statement is the one most often neglected and most informative, because profit is
an accounting construct while cash is a fact. A firm can report growing profit while consuming
cash, which occurs routinely when growth requires investment in receivables and inventory faster
than it generates cash, and it is the mechanism by which profitable firms fail. The persistent
divergence between net income and operating cash flow is the single most useful red flag in
financial statement analysis.

The three statements:
  income statement     performance over a period; revenue less expenses
  gross profit         revenue less cost of goods sold

  EBITDA               before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization; a cash

  current vs non-current  within a year or beyond; the basis of liquidity analysis

EBITDA deserves specific caution because it is presented as a cash proxy and systematically
overstates one for capital-intensive businesses. Excluding depreciation removes the cost of the
assets the business consumes, which is a real cost that must eventually be paid in capital
expenditure. For a business with substantial fixed assets, EBITDA can be healthy while free cash
flow is negative indefinitely, because the depreciation excluded is a genuine economic cost being
incurred. It is a useful measure for comparing operating performance across firms with different
capital structures and it is not a measure of cash generation.

## Accrual Accounting and Revenue Recognition

Accrual accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred rather than when cash
moves, which produces a more meaningful measure of a period's performance. A firm delivering a
service in December and collecting in February earned the revenue in December, and cash accounting
would attribute it to February, distorting both periods.

The matching principle is the corresponding rule for expenses: costs are recognized in the period
they contribute to producing revenue rather than when paid. Cost of goods sold is recognized when
the goods are sold rather than when purchased. A machine's cost is spread over its useful life
through depreciation rather than expensed on purchase. Prepaid insurance is an asset consumed over
the coverage period.

Revenue recognition is where the greatest judgment lies and where the largest accounting failures
have concentrated. The modern framework identifies the contract, the distinct performance
obligations within it, the transaction price, its allocation among the obligations, and
recognition as each is satisfied. This structure matters most for arrangements bundling several
deliverables over time — a software licence with support and future updates, a construction
contract, a subscription with an upfront fee — where the timing of recognition is genuinely
uncertain and where aggressive treatment has repeatedly been the mechanism of misstatement.

Deferred revenue is worth understanding because it appears as a liability and is a favourable
indicator. A subscription business collecting a year in advance records the cash as an asset and
an equal liability representing the obligation to deliver the service, releasing it to revenue
monthly. Growing deferred revenue therefore means growing committed future revenue and cash
already collected, which is why it is watched closely as a forward indicator in subscription
businesses — a case where reading the balance sheet line by its label rather than by its meaning
gives exactly the wrong impression.

## Cost Accounting and Budgeting

Cost accounting attributes costs to products, services, and activities so that pricing,
profitability, and operational decisions can be informed. The primary distinction is between
costs that vary with volume and costs that do not, because that determines how profit responds to
changes in activity.

Contribution margin is revenue less variable costs, and it is the amount each additional unit
contributes toward covering fixed costs and then to profit. This gives the break-even point
directly — fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit — and it is the correct basis for
short-run decisions such as whether to accept an additional order at a reduced price. If the price
exceeds the variable cost, the order contributes to fixed costs that are incurred regardless, so
it improves profit even at a price below full cost.

Allocating fixed and overhead costs is where cost accounting becomes contestable. Traditional
allocation spreads overhead by a single driver such as labour hours or machine time, which
distorts substantially when products consume overhead differently — a low-volume complex product
consumes far more setup, engineering, and handling per unit than a high-volume simple one, and
volume-based allocation charges them proportionally to volume. Activity-based costing traces
overhead through the activities that cause it, which is more accurate and more expensive to
operate.

Budgeting serves planning, coordination, and control, and the tension is that these three
purposes conflict. A budget used for planning should be a realistic expectation. A budget used to
motivate should be challenging. A budget used to evaluate performance creates an incentive to
negotiate a low target and to spend the full allocation regardless of need — the use-it-or-lose-it
behaviour that annual budgets reliably produce. Rolling forecasts updated periodically, separated
from the target-setting used for evaluation, address the worst of this and require the
organization to stop treating the budget as a single document serving every purpose.

## Controls, Audit, and Analysis

Internal controls are the processes ensuring that transactions are authorized, recorded
accurately, and that assets are safeguarded. The foundational control is segregation of duties:
the person authorizing a transaction, the person executing it, the person recording it, and the
person holding the asset should not be the same, because concentrating those functions permits a
single individual to both commit and conceal a misappropriation.

Small organizations frequently cannot achieve full segregation and should compensate rather than
ignore it. Compensating controls include owner review of bank statements received directly rather
than through the person who reconciles them, mandatory holidays during which another person
performs the role, dual authorization above a threshold, and periodic independent review. The
common thread is that someone other than the person performing the function looks at the result.

Audit provides independent assurance and its limits are frequently misunderstood. An audit
provides reasonable rather than absolute assurance, is based on sampling rather than examination
of every transaction, is designed to detect material misstatement rather than all error, and
addresses the financial statements rather than the business's prospects. An unqualified opinion
means the statements are fairly stated in all material respects, and it is not a statement that
the business is well run or that fraud is absent.

The cash conversion cycle deserves the closing emphasis because it is the operational quantity
that determines how much cash growth consumes. It measures how long money is tied up between
paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and a business with a short or negative cycle
generates cash as it grows while one with a long cycle consumes it. Retailers collecting at the
point of sale and paying suppliers on terms have negative cycles and are funded by their growth;
manufacturers holding inventory and extending credit have long cycles and must fund it. Improving
any of the three components releases cash permanently and without any change to profitability,
which makes it the highest-return financial operations work available in most businesses and one
that receives far less attention than margin improvement.
