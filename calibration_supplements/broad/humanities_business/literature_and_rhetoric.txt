## Close Reading and Interpretation

Close reading attends to the specific choices a text makes rather than to its summarizable
content, on the premise that meaning resides in how something is said as much as in what is
said. The practice is to notice what is there — a repeated word, an abrupt shift in register, a
sentence whose length breaks a pattern, an image that returns — and to ask what work it is
doing, before deciding what the text means.

The most useful discipline is separating observation from interpretation. Observation is what
can be pointed to: this passage uses nine consecutive monosyllables, this narrator refers to
the protagonist by surname until chapter twelve and by first name thereafter, this poem's rhyme
scheme breaks in its final couplet. Interpretation is the argument about significance, and it
is only as strong as the observations supporting it. Criticism that begins with a thesis and
gathers illustrations tends to find what it sought; criticism that begins with anomalies and
asks what would explain them tends to discover things.

Ambiguity in literature is frequently constitutive rather than a defect to be resolved. A text
that sustains two incompatible readings, both fully supported, may be doing so deliberately,
and flattening it to one reading discards the effect. The productive question in such cases is
not which reading is correct but what the text achieves by permitting both — which is a
different and usually more interesting question.

Close reading vocabulary:
  diction             word choice; register, connotation, etymology, precision
  syntax              sentence construction; length, subordination, inversion,
                      fragments
  imagery             sensory detail; note which senses and how concentrated
  figurative language  metaphor, simile, metonymy, synecdoche, personification
  tone                the attitude toward subject and audience
  register            the level of formality; shifts are always significant
  point of view       first, second, third limited, third omniscient; and how
                      reliable
  free indirect style  third person that adopts a character's idiom and
                      perception without quotation; the dominant technique of the
                      modern novel
  irony               verbal, situational, dramatic; a gap between statement and
                      meaning, or expectation and outcome
  motif               a recurring element; repetition is the signal
  structure           the arrangement of parts; what is withheld and when
  intertextuality     reference to and dependence on other texts

Free indirect style deserves particular attention because it is so pervasive and so easy to
miss. The narration is third person, and the language, judgments, and blind spots belong to the
character rather than to the narrator, without any quotation marks or attributive tag to mark
the transition. This lets a text simultaneously present a character's view and permit the reader
to see past it, which is how irony operates in most nineteenth and twentieth century fiction.
Recognizing when the prose has slipped into a character's idiom is the single most useful
reading skill for the novel.

Q: A third-person narrative describes a wedding as "the most brilliant match anyone could have
hoped for," with no quotation marks and no attribution. This most likely indicates:
A. The narrator's own judgment
B. Free indirect style, presenting a character's or a community's view in language the narrator
   does not endorse
C. A factual statement about the marriage
D. An error in punctuation
Reasoning: The evaluative superlative and the vague "anyone" belong to a social idiom rather
than to a narrating voice establishing facts. This is the signature of free indirect style, and
it typically sets up an ironic gap between what the community believes and what the narrative
will show. Answer: B.

## Narrative Craft

Narrative organizes events into a form that produces meaning, and the distinction between
story and plot is where analysis begins. Story is the events in chronological order; plot is
the arrangement in which they are presented, including what is withheld, reordered, compressed,
or expanded. A detective novel's story begins with the murder and its plot begins with the
discovery of the body, and nearly all of the genre's effect comes from that difference.

Character is revealed through action, speech, thought, and the response of others, and the
craft principle that follows is to dramatize rather than to assert. Telling the reader that a
character is cruel establishes it weakly; showing an act of cruelty establishes it and lets the
reader reach the judgment themselves, which makes it theirs. The corresponding caution is that
the injunction to show rather than tell is a heuristic rather than a rule — summary is what
allows a narrative to cover years in a paragraph, and a story that dramatizes everything is
interminable.

Point of view determines what can be known and is therefore the most consequential structural
decision. First person gives immediacy and voice and confines the narrative to what one person
can perceive and is willing to say. Third limited gives similar confinement with more
flexibility of distance. Third omniscient permits access to any mind and to information no
character has, which is powerful and diffuses intimacy. Each restricts as much as it enables,
and the restriction is usually where the interest lies.

Narrative elements:
  story and plot       chronology versus arrangement
  exposition           necessary background; the craft problem is delivering it
                       without stopping the narrative
  in medias res        beginning in the middle; the standard solution to exposition
  scene and summary    dramatized moment versus compressed passage; alternating
                       them controls pace
  conflict             external, internal, or against circumstance; without it,
                       events are a sequence rather than a story
  stakes               what is risked; the reader's investment is proportional
  causality            events connected by consequence rather than sequence; the
                       difference between plot and chronicle
  reversal and recognition  the classical mechanisms of dramatic turn
  unreliable narrator  the reader must read past the narration; requires evidence
                       within the text of the discrepancy
  foreshadowing        preparing a later development so it satisfies rather than
                       surprises arbitrarily
  Chekhov's gun        prominent elements create expectations; unfired guns are
                       felt as broken promises
  frame narrative      a story within a story; establishes distance and raises
                       questions about transmission

The distinction between chronicle and plot repays attention because it explains why some
narratives feel inert despite eventful content. A chronicle is a sequence of things that
happened; a plot is a sequence in which each event follows from the preceding one. The
difference is causality, and it is what makes a narrative feel like it is going somewhere. A
draft in which scenes could be reordered without loss is usually a chronicle, and the revision
is to establish consequence between them.

## Poetics and Form

Poetry organizes language by additional constraints beyond those of prose, and the constraints
are the source of the effects rather than obstacles to them. Metre imposes a pattern of stressed
and unstressed syllables; rhyme imposes sound correspondence at line ends; form imposes overall
structure. What makes these productive is the tension between the pattern and its variations —
a perfectly regular metre becomes monotonous, and the meaning frequently lives in the departure.

The line is poetry's distinguishing unit, and where a line ends is a decision with effects.
An end-stopped line completes a syntactic unit and produces closure. An enjambed line breaks
mid-phrase, creating a momentary reading that the next line revises, which produces suspension
and can generate a double meaning as the eye moves. The caesura, a pause within a line, works
similarly at smaller scale.

Sound patterning operates below conscious attention and is where much of poetry's effect
resides. Alliteration, assonance, and consonance create cohesion and emphasis. Vowel length and
consonant hardness affect pace — long vowels and liquid consonants slow a line, short vowels and
plosives quicken it. These effects are real and are frequently over-interpreted, since the
claim that a particular sound intrinsically conveys a particular meaning does not survive
cross-linguistic examination; what sound does reliably is control pace, emphasis, and cohesion.

Poetic elements:
  metre                iambic, trochaic, anapestic, dactylic; the foot pattern
  iambic pentameter    five iambs; the dominant English line, close to the rhythm
                       of speech
  substitution         a varied foot within a pattern; where the emphasis falls
  enjambment           the line breaks mid-syntax; suspension and double reading
  caesura              a pause within the line
  free verse           no regular metre; rhythm and line breaks still deliberate
  rhyme scheme         the pattern of end sounds
  slant rhyme          approximate; less closure, more unease
  sonnet               fourteen lines; the Petrarchan turns at line nine, the
                       Shakespearean at the final couplet
  villanelle           nineteen lines with two refrains; obsessive recurrence
  ghazal               autonomous couplets with a repeated refrain
  haiku                brevity, image, seasonal reference, juxtaposition
  blank verse          unrhymed iambic pentameter; the metre of English drama
  concrete             visual arrangement carries meaning

Form and content relate in a way worth stating carefully. A form does not have an intrinsic
meaning, and a form does establish expectations that a poem can satisfy or violate to effect.
The villanelle's compulsory returning refrains suit obsession, grief, and inevitability, which
is why the best-known examples treat those subjects — not because the form means them but
because its structural insistence enacts them. Reading a poem's form as a claim about content
is a mistake; reading it as a set of expectations the poem is working with is not.

## Critical Approaches and Rhetoric

Critical approaches ask different questions of the same text and are complementary rather than
competing. Formalist criticism attends to the text's internal workings and treats external
context as secondary. Historicist criticism situates the text in its moment and reads it as
participating in the concerns of that moment. Psychoanalytic criticism reads for unconscious
structures. Marxist criticism attends to class, labour, and material conditions. Feminist and
gender criticism attends to how gender is constructed and represented. Postcolonial criticism
attends to empire and its aftermath. Reader-response criticism locates meaning in the encounter
between text and reader rather than in the text alone.

Each is a lens that reveals some features and occludes others, and the practical skill is
knowing which question a given approach is equipped to answer. Asking a formalist analysis to
explain a text's political effects, or a historicist one to account for why a sentence is
beautiful, produces poor results not because the approaches are wrong but because they were
built for other questions.

Rhetoric is the study of persuasion and predates literary criticism by millennia. The classical
appeals are ethos, the speaker's credibility; pathos, emotional engagement; and logos, logical
argument. Effective persuasion generally uses all three, and the balance is determined by
audience and occasion rather than by any hierarchy among them. The common assumption that
logos is legitimate and pathos manipulative does not survive examination — an argument about
suffering that produces no emotional response has failed to communicate its subject.

Critical and rhetorical concepts:
  formalist            the text's internal workings
  historicist          the text within its historical moment
  psychoanalytic       unconscious structures and desire
  Marxist              class, labour, material conditions
  feminist / gender    the construction and representation of gender
  postcolonial         empire, hybridity, the position of the speaking subject
  reader-response      meaning in the encounter rather than in the text alone
  ethos                credibility of the speaker
  pathos               emotional engagement
  logos                logical argument
  kairos               the opportune moment; timing as a rhetorical resource
  audience             every rhetorical choice depends on it
  arrangement          the order of an argument; the classical parts
  concession           granting a point; increases credibility and disarms
                       objection
  rhetorical question  asserts by implying the answer
  anaphora             repetition at the start of successive clauses; builds
                       cumulative force

Logical fallacies are worth knowing as tools of analysis and are frequently over-applied. Ad
hominem is fallacious when it substitutes for engaging an argument, and it is legitimate when
credibility is genuinely at issue, as in evaluating testimony. An appeal to authority is
fallacious when the authority is irrelevant and reasonable when the authority is a
well-functioning expert consensus in the relevant domain. A slippery slope is fallacious when
the chain is asserted and legitimate when the mechanism connecting the steps is argued. Naming
a fallacy is not itself a refutation, and treating it as one is its own error.

## Style, Clarity, and Practice

Clear prose is achieved through a small number of transferable techniques, and the largest
single gain comes from putting the actor and the action into the subject and verb. Prose that
buries the action in an abstract noun and connects it with a weak verb requires the reader to
reconstruct who is doing what: compare a sentence stating that the implementation of the policy
resulted in a reduction in errors with one stating that the policy reduced errors. The second
is shorter, clearer, and identifies the agent.

Sentence variety controls pace and emphasis. A run of similar-length sentences becomes
monotonous regardless of their individual quality, and a short sentence following several long
ones lands with force. Placing the most important element at the end of a sentence gives it
emphasis, since the end is the position of natural stress, and this is why the periodic
sentence — withholding the main clause until the end — creates suspension.

Cohesion is what makes a paragraph read as connected rather than as a list. The most reliable
technique is beginning sentences with information the reader already has and ending with what
is new, so that each sentence's end becomes the next one's beginning. This produces flow
without any explicit connectives, and its absence is why prose composed of individually clear
sentences can nonetheless be hard to follow.

Style and practice:
  actor and action     put them in the subject and verb
  avoid nominalization  a verb turned into a noun hides the action
  active voice default  passive is correct when the agent is unknown, irrelevant,
                       or deliberately obscured; it is not an error
  concrete over abstract  specific detail is more persuasive and more memorable
  vary sentence length  monotony is a failure regardless of individual quality
  end weight           the most important element goes last
  old before new       begin with the known, end with the new; this creates flow
  cut qualifiers       "very", "quite", "rather" usually weaken rather than modify
  prefer the short word  when it does the same work
  parallel structure   items in a series should share grammatical form
  read aloud           catches rhythm problems, awkward constructions, and
                       accidental repetition that silent reading misses
  revision             the substance of writing; a first draft is material rather
                       than a product
  audience             every choice about diction, structure, and evidence follows
                       from who is reading and why

The advice against passive voice is worth stating precisely because it is so often given as an
absolute. The passive is the correct construction when the agent is unknown, when the agent is
irrelevant, when the object is the topic under discussion, or when the sequence of information
requires the object first. Scientific writing uses it appropriately to keep the focus on the
procedure rather than the experimenter. Its misuse is as a device for concealing agency —
stating that mistakes were made rather than naming who made them — and that is a rhetorical
choice rather than a grammatical error, which is exactly why it deserves attention.

Revision is where writing happens, and the useful discipline is separating its kinds.
Structural revision asks whether the argument or narrative is in the right order and whether
anything is missing or unnecessary, and it may require discarding a great deal. Paragraph-level
revision asks whether each paragraph does one thing and connects to its neighbours.
Sentence-level revision addresses clarity and rhythm. Proofreading catches errors. Attempting
these simultaneously is inefficient, because polishing sentences in a section that structural
revision will delete is wasted effort, and the order in which they are performed should follow
the order in which their conclusions constrain each other.
