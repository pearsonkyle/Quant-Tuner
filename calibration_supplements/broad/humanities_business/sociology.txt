## Theoretical Perspectives

Sociology studies social life at the level of groups, institutions, and societies rather than
individuals, and its central claim is that social facts — patterns of behaviour, rates, and
structures — require explanation at the social level rather than reducing to individual
psychology. Durkheim's study of suicide made the case by showing that suicide rates are stable
within societies and differ systematically between them, so whatever explains the rate is
social even though every individual case has a personal story.

Three broad perspectives organize the field, and they are complementary lenses rather than
competing theories. Structural functionalism examines how institutions contribute to social
stability, asking what function a practice serves for the system. Conflict theory examines how
groups with unequal power compete over resources, asking whose interests a practice serves.
Symbolic interactionism examines how meaning is constructed through interaction, asking how
people interpret and negotiate situations. The first two operate at the macro level and the third
at the micro.

Each perspective illuminates what the others obscure. Functionalism explains persistence and
integration and tends toward treating existing arrangements as necessary. Conflict theory
explains change and inequality and can reduce every arrangement to interest. Interactionism
explains how people experience and construct their situations and does not readily scale to
institutional analysis. Applying one exclusively produces a systematically partial account.

Theoretical concepts:
  social fact          patterns existing above the individual; rates, norms,
                       institutions
  structural functionalism  what function does this serve for the system
  conflict theory      whose interests does this serve
  symbolic interactionism  how is meaning constructed in interaction
  macro and micro      levels of analysis; both are required and neither reduces
                       to the other
  agency and structure  individuals act, and structures constrain and enable;
                       accounts privileging either alone are incomplete
  structuration        structures are reproduced by the actions they shape; the
                       standard attempt to integrate agency and structure
  manifest function    the intended and recognized purpose
  latent function      the unintended and unrecognized consequence
  social construction  categories treated as natural are frequently produced and
                       maintained socially; the claim concerns the category rather
                       than the underlying phenomenon
  reflexivity          social knowledge changes the society it describes, which
                       has no equivalent in the natural sciences

The distinction between manifest and latent functions is worth having because it explains why
practices persist that appear to fail at their stated purpose. A ritual whose manifest function
is to bring rain has a latent function of assembling the community at a time when coordination
matters, and the latter sustains it regardless of the former. An organizational process whose
manifest purpose is quality control may have a latent function of distributing accountability,
which is why removing it on the grounds that it does not improve quality frequently meets
resistance that the stated rationale does not explain.

Q: A study finds that a job training programme does not improve employment outcomes, yet it
persists and is well supported. A sociological explanation would first consider:
A. That the study is wrong
B. What latent functions the programme serves — legitimating institutions, distributing
   resources, signalling commitment — beyond its manifest purpose
C. That participants are unmotivated
D. That funding should be cut
Reasoning: Practices that fail at their stated purpose and persist are typically serving other
functions, whether recognized or not. Identifying what they are is the analytical task, and it
does not settle whether the programme should continue — that requires evaluating those functions
explicitly. Answer: B.

## Methods

Sociological methods divide by whether they aim to measure and generalize or to interpret and
understand, and the division is one of purpose rather than of rigour. Quantitative methods use
surveys, administrative records, and experiments to measure variables across many cases and
estimate relationships, trading depth for breadth and generalizability. Qualitative methods use
interviews, ethnography, and document analysis to understand meaning and process in fewer cases,
trading generalizability for depth and for the ability to discover what was not anticipated.

Sampling determines what a finding generalizes to, and it is where quantitative work most often
fails. A probability sample permits inference to the population; a convenience sample does not,
regardless of its size. Non-response is the pervasive practical problem, since those who decline
to participate differ systematically from those who agree, and response rates in general
population surveys have fallen dramatically. Weighting adjusts for known differences and cannot
correct for unmeasured ones.

Measurement is the other recurring difficulty, because sociological concepts are frequently
abstract and their operationalization is contestable. Social class, religiosity, trust,
discrimination, and wellbeing each have several defensible measures that produce different
results, and the choice is a substantive claim rather than a technical detail. Reporting which
measure was used and how sensitive the conclusion is to that choice is what distinguishes careful
work.

Methods and their limits:
  survey               breadth and generalizability; depends on sampling and
                       response
  probability sample   permits inference to the population
  convenience sample   does not, regardless of size
  non-response bias    those who decline differ; the dominant survey problem today
  panel study          the same people over time; separates change from cohort
                       differences, and loses people through attrition
  ethnography          extended immersion; depth, discovery of the unanticipated
  interviews           accounts and meaning; what people say is data about what
                       they say, which is not the same as data about what they do
  content analysis     systematic coding of texts or media
  administrative data  large, cheap, and shaped by the administrative process that
                       produced it
  natural experiment   an as-if random assignment; the strongest observational
                       design
  operationalization   how an abstract concept is measured; a substantive choice
  reliability          consistency of measurement
  validity             whether it measures the intended construct
  ecological fallacy   inferring individual relationships from group-level data;
                       the relationship can reverse
  reflexivity          the researcher's position affects access, what is said, and
                       interpretation; acknowledging it is standard practice

The ecological fallacy deserves emphasis because it is a specific and common inferential error.
A correlation observed between group-level averages need not hold at the individual level and can
reverse. Regions with higher average income may vote for one party while higher-income
individuals within every region vote for another, which is not a paradox — the group-level and
individual-level relationships are different quantities and there is no general reason they
should agree. Drawing individual conclusions from aggregate data requires either individual data
or an argument that the aggregation does not distort.

## Stratification and Inequality

Stratification is the systematic and durable ranking of groups by access to resources,
opportunity, and status. It is universal across complex societies while its basis and magnitude
vary enormously, and the questions are what dimensions organize it, how it is transmitted across
generations, and how it is legitimated.

Class, status, and power are the three dimensions Weber distinguished and they do not coincide.
Class concerns economic position — ownership, occupation, income. Status concerns social honour
and lifestyle. Power concerns the ability to realize one's will despite resistance. A
professional may have high status and modest income, a business owner high income and low status,
and the divergences are analytically productive rather than complications.

Social mobility measures movement between positions, and the distinction between absolute and
relative mobility is where public discussion is most confused. Absolute mobility concerns whether
people do better than their parents, which depends on economic growth and structural change —
when the occupational structure shifts toward higher-paying work, many people move up without any
change in the relative advantage of origins. Relative mobility concerns whether origins predict
destinations, holding the structure constant, and it is the measure of how open a society is.
Absolute mobility can rise while relative mobility is unchanged, which is what occurred in many
economies during postwar expansion.

Stratification concepts:
  class                economic position; ownership, occupation, income
  status               social honour and lifestyle
  power                ability to realize one's will despite resistance
  cultural capital     familiarity with valued cultural forms; transmitted in
                       families and rewarded by institutions
  social capital       resources accessible through networks
  human capital        skills and credentials
  absolute mobility    doing better than one's parents; driven by growth and
                       structural change
  relative mobility    whether origins predict destinations; the measure of
                       openness
  intergenerational elasticity  the correlation between parent and child outcomes;
                       varies substantially between countries
  Great Gatsby curve   higher inequality is associated with lower relative
                       mobility across countries
  intersectionality    disadvantages combine non-additively; the experience at an
                       intersection is not the sum of its parts
  meritocracy          the claim that positions reflect ability and effort; a
                       normative ideal, an empirical claim, and a legitimating
                       ideology, and distinguishing which is in play matters
  homophily            association with similar others; reproduces stratification
                       through networks without any intentional exclusion

Homophily deserves attention because it produces stratified outcomes through mechanisms that
require no discriminatory intent. People associate disproportionately with others similar to
themselves in class, education, ethnicity, and occupation, and because networks convey
information about opportunities, the opportunities circulate within groups. Hiring through
referrals therefore reproduces the existing composition of an organization even when every
individual decision is made in good faith, which is why the aggregate outcome can be highly
unequal while no participant did anything they would recognize as exclusion.

## Networks, Institutions, and Culture

Social network analysis treats relationships as the unit of analysis, and its findings have been
among the field's most transferable. The strength of weak ties is the best known: close contacts
tend to know the same people and the same information one already has, while acquaintances bridge
to different circles and therefore carry novel information. Job opportunities are found
disproportionately through weak ties for this reason, which inverts the intuition that strong
relationships are more useful.

Structural holes are the gaps between otherwise disconnected groups, and occupying one confers
advantage — access to non-redundant information and control over what flows between the groups.
Brokerage is measured by betweenness centrality, and the finding that brokers accrue advantage
recurs in organizations, in markets, and in politics.

Institutions are durable patterns of rules, norms, and practices structuring behaviour, and they
persist beyond the individuals occupying them. Institutional isomorphism is the observed
tendency of organizations in a field to resemble each other, arising through coercion where
regulation requires it, through mimicry where uncertainty makes imitating a successful peer
attractive, and through professional norms where similarly trained people bring the same
practices. The result is that organizational forms spread through a field for reasons of
legitimacy rather than efficiency, which explains why practices with no demonstrated benefit
diffuse widely.

Networks, institutions, and culture:
  strength of weak ties  acquaintances bridge to novel information
  structural hole      a gap between groups; brokerage confers advantage
  betweenness centrality  how much a node lies on paths between others
  homophily            association with similar others
  small world          high clustering with short paths
  diffusion            innovations spread through networks; adoption follows an
                       S-curve with identifiable early and late adopters
  institution          durable rules, norms, and practices
  institutional isomorphism  coercive, mimetic, normative; organizations converge
                       for legitimacy rather than efficiency
  path dependence      early contingent choices constrain later ones
  norms                unwritten rules; enforced by informal sanction and often
                       more binding than formal rules
  values               abstract conceptions of the desirable
  socialization        acquiring the norms and roles of a group; primary in
                       childhood, and continuing throughout life
  role                 expected behaviour attached to a position
  role conflict        incompatible expectations from different roles held
                       simultaneously
  subculture           a group with distinctive norms within a larger society
  cultural lag         material change outpacing normative adjustment

Pluralistic ignorance is worth naming because it explains a specific and consequential
pattern. It occurs when most members of a group privately reject a norm while believing that most
others accept it, so each conforms publicly and the appearance of consensus is sustained by
people who do not hold it. It explains why norms can collapse suddenly once the private
distribution becomes visible, why anonymous surveys sometimes reveal majorities that no one
believed existed, and why the first person to speak against a norm has a disproportionate effect.

## Deviance, Demography, and Change

Deviance is behaviour violating norms, and the sociological approach treats it as relational
rather than intrinsic: what counts as deviant depends on the norms of the group and the
context, so the analytical question is how definitions are established and applied rather than
why individuals violate them. Labelling theory adds that being identified as deviant has
consequences — restricted opportunities, altered self-conception, association with others
similarly labelled — that can produce further deviance, so the label participates in creating
what it describes.

Strain theory explains deviance as arising from the gap between culturally promoted goals and
the legitimate means available to achieve them. Where success is defined by material attainment
and legitimate routes are blocked, some pursue illegitimate ones, some abandon the goals, and
some reject both. Its strength is explaining why deviance concentrates where opportunity is
constrained rather than being distributed randomly.

Demography studies population size and composition, and its dynamics are among the most
predictable in social science because they follow from birth, death, and migration rates with
long lags. The demographic transition describes the shift from high birth and death rates to low
ones, occurring in stages that produce a period of rapid growth when death rates fall before
birth rates do — which is what drove twentieth-century population growth and which most of the
world has now passed through.

Deviance, demography, and change:
  deviance             relational; defined by norms and context
  labelling theory     the label alters opportunities and self-conception
  strain theory        the gap between goals and legitimate means
  social control       formal and informal mechanisms enforcing conformity
  moral panic          disproportionate reaction to a perceived threat; media
                       amplification is characteristic
  demographic transition  high to low birth and death rates, with a growth phase
                       between
  total fertility rate  births per woman; replacement is about 2.1 accounting for
                       mortality
  population pyramid   age and sex structure; predicts dependency ratios decades
                       ahead
  dependency ratio     non-working to working-age population; the mechanism behind
                       pension and healthcare pressure
  ageing populations   the consequence of sustained low fertility and rising
                       longevity; largely determined by births that have already
                       occurred
  migration            push and pull factors; the least predictable component
  modernization        the contested claim that societies follow a common
                       development path
  globalization        increasing interconnection; effects are uneven and
                       contested rather than uniform
  social movements     collective action for change; resource mobilization and
                       framing are the standard analytical tools

Demographic momentum deserves the closing emphasis because it makes population projections
unusually reliable and unusually counterintuitive. A population with a young age structure
continues growing for decades after fertility falls to replacement, because a large cohort of
young people is still entering reproductive age. Conversely a population that has aged continues
shrinking after fertility recovers. The consequence is that many population outcomes decades
ahead are already substantially determined by births that have occurred, which makes them among
the most confidently projectable social facts — and it means that policies intended to change
population trajectories operate with lags of a generation or more, a timescale that political
cycles do not accommodate.
