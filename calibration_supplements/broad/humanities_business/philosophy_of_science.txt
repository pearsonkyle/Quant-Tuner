## Knowledge, Justification, and the Problem of Induction

The classical analysis defines knowledge as justified true belief, and Gettier's short 1963
paper showed the analysis is insufficient with counterexamples in which all three conditions
hold and the belief is nonetheless not knowledge — because the justification connects to the
truth accidentally. A person who believes it is three o'clock from a stopped clock that
happens to read the correct time has a justified true belief and does not know the time.
Attempts to repair the definition by adding a fourth condition have proliferated without
consensus, which is itself informative: the concept may not admit a clean set of necessary and
sufficient conditions.

The problem of induction, sharpened by Hume, is more consequential for science. Inductive
inference generalizes from observed cases to unobserved ones, and no deductive argument
justifies it: that the sun has risen every day does not entail it will rise tomorrow. The
attempt to justify induction by its past success is circular, since that argument is itself
inductive. Hume's conclusion was that induction is a habit of mind rather than a rational
inference, and no subsequent proposal has achieved general acceptance as a solution.

Goodman's new riddle of induction sharpens the problem further. Define a predicate that
applies to things examined before a future time and found green, or not examined and blue.
All observed emeralds satisfy both "green" and this artificial predicate equally well, so
the evidence supports both generalizations, which make contradictory predictions about future
observations. The problem is not that we lack evidence but that any body of evidence supports
indefinitely many incompatible generalizations, and something beyond the evidence must
determine which we project.

  Goodman's riddle        evidence underdetermines which predicate to project

The Duhem-Quine thesis has direct methodological consequences. A prediction derives from a
hypothesis together with auxiliary assumptions — about the instruments, the background
conditions, the absence of interference, the correctness of the mathematics. When the
prediction fails, logic tells you that something in the conjunction is wrong and does not tell
you what. Historically this has cut both ways: anomalies in Uranus's orbit were resolved by
positing a new planet, which was found, while identical reasoning applied to Mercury's
perihelion posited a planet that does not exist and the resolution came from replacing
Newtonian gravity. The same move was scientifically respectable in one case and mistaken in
the other, and no rule distinguishes them in advance.

Q: An experiment contradicts a well-established theory's prediction. Which conclusion is
licensed by logic alone?
A. The theory is false
B. At least one element of the conjunction of theory, auxiliary assumptions, and experimental
   conditions is false
C. The experiment was flawed
D. The theory needs modification
Reasoning: The prediction follows from the theory together with auxiliaries about
instrumentation, background conditions, and the absence of confounds. A failed prediction
falsifies the conjunction, and logic does not identify which conjunct to reject. This is the
Duhem-Quine thesis, and it is why a single anomalous result rarely overturns a theory in
practice. Answer: B.

## Demarcation and Scientific Method

The demarcation problem asks what distinguishes science from non-science. Popper's answer was
falsifiability: a scientific theory forbids something, and a theory compatible with every
possible observation says nothing. This has real diagnostic value — it identifies why an
unfalsifiable claim, however plausible, is not doing scientific work — and it does not
survive as a sharp criterion, for the reason Duhem and Quine identified: no theory is
falsifiable in isolation, since any prediction can be preserved by adjusting auxiliaries, and
scientists routinely and correctly do exactly that.

Lakatos proposed research programmes as the unit of appraisal rather than individual
theories. A programme has a hard core protected by convention and a protective belt of
auxiliary hypotheses that absorb anomalies. What distinguishes a progressive programme from a
degenerating one is whether its modifications generate novel predictions that are subsequently
confirmed, or merely accommodate what is already known. This preserves Popper's insight that
content and risk matter while accounting for the fact that theories are defended rather than
abandoned at the first anomaly.

The image of a single scientific method proceeding from observation through hypothesis to
experiment to conclusion is a textbook simplification that describes little actual practice.
Real inquiry involves theory-driven observation, hypotheses generated by analogy and
mathematical structure as often as by data, extended periods of model refinement, and results
that are negotiated through peer review and replication. That there is no algorithm does not
mean there is no method — the practices of controlled comparison, quantification, prediction,
replication, and open criticism are genuinely distinctive and genuinely effective.

Demarcation and method:
  falsifiability        a theory must forbid something; useful heuristic, not a
                        sharp criterion
  ad hoc modification   a change that saves a theory without adding testable
                        content; the real target of Popper's objection
  research programme    hard core plus protective belt; progressive if it predicts
                        novel facts, degenerating if it only accommodates
  novel prediction      predicting something not used in constructing the theory;
                        the strongest form of confirmation
  severe testing        a test the hypothesis would probably have failed if false
  replication           independent reproduction; the practical core of reliability
  peer review           a filter, not a guarantee; catches some errors and misses
                        many
  consilience           independent lines converging; the strongest evidence
                        available in practice
  parsimony             prefer fewer assumptions; a heuristic with no guarantee
                        that nature is simple
  pseudoscience markers unfalsifiable claims, immunity to disconfirming evidence,
                        reliance on anecdote, absence of a mechanism, appeal to
                        conspiracy to explain rejection

Consilience deserves emphasis because it is what actually produces confidence in mature
science and is underrepresented in philosophical accounts focused on single tests. The age of
the Earth is established by radiometric dating of multiple isotope systems, by stellar
physics, by sedimentation rates, by the sea's salinity budget, and by the cosmic distance
ladder — methods with entirely different assumptions, instrumentation, and failure modes, all
converging. The improbability of independent methods agreeing by coincidence is what makes the
conclusion secure, and it is a far stronger argument than the falsification of any single
alternative.

## Theory Change and Explanation

Kuhn's account of scientific change distinguished normal science, in which a paradigm
supplies the problems and the standards for solving them, from revolutionary science, in
which anomalies accumulate until the framework itself is replaced. His claim that competing
paradigms are incommensurable — that they differ in the meanings of their terms and in what
counts as a legitimate problem — was widely read as implying that theory choice is irrational
and that later theories are not closer to truth.

That reading was stronger than Kuhn's considered position and stronger than the historical
record supports. Successive theories in mature sciences typically preserve the empirical
successes of their predecessors as limiting cases: Newtonian mechanics is recovered from
relativity at low velocity and from quantum mechanics at large scale. That structure of
retention is difficult to explain if the theories are merely different rather than
successively better. Laudan's pessimistic induction pushes the other way, observing that
many past theories were empirically successful and are now considered false, so current
success is weak evidence for truth.

Explanation is a distinct question from prediction, and theories can do one well and the
other badly. The deductive-nomological model treats explanation as deduction from laws plus
initial conditions, which captures a great deal and has known counterexamples: the height of
a flagpole and the length of its shadow are mutually derivable, and only one direction is
explanatory, which suggests explanation involves asymmetric dependence that deduction does not
capture. Causal accounts locate explanation in identifying what produced the phenomenon;
unificationist accounts locate it in reducing the number of independent assumptions needed to
account for many phenomena.

Theory change and explanation:

  anomaly                a persistent unsolved problem
  scientific revolution  paradigm replacement
  incommensurability     differences in meaning and standards across paradigms
  correspondence         a new theory recovers the old one in a limiting regime
  pessimistic induction  past successful theories were false, so current ones

  deductive-nomological  explanation as deduction from laws; asymmetry problems
  causal explanation     identifying the producing mechanism
  unification            explaining many phenomena from few assumptions
  mechanism              decomposing a phenomenon into interacting parts; the
                         dominant model of explanation in biology and medicine
  inference to the best  accept the hypothesis that best explains the evidence;
                         requires that the best available be good enough

## Causation, Probability, and Evidence

Causal claims are the ones science most wants to make and the hardest to establish from
observation. Regularity accounts identify causation with constant conjunction and cannot
distinguish causes from correlated effects of a common cause. Counterfactual accounts define a
cause as something whose absence would have prevented the effect, which matches intuition and
requires evaluating claims about non-actual situations. Manipulationist accounts identify a
cause as something that would change the effect if intervened upon, which connects directly
to experimental practice and is the framework underlying modern causal inference.

The formal apparatus of causal inference makes these distinctions operational. Causal
diagrams represent assumed relationships, and rules on those diagrams determine which
statistical adjustments identify a causal effect. The key results are that confounders — common
causes of treatment and outcome — must be adjusted for, that mediators lie on the causal path
and adjusting for them removes the effect being estimated, and that colliders are common
effects whose adjustment induces spurious association. That last case is why controlling for
additional variables is not conservative: adjusting for a collider creates bias where none
existed.

Probability admits interpretations that support different inferential practices. Frequentism
identifies probability with long-run relative frequency, which makes it undefined for
one-off events and grounds procedures with guaranteed error rates over repeated use. Bayesian
interpretation treats probability as degree of belief updated by evidence, which applies to
single events and requires a prior. The practical difference is what a result means: a
confidence interval is a statement about the procedure's long-run behaviour, and a credible
interval is a statement about the parameter given the data — the latter being what people
almost always intend.

Confounding is the first. Coffee drinking correlates with smoking, income, education, and
occupation, and the study adjusted for only some of these. Smoking in particular is a strong
confounder in the opposite direction, so residual confounding by unmeasured smoking intensity
can produce or destroy an apparent effect. Adjusting for the variables you happen to have
measured does not address the ones you did not.

Reverse causation is the second and is easy to miss. People who are already unwell often
reduce or stop coffee — the sick-quitter effect — so the non-drinking group is enriched in
people with pre-existing illness, and their higher mortality is caused by the illness rather
than by the absence of coffee. The standard mitigation is excluding deaths in the first
several years of follow-up and excluding people with baseline disease, and reporting whether
the effect survives.

Healthy user bias is the third: behaviours cluster, and whatever makes someone a moderate
coffee drinker may correlate with other health-promoting behaviours not captured.

What would strengthen the causal claim, roughly in ascending order of strength. A
dose-response gradient consistent across the range is suggestive though also explicable by
graded confounding. Consistency across populations with different confounding structures —
where coffee drinking correlates with smoking in one country and not another — is more
persuasive, because the confounders differ while the association persists. Mendelian
randomization, using genetic variants affecting caffeine metabolism as instruments,
approximates randomization since genotype is assigned at conception and is unlikely to
correlate with lifestyle confounders. And a randomized trial on an intermediate outcome, such
as a biomarker, provides direct experimental evidence for a mechanism even where a mortality
trial is infeasible.

One caution on adjustment: do not adjust for variables that could be on the causal path, such
as blood pressure or inflammatory markers, if the hypothesis is that coffee acts through them.
That removes the very effect being estimated, and adjusting for a common effect of coffee and
mortality would actively create bias.

## Values, Practice, and the Social Structure of Science

The claim that science is value-free does not survive examination, and the useful distinction
is between where values legitimately operate and where they do not. Values are unavoidable in
choosing which questions to pursue, in deciding what counts as an acceptable risk of error, in
selecting how to categorize and measure, and in judging when evidence is sufficient for
action. Values are illegitimate in determining what the evidence says. The argument from
inductive risk observes that any decision to accept or reject a hypothesis carries a risk of
error whose consequences are asymmetric, and weighing those consequences requires value
judgments that cannot be derived from the data.

The social structure of science is part of its epistemology rather than incidental to it.
Objectivity is achieved less by individual detachment than by a community with diverse
perspectives, shared standards for criticism, and mechanisms by which criticism can change
what is believed. Peer review, replication, open data, and adversarial collaboration are the
institutional expression of this, and their failures — publication bias, incentive
misalignment, homogeneous reviewer pools, unreplicable findings — are epistemological problems
rather than merely administrative ones.

The replication crisis across several fields is the most instructive recent case. Its causes
are structural: publication favours positive novel results, analytic flexibility permits
significance to be reached from noise without conscious dishonesty, small samples produce
inflated effect estimates when they do reach significance, and replication attempts were
neither rewarded nor published. The reforms that followed — preregistration, registered
reports where acceptance precedes results, open data and code, larger samples, and the
publication of null results — are corrections to the institutional structure rather than to
individual reasoning, which is consistent with the view that scientific reliability is a
property of communities and their practices.
