## Process Analysis and Flow

Operations management analyses how work flows through a system and where it does not. The
fundamental quantities are throughput — the rate of output — flow time, the duration a unit spends
in the system, and inventory, the number of units in it at any moment. These three are linked by
Little's law, which states that inventory equals throughput times flow time and holds for any stable
process regardless of its internal complexity.

The bottleneck is the resource with the least capacity relative to demand, and it determines the
throughput of the entire process. Improvements anywhere else change nothing, which sounds obvious
and is routinely violated in practice — investment in a non-bottleneck resource is a common and
expensive mistake. The corollary is that time lost at the bottleneck is lost permanently for the
whole system while time lost elsewhere is usually recoverable.

Utilization and responsiveness trade against each other in a way that surprises people who have not
seen the queueing result. As a resource's utilization approaches its capacity, waiting time grows
hyperbolically rather than linearly, so the last few percent of utilization cost enormously in flow
time. A process designed to keep every resource fully busy will have long and unpredictable lead
times, and deliberately leaving capacity idle is what buys responsiveness.

Q: A factory adds capacity to a machine that is not the bottleneck. What happens to throughput?
A. It increases proportionally
B. Nothing — throughput is set by the bottleneck, so capacity added elsewhere only increases idle
   time or accumulates work-in-progress inventory ahead of the constraint
C. It decreases
D. It increases slightly
Reasoning: The constraining resource determines system output. Non-bottleneck capacity that was
already sufficient remains sufficient. The visible effect is usually more inventory piling up before
the bottleneck, which is a cost rather than a benefit. Answer: B.

## Inventory and Variability

Inventory exists for identifiable reasons and each reason implies a different management approach.
Cycle stock results from ordering in batches larger than immediate need. Safety stock buffers
uncertainty in demand and supply. Pipeline stock is material in transit. Anticipation stock is built
ahead of known demand peaks or supply interruptions. Inventory held for none of these reasons is
waste, and classifying what is on hand by reason is a useful diagnostic exercise.

The economic order quantity balances ordering cost against holding cost and yields a square-root
relationship: the optimal batch size grows with the square root of demand and of ordering cost. The
practical implication is that the total cost curve is flat near the optimum, so an order quantity
within a wide band of the calculated value costs almost the same — which means precision in the
calculation matters far less than getting the order of magnitude right, and that reducing setup or
ordering cost shifts the optimum substantially.

Safety stock is determined by the variability of demand during the replenishment lead time and by
the service level chosen. Both drivers are worth noting: halving the lead time reduces the required
safety stock by more than half because the variability accumulates over a shorter window, and
increasing the service level target has an accelerating cost because the normal tail requires
disproportionately more stock for each additional increment of coverage. Lead time reduction is
therefore usually a better investment than accepting a lower service level.

## Forecasting and Planning

Forecasting supports every planning decision and is always wrong; the useful question is how wrong
and in which direction. A forecast without an error estimate cannot support an inventory decision,
because the safety stock depends entirely on the forecast error distribution rather than on the
forecast itself. Measuring and tracking forecast error is therefore more valuable than improving the
point forecast, and it is more often neglected.

Two properties of forecasting are worth internalizing. Aggregate forecasts are more accurate than
disaggregated ones, because individual variations partially cancel, so a total is predictable when
its components are not. And shorter horizons are more accurate than longer ones, roughly
proportionally to the square root of the horizon for many series. Together these say that the
planning system should commit to specifics as late as possible and at as aggregate a level as the
decision permits.

Planning hierarchies reflect this. Aggregate planning sets capacity and workforce over months at a
product-family level. Master scheduling commits to specific products over weeks. Detailed scheduling
sequences work over days and hours. Each level constrains the next, and the discipline is to make
each decision at the latest level where it can still be executed — a commitment made earlier than
necessary is a forecast error waiting to be paid for.

## Lean, Quality, and Improvement

Lean thinking defines value from the customer's perspective and treats everything else as waste to
be eliminated. The waste categories — overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing,
inventory, motion, and defects — are a checklist for examining a process, and overproduction is
singled out as the worst because it generates the others: producing more than needed creates
inventory, which requires transport and storage and conceals defects until much later.

Flow and pull are the structural principles. Flow means work moves continuously without accumulating
between steps, which requires balanced capacity and small batches. Pull means downstream demand
triggers upstream production rather than a schedule pushing material forward, which prevents
overproduction by construction. The two together produce a system where problems are visible
immediately, because there is no inventory buffer concealing them — which is uncomfortable and is
precisely the point.

Quality management shifted from inspecting defects out to building processes that do not produce
them. The insight underlying it is that inspection is expensive, imperfect, and occurs after the cost
has been incurred, while process control prevents the defect. Statistical process control
distinguishes common-cause variation inherent in the process from special-cause variation from an
identifiable source, and this distinction is the practical core: reacting to common-cause variation
as though it had a specific cause adds variation rather than reducing it, which is one of the most
common and least recognized management errors.

## Supply Networks, Logistics, and Risk

Supply network design decides where facilities are, what each does, and how material moves between
them, and the decisions are long-lived and expensive to reverse. The recurring tradeoff is between
centralization, which pools demand variability and concentrates fixed costs efficiently, and
distribution, which reduces transport cost and lead time to customers. The optimum depends on the
ratio of inbound to outbound transport cost, on the value density of the product, and on the service
level required.

Transportation mode selection follows a consistent cost-speed relationship, and the total cost
comparison must include inventory. A slower mode has lower freight cost and more material in transit,
which is inventory the business is financing, and for high-value goods the inventory carrying cost
can exceed the freight saving entirely. Comparing modes on freight cost alone systematically favours
slow modes for products where it should not.

Supply risk received sustained attention after a sequence of disruptions demonstrated that
efficiency-optimized chains are fragile. The vulnerabilities are concentration — single sources,
single facilities, single regions — and lack of visibility, since most organizations know their
direct suppliers and not their suppliers' suppliers, where the actual concentration frequently lies.
The mitigations all cost something: dual sourcing, buffer inventory, qualified alternates, and
regional redundancy each trade efficiency for resilience, and the honest framing is that resilience
is purchased rather than achieved through better management.
