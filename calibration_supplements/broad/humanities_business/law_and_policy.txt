## Legal Systems and Reasoning

Legal systems divide broadly into common law and civil law traditions, and the difference
shapes how law is found and argued. Common law systems, originating in England, develop law
substantially through judicial decisions that bind later courts under the doctrine of
precedent, so the law on a question is assembled from a line of cases. Civil law systems,
descended from Roman law and codified in the Napoleonic and German traditions, place primary
authority in comprehensive statutory codes, with judicial decisions carrying persuasive rather
than binding force.

The distinction is real and often overstated. Common law jurisdictions are heavily statutory
in most practical areas, and civil law jurisdictions accumulate consistent lines of decisions
that function much as precedent does. The practical difference is one of emphasis in reasoning:
a common law argument reasons by analogy from decided cases, and a civil law argument reasons
by deduction from code provisions, and lawyers trained in one find the other's style
unfamiliar.

Precedent operates through the distinction between the holding and everything else. The ratio
decidendi is the legal principle necessary to the decision and is what binds; obiter dicta are
observations not necessary to the outcome and are merely persuasive. Distinguishing a case —
arguing that a factual difference makes the precedent inapplicable — is the standard technique
for arguing against binding authority, and the art of common law advocacy is largely the art of
characterizing facts so that favourable precedents apply and unfavourable ones do not.

Legal reasoning concepts:
  common law            precedent-driven; law develops through decided cases
  civil law             code-driven; comprehensive statutes as primary authority
  stare decisis         courts follow their own and higher courts' prior decisions
  ratio decidendi       the binding principle; what was necessary to the decision
  obiter dicta          observations not necessary; persuasive only
  distinguishing        arguing a precedent does not apply on the facts
  binding vs persuasive  hierarchy determines which decisions must be followed
  burden of proof       who must prove what; determines outcome when evidence is
                        balanced
  standards of proof    beyond reasonable doubt (criminal), balance of
                        probabilities (civil), clear and convincing (intermediate)
  statutory interpretation  text, structure, purpose, and legislative history;
                        their relative weight is itself contested
  jurisdiction          which court and which law applies; frequently the first
                        and most consequential question
  procedural vs substantive  how a case proceeds versus what the law requires

The standards of proof deserve emphasis because they are frequently conflated in public
discussion. A criminal conviction requires proof beyond reasonable doubt, which is deliberately
demanding because the state is depriving a person of liberty and the system accepts that some
guilty people will be acquitted rather than convict the innocent. A civil claim requires only
that the claim be more likely than not. The same conduct can therefore produce an acquittal and
a civil judgment without any inconsistency, since different questions were asked at different
thresholds.

Q: A defendant is acquitted in a criminal trial and later found liable for the same conduct in
a civil suit. This is:
A. A contradiction requiring one verdict to be overturned
B. Consistent, because the standards of proof differ
C. Double jeopardy
D. Only possible in civil law systems
Reasoning: Criminal liability requires proof beyond reasonable doubt; civil liability requires
only a balance of probabilities. Evidence insufficient for the first can be sufficient for the
second, and the two proceedings ask different questions with different consequences. Double
jeopardy bars repeated criminal prosecution, not a subsequent civil claim. Answer: B.

## Constitutional Structure and Public Law

A constitution establishes the structure of government and, in most modern systems, limits on
its power. Separation of powers divides authority among legislative, executive, and judicial
branches so that each checks the others, on the theory that concentrated power is dangerous
regardless of who holds it. Federalism divides authority between national and subnational
governments, creating a second axis of division and a persistent set of questions about which
level may act.

Judicial review — the power of courts to invalidate legislation inconsistent with the
constitution — is not universal and its scope varies enormously. Some systems vest it in a
specialized constitutional court, some in the ordinary judiciary, and some jurisdictions retain
parliamentary sovereignty in which courts may interpret and not invalidate primary legislation.
The design tradeoff is between protecting entrenched rights against transient majorities and
subjecting fundamental questions to an unelected body, and reasonable systems land in different
places.

Rights protections operate through a common structure across systems even where the specific
rights differ. A right is identified, an interference is established, and the question becomes
whether the interference is justified — typically requiring that it pursue a legitimate aim, be
prescribed by law, be necessary, and be proportionate to the aim. Proportionality analysis is
now the dominant framework internationally, and it makes explicit that most rights are not
absolute but are weighed against competing interests.

Constitutional concepts:
  separation of powers   legislative, executive, judicial; mutual checks
  federalism             divided authority between levels of government
  judicial review        courts invalidating unconstitutional acts; scope varies
  parliamentary sovereignty  the legislature is supreme; courts interpret only
  entrenched rights      protected against ordinary legislative change
  proportionality        legitimate aim, prescribed by law, necessary, and
                         proportionate; the dominant rights framework
  derogation             permitted suspension in emergencies; typically with
                         non-derogable core rights
  due process            fair procedure before deprivation of life, liberty, or
                         property
  equality               formal equality treats alike cases alike; substantive
                         equality attends to unequal starting positions
  rule of law            law is public, prospective, general, stable, and applied
                         to the governing as well as the governed
  administrative law     controls how agencies exercise delegated power; reasons,
                         fair process, and reviewable decisions

Administrative law has grown in importance as legislatures delegate increasing detail to
agencies, and its core requirements are procedural rather than substantive. An agency acting
under delegated authority must generally stay within the scope of the delegation, follow the
prescribed procedure, consider relevant factors and disregard irrelevant ones, give reasons,
and reach a decision a reasonable body could reach. Courts reviewing such decisions typically
examine the process rather than substituting their own judgment on the merits, which is what
distinguishes review from appeal.

## Contract, Tort, and Private Law

Contract law enforces voluntary agreements, and the elements required for enforceability are
consistent across systems even where the terminology differs. There must be agreement,
typically analysed as offer and acceptance; an intention to create legal relations, which is
presumed in commercial contexts and not in social ones; and in common law systems,
consideration — something of value exchanged — which civil law systems generally do not
require, relying instead on the seriousness of the intention.

The remedies for breach reveal the law's underlying theory. The default remedy is damages
calculated to place the injured party in the position they would have occupied had the contract
been performed — expectation damages. Specific performance, compelling actual performance, is
exceptional and available mainly where damages are inadequate because the subject matter is
unique. This ordering reflects a view that contract law protects the economic value of the
bargain rather than enforcing promises as such.

Tort law addresses civil wrongs outside contract, and negligence is its dominant form. A
negligence claim requires a duty of care owed to the claimant, a breach of that duty measured
against the standard of a reasonable person, causation both factual and legal, and damage. Each
element does real work: duty limits to whom obligations are owed, breach sets the standard,
factual causation asks whether the harm would have occurred anyway, and legal causation asks
whether the harm was too remote a consequence to be fairly attributed.

Private law essentials:
  offer and acceptance   the mechanics of agreement
  consideration          value exchanged; a common law requirement
  capacity               minors and those lacking mental capacity
  vitiating factors      misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue influence;
                         each can void or make voidable
  terms                  express and implied; implied by statute, custom, or
                         necessity
  breach and remedies    expectation damages by default; specific performance
                         exceptionally
  mitigation             the injured party must take reasonable steps to limit loss
  limitation of liability  enforceable within limits; consumer protection
                         legislation restricts what may be excluded
  negligence elements    duty, breach, causation, damage
  reasonable person      an objective standard, not the defendant's own judgment
  but-for causation      would the harm have occurred anyway
  remoteness             whether the harm was a foreseeable consequence
  strict liability       liability without fault; product defects, hazardous
                         activities
  vicarious liability    an employer's liability for employee acts in the course
                         of employment

Vicarious liability is worth understanding for anyone in an organization because it means the
organization bears responsibility for employee conduct within the scope of employment,
regardless of whether the organization was itself at fault. The rationale is that the
enterprise creates the risk and profits from the activity, and is better placed to insure and
to control conduct. Its scope — what counts as within the course of employment — is where
litigation concentrates, and it has generally expanded over time.

## Intellectual Property

Intellectual property grants time-limited exclusive rights over intangible creations, and the
justification is instrumental: creation is costly and copying is cheap, so without exclusivity
the incentive to create is undersupplied. The framing matters because it makes the scope and
duration of rights an empirical question about incentives rather than a matter of natural
entitlement, and because it implies that rights broader than necessary to induce creation are a
net social cost.

The four principal forms protect different things and are frequently confused. Copyright
protects original expression fixed in a tangible form, arising automatically without
registration, and lasting for the author's life plus several decades. Patents protect
inventions that are novel, non-obvious, and useful, requiring examination and disclosure, and
lasting around twenty years. Trademarks protect indicators of commercial origin, last
indefinitely while used and defended, and exist to prevent consumer confusion rather than to
reward creation. Trade secrets protect information kept confidential and last as long as
secrecy does, with no protection against independent discovery or reverse engineering.

The idea-expression distinction is the most important limiting principle in copyright.
Copyright protects the particular expression and not the underlying idea, method, or fact,
which is why a recipe's list of ingredients is unprotected while the accompanying prose is,
and why an independently written program implementing the same algorithm does not infringe.
Without this distinction copyright would function as a patent of unlimited duration and
without examination.

Intellectual property comparison:
  copyright         original expression; automatic; life plus 50-70 years;
                    protects expression, not ideas or facts
  patent            novel, non-obvious, useful inventions; ~20 years; requires
                    examination and public disclosure
  trademark         source indicators; indefinite while used; prevents confusion
  trade secret      confidential information; indefinite; no protection against
                    independent discovery
  fair use / dealing  limited unlicensed use; criticism, comment, news, teaching,
                    research, parody; a multi-factor and fact-specific test
  first sale        the right to resell a lawfully acquired copy
  moral rights      attribution and integrity; stronger in civil law systems
  open source       copyright used to impose sharing conditions rather than to
                    restrict; permissive licences ask only attribution, copyleft
                    licences require derivatives to carry the same terms
  licence compliance  obligations attach to distribution; the most common failure
                    is shipping copyleft code without the corresponding source
  employee inventions  ownership usually vests in the employer for work-related
                    creation, subject to statutory limits in some jurisdictions

Open source licensing deserves attention because it is a use of copyright that inverts its
usual function. The licence grants permissions that copyright would otherwise withhold, in
exchange for conditions. Permissive licences impose little beyond attribution. Copyleft licences
require that derivative works be distributed under the same terms, which propagates the sharing
obligation. The practical compliance question is what triggers the obligations — typically
distribution rather than internal use, with network-use provisions in some licences extending
this to software provided as a service — and organizations regularly fail here not through
deliberate infringement but through not tracking what is in their dependency tree.

## Regulation, Data Protection, and Policy Analysis

Regulation intervenes in markets where they fail, and the categories of failure map onto
regulatory instruments. Externalities call for taxes, tradable permits, or direct standards.
Information asymmetries call for disclosure requirements, licensing, and warranties. Natural
monopolies call for price regulation or structural separation. Systemic risk calls for
prudential requirements. Identifying which failure a regulation addresses is the first step in
assessing whether it is well designed.

The instrument choice within a category matters as much as the decision to regulate.
Prescriptive rules specify conduct precisely, which gives certainty and adapts poorly to
circumstances the drafter did not anticipate. Principles-based regulation states outcomes and
leaves the method to the regulated party, which adapts well and creates uncertainty about
compliance. Performance standards specify a result rather than a technology, which permits
innovation in how the result is achieved. Most regimes combine these.

Data protection has become the most operationally significant regulatory area for technology
work. The dominant framework rests on a set of principles: processing must have a lawful basis,
must be for specified purposes, must be limited to what is necessary, must be accurate, must be
retained no longer than needed, and must be secure. Individuals hold rights of access,
rectification, erasure, portability, and objection. Transfers to jurisdictions without adequate
protection require additional safeguards.

Regulation and data protection:
  lawful basis          consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests,
                        public task, or legitimate interests; consent is not the
                        default and is often the weakest option
  purpose limitation    collected for specified purposes; further processing must
                        be compatible
  data minimization     collect only what is necessary
  storage limitation    retain no longer than needed; requires a retention schedule
  accuracy              keep it correct and up to date
  security              appropriate technical and organizational measures
  accountability        demonstrate compliance, not merely achieve it
  subject rights        access, rectification, erasure, portability, objection
  automated decisions   rights regarding solely automated decisions with
                        significant effects
  DPIA                  assessment required for high-risk processing
  breach notification   within specified time limits
  privacy by design     build protection in rather than adding it afterward
  cross-border transfer adequacy decisions or contractual safeguards

The architectural consequence for engineering work is that data protection requirements are
design constraints rather than a compliance layer added afterward. The right to erasure
conflicts with append-only immutable storage, so either a table format supporting row-level
deletion or crypto-shredding must be chosen before the first request arrives. Purpose
limitation conflicts with collecting data for unspecified future use. Data minimization
conflicts with logging everything by default. Each of these is far cheaper to accommodate at
design time than to retrofit, which is the substance of the privacy-by-design principle.

Policy analysis assesses whether an intervention achieves its aims at acceptable cost, and its
standard tools have known limitations worth stating. Cost-benefit analysis quantifies effects
in monetary terms, which forces explicit valuation and requires assigning values to things —
health, mortality risk, ecosystems — where the valuation is contested. Discounting future
effects to present value is necessary and the choice of discount rate dominates conclusions
about long-horizon problems, which is why climate policy analyses reach different conclusions
primarily through that parameter. Distributional analysis asks who bears the costs and receives
the benefits, and its omission is how policies that improve aggregate welfare while harming
identifiable groups are adopted without anticipating the resulting political resistance.

Unintended consequences arise reliably enough to warrant systematic anticipation. A rule
changes the payoffs facing regulated parties, and they adjust on margins the drafter did not
consider — substituting an unregulated alternative, relocating the activity, restructuring to
fall outside the definition, or complying formally while defeating the purpose. Anticipating
this requires asking not whether the rule prohibits the conduct but what the cheapest available
response to the rule is, which is a different and more useful question.
