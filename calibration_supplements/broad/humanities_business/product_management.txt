## Problem Definition

Product work fails most often at the beginning, by solving a problem nobody has or by solving the
wrong version of a real one. The discipline that prevents this is separating the problem statement
from the solution and holding it separate long enough to examine it, which is difficult because
solutions are concrete and satisfying while problems are abstract and uncomfortable.

A well-formed problem statement identifies who has the problem, what they are trying to accomplish,
what currently prevents it, what they do instead, and what it costs them. The last two elements are
the ones most often missing and the most diagnostic. If there is no current workaround, the problem
may not be real enough for anyone to act on. If the workaround is cheap, the solution must be very
good to displace it, and the relevant comparison is against that workaround rather than against
nothing.

Requests are not problems, and translating between them is a core skill. A user asking for a
specific feature has already performed a solution step, and the underlying need may be better served
another way — or may be served adequately by something that already exists. The question that
recovers the problem is what they were trying to do and what happened when they tried, asked about a
specific recent instance rather than in general, because specific recollection is far more reliable
than generalization.

Q: Several users request the same specific feature. What is the appropriate response?
A. Build it; the demand is clear
B. Investigate what each was trying to accomplish, since a shared request may reflect several
   different underlying needs or one need that a different solution serves better, and the request
   already encodes a solution the users chose
C. Ignore requests as a category
D. Survey more users about the feature
Reasoning: Convergent requests are strong evidence that a problem exists and weak evidence about
what would solve it, because users propose solutions from their own limited view of what is
possible. Investigating the underlying goal frequently reveals either a better solution or that the
requests share a label and not a need. Answer: B.

## Discovery and Research

Discovery reduces uncertainty about what to build, and the methods available answer different
questions. Interviews reveal goals, context, and workarounds, and they are unreliable for
predicting behaviour — people describe what they would like to think they would do. Observation
reveals what people actually do, including workarounds they do not consciously notice. Surveys
quantify prevalence across a population and require that the right questions are already known.
Analytics reveal what happened at scale and never reveal why.

The methods are complementary and the common error is using one where another is required.
Analytics showing that users abandon a flow at a particular step establishes that something is wrong
there and cannot establish what; that requires watching people attempt it. Interviews suggesting a
need is widespread establish that some people have it and cannot establish prevalence; that requires
a survey or behavioural data.

Interviewing well is a specific skill with a few rules that carry most of the value. Ask about past
behaviour rather than future intentions, since the former is recalled and the latter is invented.
Ask about specific instances rather than typical patterns. Avoid leading questions and avoid
describing your idea, because people are agreeable and will validate almost anything presented
enthusiastically. And listen for the emotional weight, since the problems people describe with
frustration are the ones they will act to solve.

Discovery methods:
  interviews           goals, context, and workarounds
  the interview limit  unreliable for predicting behaviour
  observation          what people actually do, including unnoticed workarounds
  surveys              prevalence, once the right questions are known
  analytics            what happened at scale, never why
  the complementarity  each answers a question the others cannot
  the common error     using one method for another's question
  past behaviour       recalled rather than invented
  specific instances   rather than typical patterns
  leading questions    people validate almost anything presented enthusiastically
  the enthusiasm trap  describing your idea contaminates every response after it

  concierge and prototype tests  delivering the outcome manually to test demand

  the willingness signal  what someone does — pays, switches, commits time — is
                       evidence in a way that what they say is not

## Prioritization and Strategy

Prioritization is the central activity, because the constraint is always capacity and the question
is always what not to do. Frameworks that score items on impact, confidence, and effort are useful
mainly for making the reasoning explicit rather than for producing the answer — the numbers are
estimates and the ranking they produce should be examined rather than obeyed. Their real value is
exposing where a disagreement is: two people scoring an item differently usually disagree about a
specific assumption, and identifying it is more productive than averaging the scores.

Sequencing matters beyond ranking. Work that unblocks other work is worth more than its own value.
Work that reduces uncertainty is worth more when the uncertainty is large. Work that must be done
eventually and gets harder with delay should be done early. A ranked list treats items as
independent and they rarely are, which is why the output of prioritization should be a sequence with
reasoning rather than a sorted backlog.

Strategy is the set of choices about where to compete and how to win, and its defining property is
that it excludes things. A strategy that accommodates every opportunity is a description rather than
a strategy, and the test is whether it tells you what to say no to. Product strategy specifically
should state which users, which problems, what advantage the product has, and why that advantage
persists — and the last element is the one most often absent, leaving a plan that works until
someone else does the same thing.

## Metrics and Measurement

A product metric should reflect value delivered to users, and the recurring failure is choosing one
that reflects activity instead. Engagement measured as time spent is optimized by making a product
harder to use. Session count is optimized by fragmenting a task. Feature usage is optimized by
placing the feature where it is unavoidable. Each of these has been driven up by teams who then
discovered the product had become worse.

The structure that works uses a small number of metrics with defined roles. One primary metric that
represents the value the product creates. A small set of counter-metrics that catch degradation the
primary would not reveal. And diagnostic metrics that explain movement without being targets. Making
a metric a target changes behaviour toward it, which is why the number of targets should be small
and chosen carefully.

Leading and lagging indicators serve different purposes and both are needed. Lagging indicators —
retention, revenue, satisfaction — measure what matters and move slowly, so they cannot guide
week-to-week decisions. Leading indicators move quickly and predict the lagging ones, and the
predictive relationship must be established rather than assumed. A leading indicator that has not
been validated against a lagging one is a proxy for nothing, and teams frequently optimize such
proxies for quarters before discovering the connection was never there.

## Launch, Iteration, and Communication

Launching is a process rather than an event, and staging it reduces risk substantially. Internal use
catches the obvious. A limited external group catches what internal use cannot, because internal
users know too much. A percentage rollout with monitoring catches what small groups cannot, because
some failures appear only at scale. Each stage should have a defined question it answers and a
criterion for proceeding, otherwise it becomes a delay rather than a check.

Iteration after launch is where most of the value is created and where attention typically
collapses. The initial version is a hypothesis, the usage data is the first real evidence about it,
and the period immediately after launch is when that evidence is richest and the team's context is
freshest. Teams that move to the next project at launch systematically leave value uncollected, and
the practice that prevents it is planning the post-launch iteration period as part of the work
rather than as a contingency.

Communication is a large fraction of the role and its content differs by audience. Engineers need
the problem, the constraints, and the reasoning behind the priority, so they can propose better
solutions than the one specified. Executives need the decision required, the options, and the
recommendation with its reasoning, compressed. Customers need what changed and why it helps them.
Presenting the same material to all three is the default and it serves none of them.
