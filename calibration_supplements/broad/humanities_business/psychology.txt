## Research Methods and the Replication Crisis

Psychology's methods deserve to be discussed first because the field's credibility
depends on them and because a great deal of widely repeated psychological "knowledge"
has not survived scrutiny. The core problem is that psychological effects are usually
small, variable across contexts, and measured with noisy instruments, which makes the
discipline unusually vulnerable to methodological error.

Experimental design in psychology follows the general logic of causal inference. Random
assignment to conditions is what licenses causal claims, since it makes groups
equivalent in expectation on every variable, measured or not. Control groups establish
the counterfactual. Blinding prevents expectation effects: single-blind keeps
participants unaware of their condition, double-blind keeps experimenters unaware too,
which matters because experimenters unconsciously cue participants toward expected
results. Placebo controls are essential wherever expectation itself can produce the
outcome, which in psychology is nearly everywhere.

The replication crisis emerged when large coordinated efforts attempted to reproduce
published findings and succeeded only around forty percent of the time in some
subfields. The causes are structural rather than fraudulent. Publication bias means
positive results are published and null results are not, so the literature is a biased
sample of the research conducted. Researcher degrees of freedom — undisclosed choices
about which participants to exclude, which measures to analyze, when to stop collecting
data, which covariates to include — allow a determined analyst to reach significance from
noise without any conscious dishonesty. Small samples produce underpowered studies that
either miss real effects or, when they do reach significance, necessarily overestimate
the effect size.

Methodological reforms and concepts:
  preregistration      hypotheses and analysis plan filed before data collection,
                       eliminating undisclosed flexibility
  registered reports   peer review of the design before results exist; acceptance
                       does not depend on the outcome
  power analysis       required sample size computed in advance from the smallest
                       effect worth detecting
  effect size          Cohen's d, correlation, odds ratio; report alongside p, since
                       significance without magnitude is uninformative
  confidence interval  the range of effects compatible with the data; more
                       informative than a binary significance verdict
  p-hacking            analytic flexibility exploited until p < 0.05
  HARKing              hypothesizing after results are known, presented as a priori
  multiverse analysis  report results across all defensible analytic choices
  open data and code   permits reanalysis and error detection

The distinction between statistical and practical significance is where a great deal of
psychological reporting goes wrong. With a large enough sample, a trivially small
difference reaches significance; with a small sample, a large effect may not. The
question that matters is the size of the effect and whether it is large enough to matter
for the application, which is why effect sizes and intervals should be the headline and
the p-value a footnote. Cohen's conventional benchmarks — d of 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8
large — are rough and field-dependent, and a small effect can be enormously important
when applied at population scale or trivial when applied to an individual.

Q: A study with 40 participants finds a significant effect with d = 1.2. A replication
with 400 participants finds d = 0.15, not significant. The most likely interpretation is:
A. The replication was flawed
B. The original overestimated the effect, as underpowered significant results must
C. The effect disappeared over time
D. The larger sample introduced noise
Reasoning: An underpowered study can only reach significance when the observed effect is
large, so conditional on being significant, its effect estimate is inflated — the winner's
curse, or Type M error. The larger study gives a more precise estimate with a narrower
interval. This pattern is the signature of the replication crisis rather than of a flawed
replication. Answer: B.

## Perception and Attention

Perception is constructive rather than passive. The sensory apparatus delivers an
impoverished, ambiguous signal — a two-dimensional retinal projection of a
three-dimensional world, with a blind spot, poor peripheral acuity, and constant
saccadic motion — and the visual system infers a stable scene from it using strong prior
assumptions about how the world is structured. Visual illusions are not malfunctions;
they are cases where those assumptions, normally correct, are deliberately violated.

The Gestalt principles describe how the system groups elements into objects: proximity,
similarity, closure, continuity, common fate, and figure-ground segregation. These operate
before conscious inspection and are difficult to override, which is why they are so
powerful in interface and information design — elements placed close together are
perceived as related whatever the labels say, and a designer fighting a Gestalt principle
loses.

Attention is a selection mechanism made necessary by limited processing capacity. Selective
attention filters the incoming stream, as demonstrated by the cocktail party effect: a
listener follows one conversation among many, yet their own name spoken in an unattended
stream captures attention, showing that unattended input is processed at least to the
level of meaning. Divided attention is severely limited, and the popular notion of
multitasking is mostly rapid task-switching with a substantial switching cost — measurably
slower and more error-prone than sequential completion.

Attention phenomena worth knowing:
  inattentional blindness   failure to notice an unexpected object while attending
                            elsewhere; the invisible gorilla demonstration
  change blindness          failure to detect large scene changes across an
                            interruption; we retain far less than it feels like
  attentional blink         a second target within ~500 ms of the first is missed
  Stroop effect             naming ink colour is slowed by conflicting word meaning;
                            reading is automatic and cannot be suppressed
  task-switching cost       measurable time and accuracy penalty per switch
  vigilance decrement       sustained attention degrades within 20-30 minutes
  cocktail party effect     selective attention with breakthrough for salient input

Inattentional and change blindness together undermine the intuition that we perceive a
complete, detailed scene. What we actually maintain is a sparse representation plus the
ability to look again, and the feeling of richness comes from the fact that whenever we
check a detail, it is there. This has direct practical consequences: eyewitness testimony
of unattended events is unreliable, safety-critical displays cannot assume that an
unexpected change will be noticed, and interfaces must actively direct attention rather
than assume it.

## Memory

Memory is not a recording. It is a reconstructive process in which a partial trace is
combined with schemas, expectations, and post-event information to produce something that
feels like a recollection. This is the single most consequential fact about memory and
the one most at odds with intuition, and it has reshaped legal practice around eyewitness
evidence.

The standard model divides memory into stages. Sensory memory holds a large-capacity,
very brief trace — under a second for vision. Short-term or working memory holds a small
amount for tens of seconds, classically estimated at seven plus or minus two items though
modern estimates cluster nearer four when rehearsal is prevented. Long-term memory has no
known capacity limit and can persist for a lifetime. Information moves from short-term to
long-term through encoding, which is greatly improved by depth of processing — attending
to meaning rather than to surface features — and by relating new material to existing
knowledge.

Working memory is better understood as an active system than as a storage buffer.
Baddeley's model comprises a phonological loop for verbal material, a visuospatial
sketchpad for visual and spatial material, an episodic buffer integrating them, and a
central executive allocating attention. The practical implication is that verbal and
spatial tasks interfere with each other far less than two verbal tasks do, which is why
listening to instrumental music while reading is easier than listening to speech.

Memory systems and effects:
  episodic            personal events with context; the "remembering" system
  semantic            general facts, decontextualized
  procedural          skills, implicit, resistant to forgetting and to conscious
                      access
  spacing effect      distributed practice beats massed practice, substantially
  testing effect      retrieval practice strengthens memory more than restudying
  serial position     primacy and recency; middle items are recalled worst
  encoding specificity retrieval succeeds when cues match encoding context
  interference        proactive (old disrupts new) and retroactive (new disrupts old)
  reconsolidation     retrieved memories become labile and are re-stored, altered
  misinformation      post-event information incorporated into the memory itself
  flashbulb memories  vivid and confidently held; no more accurate than ordinary
                      memories, which is the striking finding

The spacing and testing effects are the two best-supported findings in applied cognitive
psychology and remain widely ignored in educational practice. Distributing study across
sessions produces far better long-term retention than the same total time massed, even
though massed practice produces better immediate performance — which is exactly why
learners prefer it and misjudge its effectiveness. Retrieval practice, attempting to
recall rather than rereading, likewise feels harder and less productive while producing
substantially better retention. Both are instances of desirable difficulty: conditions
that impair performance during learning while improving it afterward.

Amnesia dissociates the memory systems in a way that reveals their independence.
Patients with hippocampal damage typically lose the ability to form new episodic memories
while retaining procedural learning — they can acquire a motor skill across sessions
while denying ever having practised it. This double dissociation is the strongest
evidence that these are genuinely separate systems rather than aspects of one.

## Learning, Cognition, and Bias

Classical conditioning associates a neutral stimulus with a biologically significant one
until the neutral stimulus alone produces the response. The details matter more than the
famous demonstration: conditioning depends on the conditioned stimulus reliably
predicting the unconditioned one, not merely on pairing, which is why a stimulus that
occurs both with and without the outcome conditions poorly. This predictive framing,
formalized in the Rescorla-Wagner model, connects conditioning directly to prediction
error and hence to reinforcement learning in machine learning.

Operant conditioning shapes behaviour through consequences. Reinforcement increases
behaviour and punishment decreases it, and each can be positive (adding a stimulus) or
negative (removing one) — a two-by-two whose labels are frequently confused. Negative
reinforcement is not punishment; it is the strengthening of behaviour by removing
something aversive, which is why taking a painkiller is reinforced.

Reinforcement schedules govern how behaviour is maintained. Continuous reinforcement
produces fast acquisition and fast extinction. Partial schedules produce slower
acquisition and much greater resistance to extinction, and variable-ratio schedules —
reinforcement after an unpredictable number of responses — produce the highest and most
persistent response rates. This is the schedule that gambling machines and infinite-scroll
feeds implement, and it is not a coincidence.

Cognitive biases, grouped by what produces them:
  availability heuristic   judging frequency by ease of recall; media coverage
                           distorts risk perception systematically
  representativeness       judging by similarity to a prototype; produces base rate
                           neglect and the conjunction fallacy
  anchoring                initial values contaminate subsequent estimates, even
                           when transparently irrelevant
  confirmation bias        seeking and weighting evidence that supports current
                           belief; the most consequential single bias
  hindsight bias           past events seem more predictable after the fact
  fundamental attribution  others' behaviour attributed to disposition, one's own
                           to situation
  loss aversion            losses weigh roughly twice as heavily as equal gains
  sunk cost fallacy        continuing because of unrecoverable prior investment
  Dunning-Kruger           poor calibration at low skill; substantially a
                           statistical artifact of regression plus a real effect
  overconfidence           subjective confidence exceeds accuracy across domains

Dual-process theory frames these as arising from two modes of thinking: a fast,
automatic, associative system and a slow, effortful, rule-based one. The framing is
useful as an organizing metaphor and should not be reified — there is no anatomical
"System 1" — but it captures the real phenomenon that most judgment is intuitive and that
deliberate reasoning is expensive enough that it is deployed sparingly. Biases are the
predictable output of heuristics that are efficient and usually adequate, which is why
simply knowing about a bias rarely eliminates it; structural corrections such as
checklists, prespecified criteria, and independent judgments aggregated afterward work
far better than exhortations to think carefully.

Unconscious bias training reliably changes what people say on attitude measures and
reliably fails to change hiring outcomes. Some studies find backlash effects where it
increases resentment or licenses subsequent biased behaviour — having demonstrated
one's good faith by attending. It is popular because it is cheap and visible, not
because it works.

What does work is removing the discretion that bias operates through.

Structured interviews are the highest-value change. Every candidate gets the same
questions in the same order, answers are scored against a rubric defined before anyone is
interviewed, and interviewers record scores independently before discussing. This roughly
doubles the predictive validity of the interview compared with unstructured conversation,
and it constrains bias because there is far less room for an impression to shape which
questions get asked and how answers are interpreted.

Work samples are equally strong and often better received. A task resembling the actual
job, scored blind against a rubric, predicts performance as well as anything available.
Keep it short and paid if it is substantial.

Blind resume screening helps at the top of the funnel, though the evidence is context
dependent — it removes name and demographic cues but not school, employer, or activity
signals that correlate with background.

Two process points that matter more than they sound. Define the evaluation criteria and
their weights before you see any candidates, because criteria chosen afterward get chosen
to fit whoever you already like. And have interviewers submit independent scores before
any group discussion, since the first opinion voiced in a debrief anchors the rest of the
panel and converts several independent judgments into one.

## Development, Social, and Clinical

Developmental psychology studies change across the lifespan. Piaget's stage theory —
sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational — remains a useful
descriptive framework while being substantially revised in detail: infants demonstrate
object permanence and rudimentary number sense far earlier than Piaget's methods
detected, because his tasks required motor responses that infants could not yet produce.
Vygotsky's emphasis on social scaffolding and the zone of proximal development — the gap
between what a learner can do alone and with guidance — has proved more directly
applicable to education.

Attachment research established that early caregiver relationships shape later social
functioning. The classification into secure, anxious-ambivalent, avoidant, and
disorganized patterns predicts a range of later outcomes, though the effect sizes are
moderate and the deterministic reading common in popular accounts is not supported.
Development is probabilistic, and the research consistently finds more plasticity than
early formulations expected.

Social psychology studies how the presence and expectations of others shape behaviour,
and its central finding is that situational forces are far stronger than intuition
allows. Conformity experiments show people endorsing obviously wrong perceptual judgments
when a unanimous group does. Obedience studies show ordinary participants administering
what they believe are dangerous shocks under mild authority pressure. Bystander research
shows that the probability of intervention falls as the number of witnesses rises, through
diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance. These classic studies have
methodological and ethical problems that later scholarship has documented carefully, and
the core situational finding has held up in modified replications even where specific
numbers have not.

Social and clinical concepts:
  fundamental attribution error   overweighting disposition, underweighting situation
  cognitive dissonance            discomfort from inconsistent beliefs and actions,
                                  resolved by changing the belief more often than
                                  the action
  self-fulfilling prophecy        expectations altering behaviour to confirm them
  in-group favoritism             arises from arbitrary group assignment alone
  social facilitation             presence of others improves simple tasks and
                                  impairs complex ones
  groupthink                      cohesion suppressing dissent and realistic appraisal
  diathesis-stress                disorder arises from vulnerability plus stressor,
                                  not from either alone
  biopsychosocial model           biological, psychological, and social factors
                                  jointly; the standard framework in clinical work

Clinical psychology addresses psychological disorder, and the definitional question is
harder than it appears. Diagnostic categories are descriptive clusters of symptoms rather
than validated disease entities with known mechanisms, comorbidity between them is the
norm rather than the exception, and the boundary between disorder and ordinary
distress is drawn partly by the criterion of functional impairment. Recognizing this does
not make the categories useless — they organize treatment and communication — but it
argues against treating a diagnosis as an explanation.

Treatment evidence is clearer than diagnostic theory. Cognitive behavioural therapy has
the strongest evidence base across the most conditions, working by identifying and
testing maladaptive thought patterns and by changing behaviour directly through exposure
and activation. Behavioural activation for depression and exposure therapy for anxiety
disorders are among the most robustly supported interventions in the field. Medication is
effective for several conditions and is generally most effective in combination with
therapy rather than alone. Across all modalities, a substantial fraction of the benefit
is attributable to common factors — the therapeutic alliance, expectancy, and a coherent
explanatory framework — rather than to the specific technique, which is why therapist
quality often predicts outcome better than therapeutic orientation does.
