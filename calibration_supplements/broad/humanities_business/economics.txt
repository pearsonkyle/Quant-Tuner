## Scarcity, Opportunity Cost, and Incentives

Economics studies how societies allocate scarce resources among competing uses.
Scarcity is the binding premise: wants exceed available means, so every choice
forecloses others. This gives rise to the field's most portable idea, opportunity cost
— the value of the best forgone alternative. The cost of a year in graduate school is
not tuition; it is tuition plus the salary not earned, minus whatever consumption
value the year itself provides. Accounting cost measures money spent; economic cost
measures what was given up, and the two diverge whenever a resource has an alternative
use that does not involve a transaction.

The sunk cost principle follows directly and is violated constantly. Costs already
incurred and unrecoverable should not influence forward-looking decisions, because
they are identical under every option and therefore cannot distinguish between them.
The rational question about a failing project is always whether the remaining
investment is justified by the remaining expected return, never how much has already
been spent. That the fallacy persists so robustly — in individuals, firms, and
governments — is itself a finding, and behavioral economics treats it as a systematic
feature of human cognition rather than as an error to be scolded away.

Marginal analysis is the operational core of the discipline. Optimal decisions are made
at the margin: continue an activity while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, stop
where they are equal. This resolves the classical diamond-water paradox — water is
essential and cheap while diamonds are frivolous and expensive, because price reflects
the value of the *last* unit rather than the total value of the good, and water is
abundant enough that the marginal litre is worth very little. Total utility and
marginal utility answer different questions, and confusing them produces most naive
pricing errors.

Foundational concepts:
  opportunity cost      value of the best forgone alternative
  sunk cost             already incurred and unrecoverable; irrelevant going forward
  marginal analysis     act while MB > MC; optimum at MB = MC
  diminishing returns   adding one input while holding others fixed eventually
                        raises output at a decreasing rate
  comparative advantage lower opportunity cost, not lower absolute cost, drives
                        gains from specialization and trade
  incentives            behaviour responds to relative payoffs; unintended
                        consequences follow from unmodelled margins of adjustment
  efficiency vs equity  the size of the pie and its division are separable questions

Incentive analysis is where economics earns its reputation for uncomfortable
conclusions, because it predicts responses on margins that policy designers did not
consider. A rule requiring severance pay makes hiring riskier and reduces employment
at the margin. Fuel-efficiency mandates lower the cost per mile and induce more driving,
partially offsetting the intended saving — the rebound effect. A bounty on cobra skins
creates cobra farms. None of these observations settle whether a policy is desirable;
they establish that the full effect includes the behavioural response, and that
evaluating a policy by its intent rather than its incentive structure is not analysis.

Q: A firm has spent $2M developing a product. Completing it requires $500k more and
would generate $700k in revenue. The firm should:
A. Abandon it; total cost of $2.5M exceeds $700k revenue
B. Complete it; $700k exceeds the $500k still required
C. Abandon it; the project was a mistake
D. Complete it to recover the $2M
Reasoning: The $2M is sunk and identical under both options, so it cannot inform the
choice. The forward-looking comparison is $500k of additional cost against $700k of
additional revenue, a $200k gain. The project was indeed a bad investment overall, and
that fact should inform future decisions without affecting this one. Answer: B.

## Supply, Demand, and Elasticity

The demand curve slopes downward because higher prices reduce quantity demanded, through
a substitution effect (buyers switch to alternatives) and an income effect (purchasing
power falls). The supply curve slopes upward because higher prices make production
worthwhile for suppliers with higher costs. Their intersection is the equilibrium — the
only price at which quantity supplied equals quantity demanded, and the price toward
which a competitive market tends because surpluses push prices down and shortages push
them up.

The essential distinction is between a movement along a curve and a shift of the curve.
A change in the good's own price moves along the curve, changing quantity demanded. A
change in anything else — income, the price of substitutes or complements, expectations,
the number of buyers, tastes — shifts the entire curve, changing demand. Conflating the
two produces the circular reasoning that "higher prices increase supply," which
describes movement along a supply curve while sounding like a shift. This is the single
most common error in casual economic argument.

Elasticity measures responsiveness, and it determines who bears costs and how revenue
responds to price. Price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity per
percentage change in price. Demand is elastic when the magnitude exceeds one, so
quantity responds proportionally more than price and a price increase reduces total
revenue. It is inelastic below one, so a price increase raises revenue. Elasticity is
higher when close substitutes exist, when the good is a large share of the budget, when
the purchase is discretionary, and when buyers have more time to adjust — which is why
short-run and long-run elasticities differ substantially, most famously for gasoline,
where the immediate response to a price rise is small and the multi-year response
through vehicle choice and residential location is much larger.

Elasticity reference:
  price elasticity of demand    E = %dQ / %dP;  |E| > 1 elastic, < 1 inelastic
  total revenue test            inelastic: raise price to raise revenue
                                elastic: lower price to raise revenue
                                unit elastic: revenue is at a maximum
  income elasticity             > 0 normal good, > 1 luxury, < 0 inferior good
  cross-price elasticity        > 0 substitutes, < 0 complements
  price elasticity of supply    higher with spare capacity and longer horizons
  tax incidence                 the more inelastic side bears more of the burden,
                                regardless of who legally remits the tax

Tax incidence is the clearest demonstration that legal and economic burden differ. A tax
levied on sellers shifts the supply curve up by the tax amount, raising the price buyers
pay and lowering the price sellers keep; the split between them depends entirely on the
relative elasticities and not at all on which side writes the cheque. Payroll taxes
split nominally between employer and employee are borne overwhelmingly by workers,
because labour supply is far less elastic than labour demand. A tax on a good with
perfectly inelastic demand is paid entirely by buyers; on one with perfectly elastic
demand, entirely by sellers.

Price controls illustrate the same machinery. A binding price ceiling below equilibrium
creates a shortage, and because price can no longer ration the good, other mechanisms
take over: queues, quality degradation, discrimination by sellers, and secondary
markets. Rent control is the standard case, reliably producing reduced housing supply,
reduced maintenance, and allocation by tenure rather than by need. A binding price
floor above equilibrium creates a surplus — agricultural price supports produce
unsellable stockpiles, and minimum wages produce disemployment effects whose empirical
magnitude is genuinely contested, since labour markets deviate from the competitive
model in ways (search frictions, monopsony) that can reverse the textbook prediction.

## Market Structure and Firm Behaviour

Perfect competition is a benchmark rather than a description: many small firms selling
an identical product with free entry and full information, so each faces a horizontal
demand curve and is a price taker. Firms produce where price equals marginal cost,
economic profits attract entry that drives price to minimum average cost, and the
outcome is allocatively and productively efficient. Its analytical value is as a
reference point against which real structures are measured, since the ways actual
markets depart from it are exactly what generates market power.

Monopoly is the opposite pole: one seller facing the entire market demand curve, so it
must lower price to sell more and marginal revenue falls below price. Profit
maximization at marginal revenue equal to marginal cost yields a higher price and lower
quantity than the competitive outcome, transferring surplus from consumers to the firm
and destroying some entirely — the deadweight loss. Monopolies arise from barriers to
entry: control of an essential input, patents and copyrights, network effects,
regulatory protection, and natural monopoly where economies of scale are large enough
relative to the market that one firm can serve it at lower cost than several.

Between the poles lie the common structures. Monopolistic competition has many firms
selling differentiated products with free entry, so firms have some pricing power but
economic profits are competed away, leaving excess capacity and product variety as the
characteristic outcome — restaurants, clothing, consumer software. Oligopoly has few
firms whose decisions are interdependent, which makes it strategic and the natural
domain of game theory. The prisoner's dilemma captures its central tension: collusion
would maximize joint profit, each firm individually gains by defecting, so the
non-cooperative equilibrium is more competitive than the cartel outcome and cartels are
unstable without enforcement.

Market structures compared:
  perfect competition      many firms, identical product, free entry, P = MC,
                           zero long-run economic profit, efficient
  monopolistic competition many firms, differentiated, free entry, P > MC,
                           zero long-run profit, excess capacity
  oligopoly                few firms, interdependent, strategic, outcome ranges
                           from near-competitive to near-cartel
  monopoly                 one firm, blocked entry, P > MC, deadweight loss,
                           positive economic profit sustained
  monopsony                one buyer; symmetric analysis, depresses input prices
                           and quantities below competitive levels

Price discrimination lets a firm with market power capture more surplus by charging
different prices for the same good. It requires market power, the ability to segment
buyers by willingness to pay, and prevention of resale. First-degree charges each buyer
their maximum, second-degree offers a menu of quantity or quality tiers that induce
self-selection, and third-degree charges different groups different prices — student
discounts, regional pricing, advance-purchase airfares. Welfare effects are ambiguous
rather than simply negative: discrimination transfers surplus to the firm but can
increase total output, and it can make markets viable that uniform pricing would leave
unserved, which is the standard argument for tiered pharmaceutical pricing across
countries.

## Market Failure and Public Goods

Competitive markets are efficient under strong assumptions, and market failure is the
name for their systematic violation. Externalities occur when a transaction imposes
costs or confers benefits on third parties who are not party to it. Because those
effects do not enter the decision-maker's calculus, the market produces too much of
goods with negative externalities and too little of those with positive ones. Pollution
is the canonical negative case, education and vaccination the canonical positive ones.

The remedies each internalize the externality by different means. A Pigouvian tax set
equal to the marginal external cost makes the private cost equal the social cost, after
which the market outcome is efficient. Tradable permits cap the total quantity and let
the price emerge, which fixes the environmental outcome and lets abatement occur
wherever it is cheapest. The Coase theorem observes that with well-defined property
rights and negligible transaction costs, parties will bargain to an efficient outcome
regardless of who initially holds the right — a result whose practical importance lies
mostly in its conditions, since transaction costs are rarely negligible when the
affected parties are numerous and diffuse, which is exactly the situation for most
serious environmental problems.

Public goods are non-rival, meaning one person's consumption does not diminish
another's, and non-excludable, meaning non-payers cannot be prevented from benefiting.
National defence, basic research, and a lighthouse are standard examples. Non-
excludability creates free riding: each individual's contribution is negligible to the
outcome while costly to themselves, so voluntary provision falls short of the efficient
level. This is the central economic argument for public provision or subsidy, and it
applies with particular force to knowledge, which is perfectly non-rival — the marginal
cost of another person using an idea is zero — and which patent systems make artificially
excludable to restore the incentive to produce it, at the cost of underuse.

Market failures and standard responses:
  negative externality   Pigouvian tax, tradable permits, regulation, liability
  positive externality   subsidy, public provision, mandate
  public good            public provision funded by taxation; free-rider problem
  common-pool resource   rival but non-excludable; the tragedy of the commons.
                         Ostrom documented that community governance frequently
                         solves this without privatization or state control
  information asymmetry  adverse selection (hidden characteristics) and moral
                         hazard (hidden actions)
  market power           antitrust, price regulation of natural monopolies
  behavioural failure    default settings, disclosure rules, cooling-off periods

Information asymmetry produces two distinct failures worth keeping separate. Adverse
selection arises before a transaction when one party knows something relevant that the
other does not — Akerlof's used-car market, where buyers cannot distinguish quality, so
they offer an average price, so good cars are withdrawn, so average quality falls, in a
cycle that can unravel the market entirely. Insurance markets face the same dynamic,
which is the economic rationale for mandates and community rating. Moral hazard arises
after a transaction when one party's unobservable actions affect the other's payoff:
insured drivers take more risk, employees with fixed salaries exert less effort. The
remedies differ accordingly — signalling, screening, and warranties address adverse
selection, while deductibles, co-payments, monitoring, and performance-linked pay
address moral hazard.

## Macroeconomics, Money, and Trade

Gross domestic product measures the market value of final goods and services produced
within a country in a period, and its construction determines what it does and does not
capture. Intermediate goods are excluded to avoid double counting. Only final output
counts, which is why the expenditure identity Y = C + I + G + NX exhausts it. Non-market
production — household labour, volunteering — is excluded, as are environmental
degradation and the distribution of the output. GDP is a measure of market activity, and
treating it as a measure of welfare is a category error that its own designers warned
against.

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, reducing money's purchasing
power. Demand-pull inflation arises when spending outruns productive capacity;
cost-push arises from supply shocks such as an oil price rise; and expectations are
self-fulfilling, since workers and firms that expect inflation build it into wages and
prices. The quantity theory, MV = PY, is an identity that becomes a theory under the
assumption that velocity and output are stable — which holds poorly in the short run
and better over long horizons and large monetary expansions, which is why it explains
hyperinflations well and quarterly fluctuations badly. Moderate inflation is generally
preferred to zero because it permits real wage adjustment without nominal wage cuts,
which workers resist, and because it provides room to cut real interest rates below
zero in a downturn.

Unemployment is decomposed by cause, and the decomposition drives the policy response.
Frictional unemployment reflects the time taken to match workers to jobs and is
unavoidable and even productive. Structural unemployment reflects a mismatch between
worker skills or location and available jobs, and responds to training and mobility
policy rather than to demand stimulus. Cyclical unemployment reflects insufficient
aggregate demand and is what monetary and fiscal policy target. The natural rate is the
sum of the frictional and structural components, the level consistent with stable
inflation, and attempting to push unemployment below it through demand policy produces
accelerating inflation rather than sustained employment gains.

Macro and monetary reference:

Comparative advantage is the core result in trade and the most counterintuitive in the
discipline. A country benefits from specializing in goods where its opportunity cost is
lowest and trading for the rest, even if it is absolutely less productive at everything.
The gains come from reallocating production toward lower-opportunity-cost uses, so total
output rises and trade lets both parties consume beyond their own production
possibilities. The result is robust and is also frequently overstated in application:
it establishes that aggregate gains exist, not that everyone gains. Trade produces
winners and losers within a country, the losses concentrate in specific industries and
regions while the gains diffuse across all consumers, and the political economy of that
asymmetry — concentrated, visible losses against diffuse, invisible gains — explains
protectionism's durability far better than any failure to understand the theory.

Behavioral economics documents systematic departures from the rational-actor model and
has been absorbed into the mainstream rather than remaining a critique of it. Loss
aversion means losses weigh roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains, producing the
endowment effect and status quo bias. Anchoring makes irrelevant initial numbers
influence subsequent estimates. Hyperbolic discounting produces preference reversals
over time and explains under-saving. Framing changes choices without changing payoffs.
Mental accounting treats fungible money as if it were earmarked. The practical
consequence is that defaults are enormously powerful — automatic enrolment in retirement
plans raises participation dramatically without restricting any choice — which is the
foundation of choice architecture and its central design lesson: the question is never
whether to influence behaviour, since some default must exist, but which default to
choose.
