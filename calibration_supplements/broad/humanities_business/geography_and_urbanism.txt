## Spatial Thinking and the First Law

Geography studies the spatial organization of phenomena and the processes producing it, and its
distinctive contribution is the insistence that where something happens is part of what happens.
Two identical policies in different places produce different outcomes; two identical firms in
different locations face different costs; two identical populations in different environments
develop differently. Location is not a label attached to an observation but a variable interacting
with everything else.

The field's most quoted generalization is that everything is related to everything else, but near
things are more related than distant things. This spatial autocorrelation is nearly universal in
geographic data and it has a direct statistical consequence: observations at nearby locations are
not independent, so standard methods assuming independence understate uncertainty and overstate
significance. Any analysis of spatial data that has not addressed this is producing confidence
intervals that are too narrow, sometimes dramatically.

Scale is the other foundational concern, and it is not merely a matter of zoom level. Relationships
observed at one scale need not hold at another, and aggregating the same underlying data using
different boundaries produces different results — a phenomenon general enough to have a name and
consequential enough to have decided elections. Any statistic computed over administrative units
depends on those units, and the choice of unit is rarely neutral.

Q: A study finds a strong relationship between two variables using data aggregated by county, and
the relationship disappears when the same data is aggregated by census tract. This demonstrates:
A. One of the analyses is incorrect
B. The modifiable areal unit problem — results computed over aggregated spatial units depend on
   the boundaries chosen, so a relationship at one aggregation level may not exist at another
C. The data is unreliable
D. Counties are the correct unit
Reasoning: Aggregation averages within units and compares across them, so different boundaries
produce different variances and covariances. Both analyses can be computed correctly and disagree.
The remedy is to state the unit explicitly and, where possible, test sensitivity across several.
Answer: B.

## Maps, Projections, and Representation

A map is a model, and every map involves generalization, selection, symbolization, and projection —
each of which is a decision that shapes what the reader concludes. The claim that a map is
objective while a written account is interpretive does not survive examination, because the choices
embedded in a map are simply less visible.

Projection is the unavoidable distortion introduced by representing a curved surface on a flat one.
No projection preserves area, shape, distance, and direction simultaneously; each preserves some
properties and distorts others, and the appropriate choice depends entirely on what the map is for.
A conformal projection preserving local shape is right for navigation and wrong for comparing the
sizes of countries, which is the source of the most familiar complaint about world maps and of a
great deal of confused argument about it.

Thematic mapping introduces further decisions with strong effects. A choropleth map shading areas by
value is the most common form and is systematically misleading when the areas differ greatly in
population, because it gives visual weight proportional to land area rather than to the people the
data describes. Classification method — how continuous values are binned into classes — changes the
apparent pattern substantially, and presenting a single classification without noting that others
would look different is a common omission.

## Spatial Data and Analysis

Geographic information systems store, analyse, and display spatial data, and the field's technical
core is a small number of data models and operations. The vector model represents features as
points, lines, and polygons with attributes, which suits discrete objects — parcels, roads,
administrative units. The raster model represents space as a grid of cells with values, which suits
continuous surfaces — elevation, temperature, satellite imagery. Most real analyses use both and
convert between them, and each conversion loses something.

Coordinate reference systems are the most common source of errors, and the errors are frequently
silent. Data in different reference systems will overlay incorrectly, often by hundreds of metres,
and unless something visibly does not line up the mistake goes unnoticed. Distance and area
calculations in a geographic coordinate system, where the units are degrees, are simply wrong, and
the correct procedure is to project into an appropriate system for the region before measuring.

The analytical operations are combinable primitives. Overlay intersects layers to find where
conditions coincide. Buffering creates zones at a specified distance. Network analysis computes
routes and service areas along a connected graph, which gives travel-time accessibility rather than
straight-line distance. Interpolation estimates values between sample points, and the method chosen —
inverse distance weighting, spline, or a geostatistical approach modelling the spatial covariance —
embeds assumptions about the underlying surface that should be stated.

## Urban Form, Transport, and Land Use

Cities exist because proximity has value. Agglomeration economies — the productivity benefits of
firms and workers being close together — arise from labour market pooling, input sharing, and
knowledge spillovers, and they are why economic activity concentrates despite the resulting land
costs and congestion. The countervailing forces are those costs, and a city's size and structure
reflect the balance.

Urban form and transport determine each other in a feedback loop that is the central dynamic of
urban development. Transport technology sets the distance reachable within a tolerable commute,
which sets the extent of the developable area, which sets density, which determines what transport
modes are viable. Cities built before mechanized transport are compact and mixed; cities built
around automobiles are dispersed and separated by use, and each pattern makes the corresponding
transport mode efficient and the alternatives inefficient.

Induced demand is the finding most consistently ignored in transport policy. Adding road capacity
reduces travel cost, which increases travel, which restores congestion — an effect large enough that
the elasticity of vehicle travel to road capacity is close to one in the medium term. This does not
mean capacity is never worth adding; it means that congestion relief is not what it delivers, and
projects justified on that basis are justified on a prediction the evidence does not support.

## Population, Migration, and Regional Economies

Population geography studies distribution, composition, and change, and the fundamental
demographic accounting is simple: population change equals births minus deaths plus in-migration
minus out-migration. What makes it interesting is that these components have different geographies
and different time constants, and a region can be growing through migration while its natural
increase is negative.

The demographic transition describes the historical shift from high birth and death rates to low
ones, with a period of rapid growth in between as death rates fall before birth rates do. It has
occurred in every region that has developed economically, with varying speed, and its late stages
produce ageing populations and eventually decline. The regional consequence is that some places face
growth pressure and others face the very different problems of shrinkage — infrastructure sized for
a larger population, fiscal bases eroding, and services that lose viability below a threshold.

Migration responds to differences in opportunity, moderated by distance, by cost, and by existing
networks. Network effects are the most predictive single factor for where migrants go: previous
migrants lower the cost and risk for those following, so flows concentrate along established
corridors far more than opportunity differences alone would predict. This is why migration patterns
are persistent and why policy interventions aimed at redirecting them frequently fail.
