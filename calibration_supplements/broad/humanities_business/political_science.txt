## Institutions and Constitutional Design

Political science studies how collective decisions are made and enforced, and its most durable
finding is that institutions matter — the same population under different rules produces different
outcomes. Constitutional design is therefore not a formality but a determinant of what governments
can do, how power changes hands, and how conflict is resolved.

The separation of powers distributes authority among branches so that each constrains the others.
Its purpose is not efficiency, which it deliberately sacrifices, but the prevention of concentrated
power. The design tradeoff is explicit: a system with many veto points is difficult to capture and
also difficult to govern with, so it resists both tyranny and reform. Which of these properties
dominates in practice depends on how many veto players exist and how far apart their preferences
are.

Presidential and parliamentary systems differ in whether the executive derives its mandate
separately from the legislature or from it. The parliamentary arrangement fuses them, so an
executive that loses legislative support falls, and deadlock between them is structurally
impossible. The presidential arrangement separates them, which produces independent legitimacy and
also the possibility of a divided government in which neither branch can act and neither can be
removed. Neither is uniformly superior, and the comparative literature is largely about the
conditions under which each performs better.

## Electoral Systems and Representation

An electoral system converts votes into seats, and the conversion rule shapes the party system, the
incentives of candidates, and the character of governments. Duverger's observation captures the
strongest regularity: single-member districts decided by plurality tend toward two parties, because
voters abandon candidates who cannot win and donors abandon them first. Proportional systems
sustain more parties because a small vote share still yields representation.

The consequences run further than party count. Plurality systems produce single-party majority
governments more often, which makes accountability clear — one party governs and can be removed —
and representation less proportional. Proportional systems produce coalitions, which represent more
of the electorate and blur responsibility, since no party fully controls policy and voters cannot
easily assign blame.

District design under plurality rules creates its own problem, since the boundaries determine the
outcome. Drawing them to advantage a party is achieved by concentrating opposition voters into few
districts and spreading one's own supporters efficiently across many. Independent boundary
commissions are the standard remedy, and quantitative measures of the resulting distortion have
become central to the litigation over it.

Q: A country switches from proportional representation to single-member plurality districts. The
most predictable consequence is:
A. Higher turnout
B. A reduction in the effective number of parties, as voters and donors abandon candidates who
   cannot win a district, and single-party majority governments become more common
C. Better policy outcomes
D. No change in party structure
Reasoning: This is Duverger's regularity and it is among the most robust findings in comparative
politics. The mechanism is strategic behaviour by voters and funders under a winner-take-all rule.
Turnout and policy quality effects are ambiguous and contested. Answer: B.

## Political Behaviour and Public Opinion

Individual political behaviour departs substantially from the model of an informed citizen
evaluating policy. Most voters hold low levels of factual political knowledge, unstable positions on
specific issues, and stable attachments to parties or groups. Partisan identity functions more like
a social identity than like a policy conclusion, and it shapes the interpretation of information
rather than being shaped by it.

Motivated reasoning is the mechanism most responsible. People evaluate evidence more critically
when it threatens a valued conclusion and less critically when it supports one, and this asymmetry
is stronger among the more politically knowledgeable rather than weaker. The counterintuitive
consequence is that political sophistication increases the capacity to defend a prior position
rather than the propensity to update it, which is why correction of misinformation is so much
harder than it appears.

Turnout is a persistent puzzle for rational-choice accounts, since the probability of a single vote
changing an outcome is negligible while the cost of voting is not. The explanations that survive
appeal to expressive value, civic duty, social pressure, and habit rather than to instrumental
benefit, and the most effective interventions to increase turnout accordingly operate on social
mechanisms — a neighbour asking in person, or a message noting that turnout is observable.

## International Relations and Political Economy

International politics differs from domestic politics in lacking an authority above the actors,
and the major theoretical traditions are largely answers to what follows from that. Realism argues
that anarchy compels states to prioritize security, that power balances form, and that cooperation
is limited by concern for relative gains. Liberalism argues that institutions, trade, and domestic
regime type make cooperation durable. Constructivism argues that interests are not given by
structure but produced by shared ideas, so anarchy means what states make of it.

The security dilemma is the most useful specific concept. Measures one state takes for defence
appear threatening to another, which responds in kind, leaving both less secure while neither
intended aggression. It explains arms races and spirals of hostility without requiring anyone to
want conflict, and it identifies the conditions that mitigate it — distinguishable offensive and
defensive capabilities, and transparency.

Political economy studies the interaction of political and economic systems, and its central
questions concern why some countries develop and others do not. The institutional answer, now
dominant, holds that inclusive institutions — secure property rights, constraints on the executive,
broad participation — generate growth, while extractive institutions concentrating both political
and economic power do not, and that the two reinforce themselves once established.

## Methods and Inference

Political science faces a hard inferential environment: it cannot usually randomize, its units are
few, and the interesting variables are entangled. The methodological development of the field is
largely a response to that, and the credible-inference standard now applied is considerably more
demanding than it was a generation ago.

Case studies remain valuable when chosen for a reason. A case selected because it should not fit a
theory and does, or should fit and does not, carries much more evidential weight than a case
selected for convenience or for its outcome. Selecting on the dependent variable — studying only
revolutions to explain revolutions — is the classic error, because it makes it impossible to know
what distinguishes the cases from the ones that did not occur.

Quantitative work confronts confounding directly. Natural experiments exploit arbitrary variation —
a boundary, a threshold, a lottery — to approximate randomization. Difference-in-differences
compares changes rather than levels across treated and untreated groups. Regression discontinuity
uses the arbitrariness of a cutoff. Each has assumptions that can fail, and the credible papers are
the ones that argue for those assumptions rather than asserting them.
