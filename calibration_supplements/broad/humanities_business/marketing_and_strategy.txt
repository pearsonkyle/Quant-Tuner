## Strategic Positioning

Strategy is the choice of what to do and, more consequentially, what not to do. A strategy that
does not exclude anything is a statement of aspiration rather than a strategy, because it provides
no basis for deciding between opportunities — and the decisions strategy exists to inform are
precisely the ones where several attractive options compete for the same finite resources.

Competitive advantage is the ability to deliver more value or the same value at lower cost than
competitors, sustained over time. The sustainability is the hard part, since any advantage that
can be copied will be, so a durable position rests on something difficult to replicate: a scale
advantage in an industry with high fixed costs, a network effect where the product's value rises
with its user count, switching costs making customers reluctant to leave, a proprietary
technology or a protected position, or an accumulated capability that took years to build.

The generic strategies distinguish cost leadership from differentiation, and the failure mode is
attempting both without the structural basis for either. A cost leader wins on price and requires
genuine cost advantages from scale, process, or position rather than merely charging less. A
differentiator wins on distinctive value and requires customers to perceive and pay for the
difference. A firm doing neither well competes on nothing in particular, which is a weak position
that is nonetheless common because it is where a firm arrives by default when it does not choose.

Strategic concepts:
  strategy             the choice of what not to do; a strategy excluding nothing
                       is not one
  competitive advantage  more value or lower cost, sustained
  sustainability       requires something hard to replicate
  scale economies      fixed costs spread over volume
  network effects      value rises with users; direct and indirect
  switching costs      the cost to a customer of leaving
  cost leadership      structural cost advantage; not merely lower prices
  differentiation      distinctive value the customer perceives and pays for
  focus                serving a narrow segment better than generalists can
  stuck in the middle  neither cost nor differentiation advantage; the default
                       position when no choice is made
  value chain          the activities producing the offering; advantage arises in
                       specific activities rather than in general
  core competence      an accumulated capability difficult to acquire quickly
  five forces          rivalry, entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power;
                       the structural determinants of industry profitability

The five forces framework is worth applying because it explains why some industries are
persistently more profitable than others independent of how well individual firms are managed.
Intense rivalry, low entry barriers, close substitutes, concentrated buyers, and concentrated
suppliers each transfer value away from the industry's incumbents. An industry with all five
unfavourable is structurally unprofitable, and a well-run firm within it earns modest returns
while a mediocre firm in a favourable structure earns good ones — which is why the choice of
industry frequently matters more than the quality of execution within it.

Q: A company reduces prices to compete without any structural cost advantage over its
competitors. The most likely outcome is:
A. Sustained market share gain
B. Competitors match, margins fall across the industry, and relative positions are unchanged
C. Increased profitability from volume
D. Successful cost leadership
Reasoning: A price cut that competitors can match is matched, since matching is less costly than
losing share. The result is lower prices industry-wide with unchanged relative positions and
reduced profitability for everyone. Cost leadership requires a structural cost advantage that
permits profitable pricing competitors cannot match, which is a different thing from choosing to
charge less. Answer: B.

## Customer Understanding

Marketing begins with understanding what customers are trying to accomplish, and the framing
that most improves this is asking what job the customer is hiring the product to do. This shifts
attention from the product's attributes to the customer's situation and desired outcome, and it
reframes the competitive set — a product's real competition is whatever else the customer might
use to accomplish the same thing, which frequently includes doing nothing.

Customer research divides into what people say, what people do, and what people are. Stated
preferences from surveys and interviews are accessible and unreliable for prediction, because
people are poor at introspecting on their own decision processes and because the answer depends on
how the question is asked. Revealed preferences from actual behaviour are far more predictive and
say nothing about why. Observation in context reveals workarounds and unarticulated needs that
neither surveys nor behavioural data surface.

The most common research error is asking customers what they want rather than understanding what
problem they have. Customers are expert in their problems and are not designers, so their proposed
solutions are constrained by what they can imagine, and taking them literally produces
incremental improvements to existing solutions. Understanding the underlying problem leaves the
solution space open.

Customer understanding:
  jobs to be done      what the customer is trying to accomplish; reframes the
                       competitive set
  competitive set      whatever else accomplishes the job, including doing nothing
  stated preference    accessible, poorly predictive, question-dependent
  revealed preference  actual behaviour; predictive and silent about why
  contextual observation  reveals workarounds and unarticulated needs
  the solution trap    customers propose solutions bounded by what they can
                       imagine; understand the problem instead
  willingness to pay   what a customer would pay; measurable by conjoint analysis
                       and by price testing, not by asking directly
  customer journey     the sequence from awareness through purchase to use and
                       renewal; friction anywhere reduces the whole
  moments that matter  the few interactions disproportionately shaping perception
  churn drivers        why customers leave; usually knowable from behaviour before
                       the departure
  qualitative then quantitative  interviews generate hypotheses, surveys and
                       behaviour test them; either alone is incomplete

The sequencing of qualitative and quantitative research is worth stating as a discipline.
Qualitative work generates hypotheses about what matters and why, and it cannot establish
prevalence because the samples are small and purposive. Quantitative work measures prevalence and
tests relationships, and it cannot discover a factor nobody thought to ask about. Running
quantitative research first produces precise measurements of the wrong variables, and running
only qualitative produces compelling narratives of unknown generality.

## Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning

Segmentation divides a market into groups with different needs or behaviours, on the premise
that a single offering cannot serve heterogeneous needs well. The dimensions available are
demographic, geographic, psychographic, and behavioural, and the practical finding is that
behavioural and needs-based segmentation predicts far better than demographic segmentation, which
is convenient to measure and weakly related to what people actually want.

A useful segmentation satisfies several criteria. The segments must be measurable, so members can
be identified. They must be substantial enough to be worth serving. They must be accessible
through some channel. They must be differentiable, responding differently to the offering — a
segmentation whose segments respond identically has divided the market without informing
anything. And the differences must be actionable given what the firm can do.

Positioning is the place the offering occupies in the customer's mind relative to alternatives,
and it is defined by the comparison rather than by the product's attributes. The positioning
statement forces the elements to be explicit: for whom, what need, what category, what benefit,
and why that claim is credible. The last element is where most positioning fails, since a claim
without a reason to believe it is an assertion.

Segmentation and positioning:
  demographic          convenient, weakly predictive of preference
  geographic           relevant where the offering or the need varies by place
  psychographic        attitudes and values; harder to measure, more predictive
  behavioural          usage, occasion, benefit sought; usually the most
                       predictive
  needs-based          the segments customers themselves would recognize
  segmentation criteria  measurable, substantial, accessible, differentiable,
                       actionable
  differentiable       segments must respond differently, or the segmentation
                       informs nothing
  targeting            which segments to serve; the choice not to serve others is
                       the substance
  positioning          the place relative to alternatives, in the customer's mind
  frame of reference   the category the customer places the offering in;
                       determines what it is compared against
  point of difference  what it offers that alternatives do not
  reason to believe    why the claim is credible; the element most often missing
  perceptual map       positions on the dimensions customers use, which are
                       frequently not the dimensions the firm uses internally

The frame of reference deserves emphasis because it determines the comparison and therefore the
evaluation. A product positioned as a premium entrant in an established category is compared
against that category's leaders on their dimensions. The same product positioned as a new
category is compared against the status quo it replaces, on dimensions where it is stronger.
Choosing the frame is therefore choosing the competition, and it is one of the highest-leverage
marketing decisions available — and one made implicitly and badly more often than deliberately.

## Product, Pricing, and Channels

Pricing is the marketing decision with the most direct effect on profitability and receives the
least analytical attention in most organizations. A price change flows almost entirely to the
bottom line, so a small percentage improvement in realized price frequently exceeds what a large
improvement in volume or cost would deliver — and volume and cost receive far more management
attention.

The three pricing approaches rest on different information. Cost-plus pricing adds a margin to
cost, which is simple, ensures a margin on each sale, and ignores what the customer would pay and
what competitors charge — so it systematically underprices differentiated offerings and
overprices commodities. Competitive pricing anchors on competitors, which is easy and cedes
pricing authority. Value-based pricing sets the price by the value delivered to the customer,
which requires understanding that value and is the only approach capturing what differentiation
earned.

Price discrimination captures more of the available value by charging different prices to
customers with different willingness to pay. It requires the ability to segment, to prevent
resale, and to have some pricing power. Versioning offers different feature tiers that induce
self-selection. Bundling combines items so that customers with complementary valuations both find
the bundle worthwhile. Two-part pricing separates access from usage. Each is a mechanism for
approaching the value each customer places on the offering rather than charging everyone the same.

Product, pricing, and channels:
  cost-plus            simple; ignores value and competition, and misprices in
                       both directions
  competitive          anchors on rivals; cedes pricing authority
  value-based          the value delivered; the only approach capturing
                       differentiation
  price elasticity     how volume responds; determines whether a cut increases
                       revenue
  price discrimination  different prices by willingness to pay; needs segmentation
                       and resale prevention
  versioning           feature tiers inducing self-selection
  bundling             complementary valuations make the bundle worthwhile to both
  freemium             a free tier acquiring users, converting a fraction; works
                       where marginal cost is near zero and the free tier is
                       genuinely useful without cannibalizing the paid one
  penetration pricing  low initially to gain share; requires a reason the low
                       price becomes sustainable
  skimming             high initially, lowering over time; captures the
                       high-willingness segment first
  price anchoring      a reference price shaping perception; the presence of a
                       premium option raises the perceived value of the middle
  channel              how the offering reaches the customer; direct, retail,
                       partner, marketplace
  channel conflict     when channels compete for the same customer; requires
                       explicit terms rather than hoping
  disintermediation    removing an intermediary; requires assuming the functions
                       it performed rather than merely removing its margin

Disintermediation deserves a caution because the intermediary's margin is frequently mistaken
for pure extraction. An intermediary typically performs functions — inventory holding, credit,
demand aggregation, local presence, returns handling, discovery — and removing it means either
assuming those functions or losing them. A direct model that captures the margin and does not
provide the functions has traded a cost for a set of capability gaps, which frequently cost more
than the margin was worth.

## Growth, Brand, and Measurement

Growth arises from acquiring customers, retaining them longer, or increasing revenue per
customer, and the relative leverage varies enormously by business. For a subscription business
with meaningful churn, retention improvements compound and typically dominate: reducing monthly
churn from five percent to three percent extends the average customer lifetime by two thirds and
raises lifetime value proportionally, which is usually a larger effect than any plausible
acquisition improvement.

The relationship between acquisition cost and lifetime value is the constraint governing whether
growth is viable. Acquiring a customer for more than they will contribute destroys value however
fast it grows the top line, and the ratio of lifetime value to acquisition cost is the standard
health metric, with the payback period — how long until the acquisition cost is recovered —
determining the working capital required to grow. A business with excellent unit economics and a
long payback period can still fail from running out of cash.

Brand is the accumulated set of associations attached to a name, and its economic function is to
reduce the customer's perceived risk and search cost. A strong brand permits a price premium,
raises the response to marketing, and provides some resilience to competitive attack and to
occasional failures. It is built over years through consistent delivery and consistent
communication, and it is damaged far faster than it is built, which is the asymmetry that makes
brand a real asset requiring protection.

Growth, brand, and measurement:
  acquisition          new customers; the most visible and often least efficient
                       lever
  retention            keeping customers; compounds, and usually dominates in
                       subscription businesses
  expansion            more revenue per customer; frequently the cheapest growth
  lifetime value       the discounted contribution over the relationship
  acquisition cost     fully loaded, including the sales and marketing overhead
  LTV to CAC ratio     the standard health measure
  payback period       determines the working capital growth requires
  cohort analysis      track groups by acquisition period; separates changes in
                       the business from changes in mix, which aggregate metrics
                       cannot
  churn                voluntary and involuntary; involuntary churn from failed
                       payments is frequently a large and easily reduced share
  net revenue retention  expansion minus churn within a cohort; above 100% means
                       growth without any new customers
  brand equity         the premium and the response lift attributable to the brand
  attribution          which touchpoints deserve credit; last-click systematically
                       overcredits the final step and undercredits discovery
  incrementality       whether the marketing caused the outcome; the only measure
                       that matters and the one attribution models do not provide
  marketing mix modelling  aggregate regression estimating channel contribution;
                       useful where individual tracking is unavailable
  geo experiments      randomize by market; the practical route to incrementality
                       at scale

Attribution deserves the closing emphasis because it is where marketing measurement is most
systematically wrong. Attribution models allocate credit among the touchpoints a converting
customer encountered, which answers a correlational question — what did converters see — and not
the causal one, which is whether the marketing caused conversions that would not otherwise have
occurred. A channel that reaches people who were already going to buy receives high attributed
credit and produces no incremental revenue, and search advertising on a firm's own brand name is
the canonical example. The only reliable measurement is experimental: withhold the spend from a
randomized set of markets or users and measure the difference. It is more work than reading an
attribution report, it frequently reveals that a well-attributed channel contributes little, and
it is the difference between measuring marketing and measuring the customers marketing happened
to touch.
