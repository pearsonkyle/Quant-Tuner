## Pitch, Intervals, and Tuning

Pitch is perceived frequency, and the perceptual system organizes it logarithmically: equal ratios
of frequency are heard as equal musical distances. Doubling the frequency gives the octave, and two
pitches an octave apart are heard as so similar that most musical systems treat them as the same
note class. This octave equivalence is close to a cross-cultural universal and it is the foundation
on which everything else in pitch organization rests.

Intervals are ratios, and the simplest ratios sound most consonant because their harmonic series
overlap most. The perfect fifth is three to two, the perfect fourth four to three, the major third
five to four. Their consonance is not a cultural convention but a consequence of how the auditory
system processes coincident harmonics, though which intervals a musical culture privileges and how
it treats dissonance certainly is cultural.

Tuning systems exist because these simple ratios cannot all be satisfied simultaneously. Stacking
twelve perfect fifths does not arrive exactly at seven octaves — it overshoots by a small interval
called the Pythagorean comma — so any system with twelve fixed pitches must distribute that error
somewhere. Equal temperament divides the octave into twelve identical steps, making every key
equally usable and every interval except the octave slightly out of tune. Historical temperaments
distributed the error unevenly, which made keys sound characteristically different, and that
difference is what composers writing before equal temperament were exploiting.

Q: Why can a keyboard tuned in just intonation not modulate freely between keys?
A. The instrument is defective
B. Just intonation fixes pure ratios relative to one tonic; in a distant key those same fixed
   pitches produce badly mistuned intervals, because pure ratios cannot be maintained
   simultaneously for all keys with twelve fixed pitches
C. Modulation was not invented
D. The keys are too far apart
Reasoning: Pure tuning is tonic-relative. A fixed-pitch instrument tuned pure for one key has
intervals in other keys that deviate audibly, including the notorious wolf fifth. Temperament
distributes that error so all keys are usable, which is the compromise that enabled freely
modulating music. Answer: B.

## Scales, Modes, and Tonality

A scale is a selection of pitches from the available continuum, and the near-universal preference
for five to seven notes per octave reflects a limit on how many distinct categories are usefully
distinguishable. The specific pattern of steps determines the scale's character, and the same
collection of pitches starting from different notes produces different modes with markedly different
qualities.

The major scale's characteristic feature is the half step between its seventh and eighth degrees,
which creates a strong pull toward the tonic. That leading tone is much of what makes major-mode
music feel directed, and the natural minor scale's lack of it is why the harmonic minor — which
raises the seventh degree specifically to restore that pull — exists as a modification rather than
as a scale in its own right.

Tonality is the organization of music around a central pitch, established by emphasis, by the
harmonic relationships that lead toward it, and by placement at structural points. It is a
perceptual reality, not merely a theoretical construct: listeners without training reliably identify
whether a melody has ended on its tonic. The extent to which a piece establishes, departs from, and
returns to a tonal centre is much of what constitutes its large-scale structure.

## Harmony and Voice Leading

Harmony is the vertical dimension, and functional harmony organizes chords by their role relative
to the tonic. The tonic function is stable, the dominant function is unstable and resolves to the
tonic, and the predominant function prepares the dominant. Most common-practice harmonic motion is
some elaboration of the progression from tonic through predominant to dominant and back, and
recognizing that skeleton in a complicated passage is most of what harmonic analysis does.

The dominant's pull comes from two elements. Its root lies a fifth above the tonic, the strongest
root relationship, and it contains the leading tone that resolves upward by half step. Adding the
seventh introduces a second tendency tone resolving downward, so a dominant seventh chord has two
voices pulling toward specific destinations, which is why its resolution feels close to obligatory.

Voice leading governs how individual lines move between chords, and its rules are descriptions of
what produces independent-sounding parts rather than arbitrary prohibitions. Small motion, common
tones retained, tendency tones resolved, and contrary motion between outer voices produce a texture
where the parts are heard as separate lines. The prohibitions on parallel fifths and octaves exist
because those intervals fuse perceptually, momentarily collapsing two independent voices into one —
which is a defect only if independence is the goal, and is exactly why parallel motion is used
deliberately in styles seeking a blended sound.

## Rhythm, Meter, and Form

Rhythm is the temporal organization of events and meter is the framework of regularly recurring
strong and weak beats against which rhythm is perceived. The distinction matters because a rhythmic
figure derives its effect from its position in the metric framework — the same durations placed
differently against the beat produce entirely different musical results.

Syncopation is the deliberate contradiction of the metric framework, placing accents where the meter
does not expect them. Its effect depends entirely on the listener holding the underlying meter, so a
syncopated passage that destroys the sense of meter stops being syncopated and becomes merely
irregular. This is why syncopation in practice is usually supported by another layer maintaining the
pulse.

Musical form is the organization of material in time, and the recurring devices are few: statement,
repetition, variation, contrast, and return. Binary and ternary forms combine these at small scale.
Sonata form organizes a large movement as an exposition establishing two key areas, a development
destabilizing them, and a recapitulation returning with the tonal conflict resolved. Its logic is
tonal rather than thematic, which is the point most often missed — the drama is in the key
relationships and the themes are what makes it audible.

## Timbre, Acoustics, and Beyond Common Practice

Timbre is what distinguishes two instruments playing the same pitch at the same loudness, and it is
determined by the spectral content and, crucially, by the attack transient. The onset of a note
carries much of the identifying information, which is demonstrable by removing it: recordings with
attacks edited out become surprisingly difficult to identify by instrument.

The physical acoustics of instruments explain their characteristic behaviour. A string or air column
vibrating in a mode series produces harmonics at integer multiples of the fundamental, which is why
such instruments have definite pitch. A struck plate or membrane produces inharmonic partials,
which is why most percussion has indefinite pitch, and the exceptions — timpani, tuned percussion —
are instruments whose shape has been engineered to bring some partials into harmonic relationships.

Twentieth-century and later practice expanded beyond the common-practice framework in several
directions, and the analytical tools followed. Extended tonality stretched functional harmony until
its centre became ambiguous. Serial techniques organized pitch by ordered sets rather than by
hierarchy, which required set-theoretic analysis rather than functional. Spectral approaches derived
harmony from the acoustic spectrum itself. Popular and vernacular musics developed harmonic
practices — modal progressions, loop-based structures, groove as the organizing principle — that
functional analysis describes poorly and that need their own vocabulary rather than being treated as
deficient versions of common practice.
