## Decision Framing

The framing of a decision determines its outcome more than the analysis applied within the frame,
and the most common failure is answering a well-posed version of the wrong question. A decision
framed as whether to do a specific thing admits only yes or no; the same situation framed as how
to achieve an underlying objective admits the alternatives that were never considered.

The distinction between a good decision and a good outcome is the discipline that makes decision
quality assessable. A decision is made with the information available at the time; the outcome
depends additionally on chance and on factors outside the decision-maker's control. A good decision
can produce a bad outcome and a bad decision can produce a good one, and evaluating decisions by
their outcomes — resulting — systematically rewards luck and punishes sound reasoning under
uncertainty.

This matters organizationally because it determines what is learned from experience. An
organization that reviews decisions by their outcomes teaches its people to avoid decisions with
variance rather than decisions with poor expected value, and it teaches them to construct
justifications after the fact. Reviewing the process — what was known, what alternatives were
considered, how the uncertainty was assessed — is what produces improvement.

Q: A project has consumed two years and substantial budget. Completing it requires another six
months and would deliver value below the remaining cost. The correct decision is:
A. Complete it, since abandoning wastes the two years
B. Abandon it; the two years are sunk and irrelevant, and the forward comparison is the remaining
   cost against the remaining value
C. Complete it to preserve credibility
D. Reduce scope and complete
Reasoning: The two years are spent regardless of what is decided now, so they are identical under
both options and cannot distinguish between them. The comparison is the remaining six months of
cost against the value delivered, which is unfavourable. Option D may be correct if the reduced
scope changes the forward economics, which is a different calculation. Answer: B.

## Probability, Expected Value, and Biases

Reasoning under uncertainty requires quantifying it, and the most useful discipline is stating
probabilities explicitly rather than using verbal terms. Studies of verbal probability expressions
consistently find enormous variation in how they are interpreted — "likely" spans roughly
fifty to ninety percent across readers, and "a real possibility" spans nearly the whole range — so a
document using them communicates far less than its author believes.

Expected value combines outcomes with their probabilities and is the correct basis for repeated
decisions. Its limitation is that it is a long-run average, and a decision made once with a small
probability of ruin should not be evaluated on expected value alone — a bet with positive expected
value and a ten percent chance of bankruptcy is a bad bet for someone who cannot afford
bankruptcy. Utility rather than money is the quantity to maximize, and utility is concave in
wealth, which is what makes risk aversion rational rather than a bias.

Base rate neglect is the most consequential probabilistic error. Given a diagnostic test result,
people reason from the test's accuracy and ignore the prior prevalence, which produces confident
conclusions that are wrong by an order of magnitude. The corrective is to reason in frequencies
rather than probabilities — out of ten thousand people, how many have the condition, how many test
positive, and what fraction of those actually have it — which people handle far more reliably.

Calibration deserves emphasis because it is measurable and trainable and is almost never
practised. A forecaster who assigns seventy percent to a set of claims should be right about
seventy percent of the time, and most people are substantially overconfident — their ninety
percent intervals contain the truth perhaps fifty percent of the time. Practice with prompt
feedback improves this measurably within a few sessions, and an organization whose people are
calibrated can aggregate their estimates meaningfully, which an organization of overconfident
estimators cannot.

## Group Decisions

Groups can decide better than individuals and frequently decide worse, and which occurs depends on
process. The advantage available is aggregation of independent information and perspectives; the
mechanisms destroying it are the social dynamics that make the judgments non-independent.

Anchoring on the first opinion voiced is the most mechanical failure. Whoever speaks first sets a
reference point, and subsequent contributions cluster around it, so the group's apparent consensus
reflects the ordering rather than the balance of views. Collecting written independent judgments
before any discussion eliminates it and takes minutes.

Status effects compound this. When a senior person states a view early, disagreement carries a
cost and the information held by others does not surface. The remedy is procedural — the most
senior person speaks last — and it is effective and requires the senior person to enforce it, since
the group will defer to them regardless of instruction if they volunteer a view.

Groupthink is the failure of a cohesive group under pressure to converge on a position without
adequate examination, and its signature is the absence of dissent rather than its resolution.
Assigning someone to argue the opposing case makes dissent a role rather than a personal position,
which removes the social cost, and the technique works even when everyone knows the assignment is
artificial.

Shared information bias deserves specific attention because it defeats the purpose of assembling a
group. Discussion disproportionately covers information that several members already hold, because
each mention is recognized and validated, while information held by only one person is mentioned
once and not taken up. The group therefore converges on what was already common knowledge and
neglects the unique information that motivated bringing several people together. Structuring the
discussion to explicitly solicit what each person knows that others may not is the corrective.

## Negotiation Preparation and Process

Preparation determines negotiation outcomes more than technique, and the single most important
preparation is knowing the alternative to agreement. The best alternative to a negotiated agreement
defines the walk-away point: any agreement better than it is worth accepting and any agreement
worse is not. A party who has not determined theirs cannot know when to accept and negotiates from
anxiety rather than from position.

Improving the alternative improves the negotiating position more than any tactic. Developing a
second supplier, a competing offer, or an internal capability changes what agreement is necessary,
and it does so before the negotiation begins. This is why preparation time spent on alternatives
returns more than preparation time spent on arguments.

The distinction between positions and interests is what makes value creation possible. A position
is what someone says they want; an interest is why they want it. Two parties with incompatible
positions frequently have compatible interests, and the classic illustration — two people wanting
the same orange, one for the juice and one for the peel — is trite and captures the mechanism
exactly. Asking why, and understanding the constraint or objective behind a demand, is what
surfaces the trades that make both parties better off.

Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers deserve emphasis because they solve a real information
problem. Both parties want to know the other's priorities and neither wants to disclose them, since
disclosure appears to weaken their position. Presenting two or three packages of equal value to
oneself but different composition — more of one thing and less of another — and observing which the
other party prefers reveals their relative priorities without any disclosure of one's own
valuations. It also signals flexibility and seriousness, and it moves the conversation from
haggling over one number to comparing structures.

Then understand what is behind their number. Ask what drives it. A price 40% above your budget may

commitment, an assumption about your requirements that is not actually necessary, or an opening
position with substantial room. These require entirely different responses, and you cannot tell
which without asking.

immediately with a number anchors it in return and locks both parties into a range before either
understands the structure. Responding with questions rather than a number is not weakness — it
delays the anchoring until you have information, and it signals that you are solving a problem
rather than haggling.

One caution from the assessment: they appear to know your timeline, which is leverage. If you have
disclosed urgency, that is difficult to retract, and the counter is developing an alternative
quickly enough that the timeline binds them as much as you.

## Difficult Conversations and Deep Uncertainty

Difficult conversations fail predictably, and the structure that makes them productive separates
three things that get conflated. The substance is what happened and what should happen. The
feelings are the emotional content, which is present whether or not it is acknowledged and which
distorts the substance when suppressed. The identity content is what the conversation implies
about each person's competence or character, and it is why conversations that appear to be about a
small matter become disproportionate.

Separating the observable from the interpretation is the technique that most reduces defensiveness.
Stating what was observed — a deadline was missed, a message was not answered, a decision was made
without consultation — is factual and disputable only on the facts. Stating the interpretation — you
do not respect this project — is an attribution the other person will contest, and the conversation
becomes about whether the attribution is fair rather than about the observable problem.

Decisions under deep uncertainty, where the probabilities themselves are unknown, require
different approaches from decisions under risk. Expected value calculations require probabilities;
when those are not available, the useful criteria change. Robustness asks which option performs
acceptably across the plausible range of futures rather than optimally in the expected one.
Flexibility asks which option preserves the ability to adjust as information arrives. And staging
asks whether the decision can be broken into steps with information gathered between them.

Preferring reversible options under deep uncertainty deserves the closing emphasis because it
inverts the ordinary optimization. Where probabilities are known, the expected value calculation is
correct and a reversible option costing more has to justify the difference. Where the probabilities
are genuinely unknown, the expected value calculation cannot be performed honestly, and the option
preserving the ability to change course as information arrives has value that the calculation
cannot express. Paying something for reversibility is therefore rational rather than timid, and
the amount worth paying rises with how uncertain the situation genuinely is — which is a judgment
that should be made explicitly rather than smuggled into a probability estimate that nobody
believes.
