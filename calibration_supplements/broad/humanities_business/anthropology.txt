## The Discipline and Its Method

Anthropology studies human beings comparatively and holistically, and it is distinguished from
neighbouring disciplines less by its subject than by its method and its scope. The comparative
commitment means that no society is treated as the default against which others are variations; the
holistic commitment means that economy, kinship, religion, and politics are studied as an
interlocking whole rather than as separate domains.

The traditional four-field structure covers cultural anthropology, which studies contemporary human
societies; archaeology, which studies past societies through material remains; biological
anthropology, which studies human evolution and variation; and linguistic anthropology, which
studies language in its social context. The integration is the point — questions about human
origins, social organization, and meaning are pursued with whichever evidence bears on them.

Ethnography is the characteristic method: extended participant observation in which the researcher
lives within a community, participates in daily life, and records what people do alongside what
they say about it. The duration matters because the gap between stated norms and actual practice is
usually invisible to a short visit, and because trust sufficient for candid conversation takes time
to establish. Its epistemic strength is access to context that no survey instrument reaches; its
weakness is that it is unrepeatable and that the observer is part of the situation observed.

  biological anthropology  human evolution and variation

Q: A researcher spends two weeks in a community and reports that its stated marriage rules are
strictly followed. An ethnographer working there for two years finds frequent exceptions. This
illustrates:
A. The first researcher was dishonest
B. Stated norms and actual practice diverge routinely, and the divergence is visible only with
   enough time and trust for people to discuss what actually happens rather than what should
C. The community changed
D. Rules do not exist
Reasoning: People describe their normative system when asked, and the exceptions are neither
volunteered to strangers nor visible in a short visit. The gap between norm and practice is a
standard finding and the reason extended fieldwork is the method rather than interviewing.
Answer: B.

## Kinship and Social Organization

Kinship was anthropology's first systematic subject because in societies without formal state
institutions, kinship organizes production, residence, inheritance, alliance, and authority.
Studying it revealed the discipline's foundational insight: the categories that appear natural —
who counts as a relative, what a family is, what obligations follow from a relationship — vary
enormously and are cultural constructions built on a biological substrate rather than readings of
it.

Descent systems trace group membership through one line or both. Patrilineal systems trace through
fathers, matrilineal through mothers, and the latter is not the mirror image of the former, because
authority in matrilineal societies commonly rests with a woman's brother rather than with her
husband, which produces a distinctive tension between a man's obligations to his sister's children
and to his own. Bilateral systems trace through both and produce overlapping rather than bounded
groups, which is the arrangement most familiar to industrialized societies and is a minority
pattern globally.

Marriage systems regulate alliance between groups as much as they regulate individual pairing.
Rules of exogamy require marriage outside a defined group, which builds ties between groups and is
the more common arrangement; endogamy requires marriage within one, which concentrates resources
and maintains boundaries. Bridewealth, dowry, and bride service transfer resources in different
directions and correlate with different economic and residential arrangements. The analytic point
is that these are systems of exchange between groups, and interpreting them as individual romantic
choice misses what they organize.

  the general lesson   what looks like a natural category is usually a

                       the discipline's most transferable contribution

## Economic and Political Anthropology

Economic anthropology examines how societies organize production, distribution, and consumption,
and its central finding is that market exchange is one mode among several rather than the
underlying reality of which others are variants. Reciprocity — the mutual giving of gifts creating
and sustaining obligation — organizes much exchange in many societies. Redistribution collects
resources centrally and disperses them, which is what chiefdoms and states do through tribute and
taxation. Market exchange with impersonal prices coexists with both.

The classic analysis of gift exchange established that the gift is not the opposite of the
commodity but an alternative structure with its own logic. A gift creates an obligation to
reciprocate at a later time and in a manner that is not exactly equivalent, and the delay and
inexactness are constitutive rather than incidental: precise immediate repayment would cancel the
relationship rather than sustain it. This analysis illuminates a great deal of exchange in market
societies too, where gift and commodity logics coexist and confusing them causes real social
offence.

Political anthropology examines authority in societies with and without states. The classic typology
runs from bands, small and egalitarian with leadership by influence rather than office, through
tribes and chiefdoms with inherited rank and redistribution, to states with formal institutions,
specialized coercive capacity, and social stratification. The typology is a useful heuristic and it
is not an evolutionary sequence — societies have moved in both directions, and the assumption of
unidirectional progression was one of the discipline's early errors.

Economic and political anthropology:

  the coexistence      gift and commodity logics both operate in market societies
  substantivism        the economy embedded in social relations
  formalism            economic reasoning as universally applicable

  informal economy     the large fraction of economic activity outside formal
                       measurement; ethnography sees it where statistics do not

## Symbolic Systems, Ritual, and Meaning

Symbolic anthropology treats culture as a system of meanings that people use to interpret
experience and act in the world. The interpretive approach reads social action as a text — the
analytic task being to understand what a practice means to those performing it — and it makes
description rather than causal explanation the primary goal, which is a substantial methodological
commitment and a contested one within the discipline.

Ritual is the most studied category because it makes implicit structure explicit. Rites of passage
have a consistent three-part structure across societies: separation from the previous status,
a liminal period outside ordinary categories, and reincorporation with a new status. The liminal
phase is where the interesting things happen — ordinary rules are suspended, hierarchy is inverted
or dissolved, and the participants form intense bonds. Recognizing this structure explains a great
deal about initiation, pilgrimage, and even organizational practices in modern institutions.

Classification systems are the other major theme. Anomalous things — those that do not fit the
categories a society uses to organize the world — attract disproportionate ritual attention, and
purity and pollution rules frequently track category violation rather than hygiene. The general
argument that dirt is matter out of place, that pollution beliefs are about maintaining conceptual
order, illuminates rules that appear arbitrary when read as practical measures and become coherent
when read as boundary maintenance.

Symbolic systems:
  culture as meaning   a system for interpreting experience
  interpretive approach  reading action as a text
  thick description    an account including the meaning, not just the behaviour
  ritual               makes implicit structure explicit
  rites of passage     separation, liminality, reincorporation
  liminality           outside ordinary categories; rules suspended
  communitas           the intense undifferentiated bonding of the liminal phase
  the modern application  initiation practices in institutions follow the same

  classification       the categories organizing the world
  anomaly              what does not fit; attracts ritual attention
  purity and pollution  frequently about category maintenance rather than hygiene
  myth                 narrative doing structural work; not failed history
  taboo                prohibitions marking boundaries

                       something else
  material culture     objects carrying meaning; what people make and keep is
                       evidence about what they think

## Archaeology and Applied Practice

Archaeology recovers past societies from material remains, and its central methodological problem is
inference from a heavily filtered record. What survives is biased toward the durable, the buried,
and the deliberately deposited, so an assemblage of stone and ceramic from a society that also used
wood, fibre, and leather represents a small and unrepresentative fraction of its material life.
Reasoning from what is present to what existed requires explicit models of preservation and
deposition.

Context is the primary evidence and it is destroyed by excavation, which makes archaeology unusual
among sciences in that its method consumes its subject. An artifact removed without its
stratigraphic position, associations, and surroundings recorded has lost most of its information
value, which is why looted objects are archaeologically nearly worthless regardless of their
condition and why recording standards are so exacting.

Applied anthropology brings the discipline's methods to practical problems, and its value comes
from the same source as its academic value: attention to what people actually do, in context, over
time. Development projects that fail despite technical soundness usually fail for social reasons
that ethnographic assessment would have identified. Design and technology research adopted
ethnographic methods for the same reason — observing use in context reveals requirements that
interviews and surveys do not, because people cannot report what they do not consciously notice.
