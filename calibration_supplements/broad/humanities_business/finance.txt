## Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow, because today's dollar can be
invested. This is the foundational principle of finance, and essentially every valuation
technique is an elaboration of it. Discounting converts future cash flows to present value
by dividing by one plus the discount rate raised to the number of periods; compounding runs
the same arithmetic forward.

The discount rate is where the judgment lives. It should reflect the opportunity cost of
capital — the return available on an investment of comparable risk — so a riskier cash flow
is discounted at a higher rate and is worth less today. Two analysts valuing the same
project will agree on the cash flow arithmetic and disagree on the rate, and the rate
dominates the answer for long-dated flows: at a ten percent rate, a dollar in thirty years
is worth six cents, while at five percent it is worth twenty-three cents, a fourfold
difference from a five-point disagreement.

Compounding frequency matters more than intuition suggests. An annual rate compounded more
frequently yields a higher effective return, converging to the continuous limit as the
frequency increases. This is why the stated nominal rate and the effective annual rate
differ, why credit products must disclose an annual percentage rate on a comparable basis,
and why the distinction between a rate and a yield is worth keeping precise.

Time value formulas:
  future value          FV = PV (1 + r)^n
  present value         PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
  effective annual rate EAR = (1 + r/m)^m - 1 for m compounding periods
  continuous compound   FV = PV e^(rt)
  annuity PV            PV = C [1 - (1+r)^-n] / r
  perpetuity            PV = C / r
  growing perpetuity    PV = C / (r - g),  requires r > g
  net present value     sum of discounted cash flows minus initial investment
  internal rate         the rate making NPV zero
  rule of 72            doubling time ≈ 72 / percentage rate

Net present value is the correct capital budgeting criterion and internal rate of return is
the more popular one, which is a persistent problem. IRR is intuitive because it is a
percentage, and it has genuine defects: it can have multiple values when cash flows change
sign more than once, it implicitly assumes reinvestment at the IRR itself, and it ranks
mutually exclusive projects incorrectly when they differ in scale or timing. A small
project with a fifty percent return may create less value than a large one with a fifteen
percent return, and only NPV expresses that. Where both are reported, NPV governs.

Q: A project requires 100,000 now and returns 40,000 per year for three years. At a 10%
discount rate, should it be accepted?
A. Yes, total returns of 120,000 exceed the 100,000 cost
B. No, the NPV is negative
C. Yes, the NPV is about -520
D. Cannot be determined without the IRR
Reasoning: The present value of a three-year annuity of 40,000 at 10% is 40,000 x [1 -
1.1^-3]/0.1 = 40,000 x 2.4869 = 99,474. Subtracting the 100,000 cost gives an NPV of about
-526. Undiscounted totals are the classic error; the timing of the flows is exactly what
matters. Answer: B.

## Risk, Return, and Portfolio Theory

Risk in finance is conventionally measured as the volatility of returns, usually their
standard deviation. This is a convenient and incomplete definition: it treats upside and
downside deviation symmetrically, and it assumes a distribution well described by its
first two moments, which financial returns are not — they exhibit fat tails, so extreme
moves occur far more often than a normal distribution predicts.

The central insight of portfolio theory is that risk is not additive. Combining assets
whose returns are imperfectly correlated produces a portfolio whose volatility is less
than the weighted average of the components', because their fluctuations partially offset.
Diversification therefore reduces risk without a corresponding reduction in expected
return, which is as close to a free lunch as finance offers. The benefit depends entirely
on correlation: combining two assets with correlation of one achieves nothing, and lower
correlation achieves more.

Diversification eliminates idiosyncratic risk — the component specific to individual
assets — and cannot eliminate systematic risk, the component driven by common factors
affecting everything. This division is what underpins asset pricing: since idiosyncratic
risk can be diversified away at no cost, the market does not compensate investors for
bearing it, and expected return should depend only on exposure to systematic risk. Beta
measures that exposure relative to the market, and the capital asset pricing model prices
an asset's expected return as the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium.

Risk and portfolio quantities:
  expected return        probability-weighted average outcome
  volatility             standard deviation of returns; annualize by multiplying
                         by the square root of periods per year
  covariance             how two assets move together
  correlation            covariance normalized to [-1, 1]
  portfolio variance     w' Sigma w;  the cross terms are where diversification
                         acts
  beta                   covariance with the market divided by market variance
  CAPM                   E[R] = R_f + beta (E[R_m] - R_f)
  Sharpe ratio           (return - risk-free) / volatility; return per unit risk
  Sortino ratio          same, using downside deviation only
  efficient frontier     maximum return for each level of risk
  value at risk          loss threshold exceeded with probability alpha
  expected shortfall     average loss beyond VaR; coherent where VaR is not

Value at risk deserves a specific warning because it is widely used and structurally
misleading. It states a loss level that will not be exceeded with some confidence, and it
says nothing whatever about how bad things are in the tail beyond that level. Two portfolios
with identical VaR can have entirely different exposures to catastrophic loss. VaR also
fails subadditivity — the VaR of a combined portfolio can exceed the sum of its parts,
which is incoherent for a risk measure and can perversely penalize diversification. Expected
shortfall, the average loss conditional on exceeding the threshold, fixes both problems and
has become the regulatory standard.

The equity risk premium — the excess return of stocks over risk-free assets — has averaged
roughly four to six percent historically, and both the number and its interpretation are
contested. Historical estimates suffer survivorship bias, since the markets with long
continuous histories are the ones that were not destroyed by war or expropriation, and the
sample of independent long-horizon observations is small. Forward-looking estimates from
dividend yields and earnings growth generally produce lower figures. Anyone using a
historical average as a forward expectation should treat the uncertainty as very large.

## Valuation

Valuation asks what an asset is worth, and there are three broad approaches whose
disagreements are usually more informative than any one answer.

Discounted cash flow valuation projects the cash the asset will generate and discounts
them at the appropriate rate. It is the most theoretically sound approach and the most
sensitive to assumptions, particularly the terminal value, which typically accounts for
the majority of the total and depends on a perpetual growth rate applied beyond the
forecast horizon. Small changes in that growth rate or in the discount rate produce large
changes in the answer, so a DCF should always be presented as a range across a sensitivity
grid rather than as a point estimate. A perpetual growth rate exceeding long-run economic
growth is not defensible, since it implies the company eventually becomes the entire
economy.

Relative valuation compares against similar assets using multiples — price to earnings,
enterprise value to EBITDA, price to book, price to sales. It is fast, reflects current
market sentiment, and is entirely dependent on the comparables being genuinely comparable
and on the market pricing them sensibly. A multiple embeds assumptions about growth, risk,
and capital intensity, so comparing companies that differ on those dimensions produces
misleading conclusions dressed up as arithmetic.

Asset-based valuation sums the value of the underlying assets less liabilities. It provides
a floor for asset-heavy businesses and understates any business whose value derives from
intangibles, relationships, or growth prospects, which is most businesses.

Valuation methods and their use:
  DCF                   intrinsic value from projected cash flows; sensitive to
                        terminal assumptions
  free cash flow        FCF = operating cash flow - capital expenditure; what
                        actually accrues to investors
  WACC                  weighted cost of debt and equity; the standard firm
                        discount rate
  terminal value        Gordon growth or exit multiple; usually the majority of a
                        DCF's total
  P/E                   price per unit of earnings; meaningless for loss-makers,
                        distorted by leverage and accounting
  EV/EBITDA             capital-structure neutral; better for cross-company
                        comparison
  P/B                   relevant for financial institutions with marked assets
  dividend discount     for stable dividend payers; the Gordon model
  precedent transactions  what acquirers actually paid; includes a control premium
  sum of the parts      value segments separately; useful for conglomerates

Enterprise value versus equity value is the distinction most often mishandled. Enterprise
value is the value of the operating business regardless of how it is financed — market
capitalization plus net debt — and it is what pairs correctly with pre-interest measures
such as EBITDA and unlevered free cash flow. Equity value is what accrues to shareholders
after debt and pairs with net income and levered cash flow. Mixing them, such as computing
enterprise value divided by net income, produces a number that means nothing and is
nonetheless seen regularly.

multiple is the more expensive company on any measure that accounts for what you are

Three things drive the difference. Company B grows at 18% against A's 3%, and a multiple is
a claim on future earnings, so faster growth justifies paying more for current earnings. B
earns a 27% return on invested capital against a 9% cost of capital, an 18-point economic
spread, meaning every dollar it reinvests creates value. A earns 9% against an 8.5% cost —
essentially zero spread, so its growth creates almost no value and reinvestment is close to
a wash. And A carries nearly ten times the leverage, which inflates its earnings per share
in good conditions and makes those earnings far riskier.

On a PEG basis A is at 4.0 and B at 1.72, which reverses the apparent ranking entirely.

Two cautions on that conclusion. PEG is a crude heuristic that assumes a linear relation
between growth and multiple, which does not hold, and it says nothing about how durable
B's growth is — an 18% grower priced for 16% has no margin for error, and the risk in B is
that the growth decelerates rather than that the multiple is wrong today. Meanwhile A's
risk is different in kind: with a 1.9 leverage ratio and no economic spread, a downturn
compresses earnings against fixed interest obligations.

The useful reframe is that a multiple is not a price tag, it is an implied forecast.
A at 12x is implying about 2.6% perpetual growth; B at 31x is implying about 16%. The
question is not which number is lower but which implied forecast you disagree with.

## Derivatives and Corporate Finance

A derivative's value derives from an underlying asset. Forwards and futures obligate a
transaction at a set price on a future date, differing in that futures are exchange-traded,
standardized, and marked to market daily with margin, while forwards are bilateral and
carry counterparty risk. Options grant the right without the obligation — a call to buy, a
put to sell — and therefore have an asymmetric payoff, which is what makes them useful for
hedging and what makes them harder to value. Swaps exchange cash flow streams, most
commonly fixed for floating interest.

Option pricing rests on the insight that an option's payoff can be replicated by a
continuously rebalanced portfolio of the underlying and a risk-free bond. If replication
is possible, then absence of arbitrage forces the option's price to equal the cost of the
replicating portfolio, which yields the Black-Scholes formula under its assumptions. The
striking feature of the result is that the underlying's expected return does not appear:
what matters is its volatility, since the replication argument holds regardless of drift.
This is risk-neutral valuation, and it is the central technique of derivative pricing.

The model's assumptions are known to be wrong in specific ways, and the market's
adjustments are visible in the pricing. Black-Scholes assumes constant volatility and
lognormal returns; real returns have fat tails and volatility clusters. Inverting observed
option prices for implied volatility therefore does not produce a single number but a
volatility smile or skew across strikes — with out-of-the-money puts priced at higher
implied volatility, reflecting demand for crash protection and the empirical fact that
large downward moves are more likely than the lognormal assumption allows.

Derivatives and corporate finance concepts:
  call / put             right to buy / sell at the strike
  intrinsic value        immediate exercise value; zero if out of the money
  time value             the remainder; decays toward expiry, fastest at the end
  the Greeks             delta (underlying sensitivity), gamma (delta's change),
                         theta (time decay), vega (volatility sensitivity), rho
                         (rate sensitivity)
  put-call parity        C - P = S - K e^(-rT);  a model-free arbitrage relation
  implied volatility     the market's forward-looking volatility estimate
  volatility skew        higher implied vol for downside strikes; evidence against
                         the lognormal assumption
  WACC                   weighted average cost of capital; the hurdle rate
  Modigliani-Miller      capital structure is irrelevant under strong assumptions;
                         the value lies in identifying which assumption fails
  tax shield             interest is deductible, favouring debt
  distress costs         favouring equity; the tradeoff sets an optimal structure
  agency costs           conflicts between managers, shareholders, and creditors
  pecking order          firms prefer internal funds, then debt, then equity, to

The Modigliani-Miller theorem is frequently misread. Its conclusion, that capital
structure does not affect firm value, holds in a world with no taxes, no bankruptcy costs,
no information asymmetry, and no agency conflicts. Since firm value clearly does depend on
capital structure in reality, the theorem's value is diagnostic: it identifies exactly which
market imperfection is doing the work in any particular case. Interest deductibility favours
debt; the costs of financial distress and the agency problems of leverage favour equity; the
optimum balances them.

## Markets, Statements, and Behaviour

The efficient market hypothesis states that prices reflect available information, in weak
form (past prices), semi-strong form (all public information), or strong form (including
private information). The evidence supports something close to the semi-strong form for
liquid markets: most active managers underperform their benchmarks after fees over long
horizons, and the underperformance is roughly the size of the fees, which is what an
efficient market would predict. Strong-form efficiency is contradicted by the profitability
of insider trading, which is why it is illegal.

Documented anomalies complicate the picture without overturning it. Value, momentum, size,
quality, and low-volatility effects have persisted across markets and time periods, and the
interpretation is contested between risk-based explanations (these are compensations for
bearing risks the simple model omits) and behavioural ones (these are systematic errors that
limits to arbitrage prevent from being competed away). Many anomalies also weaken
substantially after publication, which is itself evidence that markets adapt.

Financial statements are the primary evidence for any fundamental analysis, and the three
statements answer different questions. The income statement reports profitability over a
period on an accrual basis, recognizing revenue when earned rather than when cash arrives.
The balance sheet reports position at a point in time, with assets equal to liabilities plus
equity by construction. The cash flow statement reconciles the two by reporting actual cash
movement, divided into operating, investing, and financing activities.

  accrual vs cash        profit is an opinion, cash is a fact; persistent

Behavioural finance documents the systematic ways in which participants depart from the
rational model, and its findings have been substantially incorporated into mainstream
practice. Loss aversion makes investors hold losers too long and sell winners too early,
the disposition effect. Overconfidence produces excessive trading, and the empirical
relation between trading frequency and net returns is strongly negative. Recency bias
extrapolates recent performance, driving money into strategies after they have worked.
Herding produces momentum and, in extremis, bubbles.

The practical implications for an individual investor follow almost mechanically from all
of the above and are notably unexciting: diversify broadly, since idiosyncratic risk is
uncompensated; minimize costs, since fees compound against you exactly as returns compound
for you; rebalance mechanically rather than tactically, which enforces buying low and
selling high without requiring a forecast; match the time horizon of the assets to the time
horizon of the liability; and trade rarely, because the evidence that frequent trading
improves outcomes is essentially nonexistent while the evidence that it degrades them
through costs and behavioural error is extensive.
