## Historical Method and Evidence

History is an argument from evidence about the past, not a chronicle of it. The distinction
matters because the evidence is always partial, always produced by someone with a purpose,
and always survives selectively. Understanding what a source can and cannot support is the
discipline's core skill, and it is what separates historical reasoning from repeating
inherited claims.

Primary sources originate from the period under study: documents, letters, artifacts,
inscriptions, images, oral testimony. Secondary sources interpret them. The distinction is
relational rather than absolute — a nineteenth-century history of Rome is a secondary source
for Rome and a primary source for nineteenth-century historiography — and recognizing which
role a text is playing in a given argument is a routine analytical step.

Source criticism asks a standard set of questions of any document. Who produced it, when,
and for what audience? What did the author know and how did they know it? What interest did
they have in the account being received a particular way? What did the author take for
granted that a modern reader would not? What is conspicuously absent? A chronicle written to
justify a dynasty's legitimacy is not worthless — it is excellent evidence for what claims
that dynasty found it useful to make — but reading it as a neutral narrative of events is a
category error.

Methodological concepts:
  primary / secondary     origin in the period versus interpretation of it
  source criticism        authorship, purpose, audience, knowledge, interest
  corroboration           independent sources converging; the strongest evidence
  argument from silence   weak; absence of evidence has many explanations
  survivorship bias       what survives is not a random sample; durable materials,
                          elite records, and winners' accounts are overrepresented
  presentism              judging the past by present categories and values, or
                          projecting modern concepts onto societies lacking them
  historiography          the history of historical interpretation; how and why
                          readings of an event have changed
  periodization           the divisions are analytical conventions, not features
                          of the past
  counterfactual          disciplined use clarifies causal claims; undisciplined
                          use is speculation
  material evidence       archaeology, isotopes, genetics, dendrochronology,
                          climate proxies; independent of textual bias

Survivorship bias in the historical record is severe and shapes what can be known. Literacy
was rare in most societies, so written sources overwhelmingly reflect elites. Durable
materials survive and organic ones do not, so stone and ceramic civilizations appear more
prominent than those working in wood and textile. Institutions preserve their own records,
so states and churches are far better documented than households. And victors' accounts
survive conquest more often than the defeated's. Each of these tilts the record in a
knowable direction, which is why archaeology, environmental data, and quantitative methods
have been so valuable — they sample differently and can contradict the textual record.

Q: A medieval chronicle written by a monastery describes a local lord as pious and generous.
The most defensible use of this source is as evidence for:
A. The lord's actual character
B. The monastery's relationship with the lord and the qualities its author found worth
   asserting
C. General medieval attitudes toward piety
D. The chronology of the lord's donations only
Reasoning: The monastery likely received donations from the lord and had a direct interest in
the portrayal. The source is strong evidence about the relationship and about what the author
expected an audience to find persuasive, and weak evidence about character. Answer: B.

## Ancient Civilizations

The Neolithic transition to agriculture, beginning around 10,000 BCE independently in
several regions, is the most consequential change in human history and was not obviously an
improvement for those who made it. Skeletal evidence shows early farmers were shorter, more
disease-ridden, and worked longer hours than the foragers they replaced. Agriculture
supported far higher population densities, and that demographic advantage rather than any
quality-of-life benefit is what made it spread irreversibly. It also produced storable
surplus, which permitted specialization, hierarchy, and the accumulation of wealth — and
therefore inequality, taxation, and the state.

Mesopotamia developed the earliest cities, writing, and codified law. Cuneiform began around
3200 BCE as an accounting system for temple redistribution, and its origin in bookkeeping
rather than literature is characteristic: writing appears repeatedly as an administrative
technology first and acquires literary uses later. The Code of Hammurabi, around 1750 BCE,
is not the first law code but is the best preserved, and its explicitly graded penalties by
social class are direct evidence for how stratification was formalized.

Egypt's unusual continuity — recognizably the same civilization for three thousand years —
owed much to geography. The Nile's predictable annual flood supported reliable agriculture,
the deserts on either side deterred invasion, and the river itself provided transport in both
directions, since the current flows north and the prevailing wind blows south. That
combination of security and connectivity is rare and helps explain both Egypt's stability and
its comparative conservatism.

Ancient landmarks:
  ~10,000 BCE   agriculture begins independently in several regions
  ~3500-3000    Sumerian cities; cuneiform; the wheel; bronze
  ~3100         unification of Upper and Lower Egypt
  ~2600-2500    Old Kingdom pyramids; Indus Valley cities with planned drainage
  ~1750         Code of Hammurabi
  ~1600-1046    Shang dynasty; oracle bone script, the ancestor of Chinese writing
  ~1200         Bronze Age collapse across the eastern Mediterranean
  ~1200-1000    alphabetic writing spreads via Phoenician traders
  ~800-500      Axial Age; foundational religious and philosophical traditions
                emerge across Eurasia
  ~550-330      Achaemenid Persia; the largest empire to that point, governed
                through satrapies and a road system

The Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BCE is instructive as a case of systemic failure.
Within a few decades, the Mycenaean, Hittite, and several Levantine states collapsed, trade
networks disintegrated, and literacy was lost in some regions for centuries. No single cause
is sufficient: drought is documented in climate proxies, the Sea Peoples appear in Egyptian
records, internal revolt and systems collapse are inferred from destruction layers.
The current consensus favours a cascade in which a tightly interconnected system dependent
on long-distance tin supply for bronze proved fragile to compound shocks — which is why the
episode is invoked so frequently in discussions of complex system resilience.

## The Classical World and Its Successors

Classical Greece produced political and intellectual innovations disproportionate to its
size and population. Athenian democracy was direct rather than representative, with
citizens voting personally and many offices filled by lot rather than election — a
mechanism whose logic was that election favours the wealthy and well-known while sortition
distributes participation. It also excluded women, resident foreigners, and the enslaved,
who together were the substantial majority of the population. Both facts are true
simultaneously and neither cancels the other.

Rome's trajectory from city-state to Mediterranean empire is the standard case study in
institutional adaptation and its limits. The Republic's institutions were designed for a
city and proved unable to govern an empire: the wealth flowing from conquest concentrated
land ownership, displaced smallholders who had been the army's recruiting base, and produced
generals whose armies were loyal to them personally rather than to the state. The civil wars
of the first century BCE followed from that structural mismatch, and the Principate that
Augustus established was a monarchy preserving republican forms — which is why he took the
title princeps, first citizen, rather than king.

Rome's western collapse in the fifth century CE is better understood as a transformation
than as a sudden fall. The eastern empire continued for another millennium. The western
provinces saw a gradual replacement of imperial administration by Germanic successor
kingdoms whose rulers often sought Roman legitimacy and titles, a decline in long-distance
trade and urbanism visible archaeologically, and continuity of local elites and of the church
as an institutional survivor. Edward Gibbon's framing of decline and fall has shaped popular
understanding for two centuries and reflects eighteenth-century preoccupations at least as
much as fifth-century evidence.

Classical and post-classical developments:
  ~508 BCE      Cleisthenes' reforms establish Athenian democratic institutions
  ~490-479      Greco-Persian wars
  ~431-404      Peloponnesian War; Thucydides' analytical history
  ~336-323      Alexander's conquests; the Hellenistic diffusion of Greek culture
  ~264-146      Punic Wars; Rome becomes the Mediterranean power
  ~27 BCE       Augustus establishes the Principate
  ~180 CE       end of the Pax Romana
  ~313          Edict of Milan legalizes Christianity
  ~476          deposition of the last western emperor
  ~527-565      Justinian; legal codification and temporary reconquest
  ~622          Hijra; beginning of the Islamic calendar
  ~750-1258     Abbasid Caliphate; the translation movement preserves and extends
                Greek science, and Islamic scholars make original contributions in
                algebra, optics, medicine, and astronomy

The intellectual continuity through the Islamic world deserves emphasis because it is
routinely omitted from narratives that jump from Athens to the Renaissance. Greek scientific
and philosophical works were systematically translated into Arabic in Baghdad from the
eighth century, studied, corrected, and substantially extended. Algebra takes its name from
al-Khwarizmi's treatise; the word algorithm derives from his name. Ibn al-Haytham's work on
optics established experimental method in a recognizably modern form. Much of this
literature reached Latin Europe through translation centres in Spain and Sicily in the
twelfth century, which is a precondition for the later European developments rather than a
footnote to them.

## Medieval, Early Modern, and Global Connection

The medieval period in Europe was neither uniformly dark nor static. Agricultural
innovations — the heavy plough, three-field rotation, horse collar, water and wind mills —
substantially raised yields and supported population growth and urbanization from the tenth
century. Universities emerged in the twelfth. Gothic architecture solved structural problems
that Roman engineering had not. The characterization as an interval of stagnation between
classical and Renaissance achievement is a Renaissance polemic that historians abandoned
long ago and popular usage retains.

The Black Death, arriving in Europe in 1347 and killing perhaps a third to half the
population within five years, is the clearest case in premodern history of a demographic
shock producing structural change. Labour scarcity strengthened the bargaining position of
surviving peasants and wage workers, accelerating the decline of serfdom in western Europe
where lords could not enforce old obligations against workers who could simply leave. In
eastern Europe the same shock produced the opposite outcome — a second serfdom — as lords
successfully tightened control. The same cause with opposite effects, mediated by differing
institutional and political conditions, is a standard illustration of why monocausal
explanations fail.

The European voyages beginning in the late fifteenth century created the first genuinely
global exchange network, with consequences that dwarf the political narratives usually
attached to them. The Columbian Exchange transferred crops, animals, and diseases between
hemispheres. Old World diseases against which American populations had no immunity caused
mortality estimated at seventy to ninety percent in the century after contact — a
demographic catastrophe without parallel — while New World crops, particularly potatoes and
maize, substantially raised the carrying capacity of Eurasia and Africa and underwrote
population growth there.

Early modern developments:
  1347-1351     Black Death; ~30-50% mortality in Europe
  1439          movable type printing in Europe; earlier in Korea and China
  1453          Ottoman capture of Constantinople
  1492          Columbus; beginning of sustained transatlantic contact
  1517          Luther's theses; Reformation, enabled by print
  1543          Copernicus; Vesalius; conventional start of the Scientific
                Revolution
  1600s         chartered companies; the joint-stock corporation as an
                institutional innovation
  1648          Peace of Westphalia; often overstated as the origin of state
                sovereignty
  1600-1800     Atlantic slave trade at its height; ~12 million people forcibly
                transported
  1687          Newton's Principia
  1750-1850     Industrial Revolution begins in Britain

Printing deserves particular attention as a case of a technology's effects exceeding
anyone's intent. Movable type reduced the cost of reproducing text by orders of magnitude,
and the immediate consequences were not primarily scientific. Vernacular Bibles and
polemical pamphlets circulated faster than authorities could suppress them, which is why the
Reformation succeeded where earlier reform movements had been contained. Standardized
editions made textual scholarship cumulative, since scholars could finally reference the same
page of the same edition. And the fixing of vernacular languages in print contributed to the
consolidation of national linguistic identities. None of this was foreseen by the printers,
who were producing indulgences and Latin devotional texts.

## Industrialization and the Modern World

The Industrial Revolution is the second great discontinuity in human history after
agriculture, and explaining why it occurred where and when it did remains an active
argument. Britain's specific advantages included accessible coal near water transport, high
wages relative to energy costs which made labour-saving machinery profitable, an
institutional environment protecting property and permitting joint-stock enterprise, colonial
markets and raw materials, and an existing textile industry ready to mechanize. Which of
these were necessary and which incidental is genuinely contested, and the debate is
substantive because the answer bears on development policy today.

The consequences reordered society. Sustained per-capita economic growth became normal for
the first time, replacing the Malthusian pattern in which productivity gains were absorbed by
population growth. Urbanization accelerated sharply, with the attendant public health crises
that eventually drove sanitation infrastructure. Work moved from household and workshop to
factory, imposing clock discipline and separating home from workplace. Living standards for
workers appear to have stagnated or declined for the first several decades before rising
substantially — the standard of living debate, which turns on which measures are used and
which decades are compared.

The twentieth century's defining events resist summary, and a few structural observations
are more useful than a chronology. The First World War demonstrated that industrial capacity
applied to warfare produced casualties on a scale no prior framework anticipated, and its
settlement created conditions contributing to the second. The interwar economic collapse
discredited existing policy orthodoxy and enabled political movements that had been marginal.
Decolonization after 1945 more than tripled the number of sovereign states within three
decades, often within borders drawn by colonial administrations for their own convenience.

Modern landmarks:
  1789          French Revolution
  1804          Haitian independence; the only successful large slave revolt
  1861-1865     US Civil War
  1914-1918     First World War; ~17 million dead
  1917          Russian Revolution
  1929-1939     Great Depression
  1939-1945     Second World War; ~70-85 million dead; the Holocaust
  1945          United Nations; nuclear weapons used in war
  1947-1991     Cold War; decolonization
  1948          Universal Declaration of Human Rights
  1989-1991     collapse of communist governments in Europe; end of the USSR
  1990s-        globalization, internet, accelerating climate change

The historiographical caution worth stating last is that the shape of a narrative determines
its conclusions. A history organized around the nation-state produces different questions
than one organized around trade networks, environmental change, disease, or technology.
World-systems approaches, environmental history, and global history each reframe the same
evidence and reach different emphases — not because the facts differ but because the unit of
analysis does. When two accounts of the same period disagree, the disagreement is more often
about framing and significance than about what happened, and identifying which kind of
disagreement is in play is the first step to evaluating either.
