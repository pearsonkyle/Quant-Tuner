## Axioms, Measure, and Conditioning

Kolmogorov's axioms found probability on measure theory. A probability space is a triple
consisting of a sample space of outcomes, a sigma-algebra of events — closed under
complement and countable union — and a probability measure assigning each event a value
in [0,1], with the whole space measuring one and countable additivity holding over
disjoint events. Everything else in the subject is a theorem.

The restriction to a sigma-algebra rather than all subsets is not fastidiousness. For an
uncountable sample space, no measure can assign a consistent size to every subset while
remaining translation-invariant and countably additive — the Vitali construction, using
the axiom of choice, produces a set for which any assigned value leads to contradiction.
The sigma-algebra is the collection of sets on which a consistent measure exists, and
restricting attention to it is what makes the theory coherent.

Conditional probability P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B) restricts the sample space to B and
renormalizes. Independence, P(A and B) = P(A)P(B), is equivalent to P(A|B) = P(A) when
P(B) is positive: knowing B tells you nothing about A. Two subtleties recur. Pairwise
independence does not imply mutual independence — three events can be pairwise
independent while the third is determined by the first two, the standard example being
two fair coin flips and the event that they agree. And conditional independence neither
implies nor is implied by unconditional independence: two variables can be dependent
overall and independent given a third (explaining away a common cause), or independent
overall and dependent given a third (conditioning on a common effect, the collider
structure that makes "controlling for everything" a source of bias rather than rigour).

Foundational results:
  axioms              P(A) >= 0;  P(Omega) = 1;  countable additivity on disjoints
  complement          P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)
  inclusion-exclusion P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)
  chain rule          P(A1...An) = P(A1) P(A2|A1) ... P(An|A1...An-1)
  total probability   P(A) = sum over a partition B_i of P(A|B_i) P(B_i)
  Bayes               P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B)
  union bound         P(union A_i) <= sum P(A_i);  crude and universally applicable
  Borel-Cantelli I    sum P(A_n) < infinity implies P(A_n infinitely often) = 0
  Borel-Cantelli II   independent A_n with divergent sum occur infinitely often

The union bound's crudeness is its strength: it requires no independence assumption
whatsoever and holds always. It is the workhorse of high-probability arguments across
computer science and statistics — bounding the probability that any of many bad events
occurs, which is exactly the structure of multiple-testing corrections, of concentration
arguments over a finite hypothesis class, and of most randomized algorithm analyses.

Q: Events A and B are independent, and A and C are independent. Does it follow that A is
independent of B and C jointly?
A. Yes, independence is transitive
B. No; pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence
C. Yes, if B and C are independent
D. Only if all three have equal probability
Reasoning: Pairwise independence is strictly weaker than mutual independence. Take two
fair coin flips B and C and let A be the event that they match; A is independent of each
individually but is determined by the pair. Option C is also false — B and C are
independent in that very counterexample. Answer: B.

## Random Variables and Transformations

A random variable is a measurable function from the sample space to the reals; its
distribution is the pushforward measure. The cumulative distribution function F(x) =
P(X <= x) characterizes it completely and always exists, which is why it is the right
general object — densities exist only for absolutely continuous distributions and mass
functions only for discrete ones, but every random variable has a CDF.

Expectation is the integral of the variable against the probability measure. Its
linearity holds unconditionally, without any independence assumption, and this is the
most exploited fact in applied probability. The expected number of fixed points in a
random permutation is computed by writing it as a sum of indicator variables, each with
expectation 1/n, giving exactly 1 regardless of n — a calculation that would be
forbidding directly and is immediate by linearity, despite the indicators being
dependent. Variance is not linear, and Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) requires
uncorrelatedness.

Moment generating functions and characteristic functions encode a distribution in
transform space, converting convolution into multiplication. The sum of independent
random variables has a density given by the convolution of theirs, which is awkward
directly and trivial in transform space, which is why sums are analyzed there. The
characteristic function, being the Fourier transform of the density, always exists
whereas the MGF may not — the Cauchy and heavy-tailed distributions have no MGF — and
Levy's continuity theorem, that pointwise convergence of characteristic functions implies
convergence in distribution, is the machinery behind the central limit theorem.

Transformation rules:
  discrete            P(Y = y) = sum over x with g(x) = y of P(X = x)
  monotone continuous f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) |d g^{-1}/dy|
  multivariate        f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) |det J|,  J the Jacobian
  sum of independents convolution; multiply MGFs or characteristic functions
  linear              E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b;  Var(aX + b) = a^2 Var(X)
  law of the unconscious statistician  E[g(X)] = integral g(x) f(x) dx, no need
                      to derive the distribution of g(X)
  tower property      E[X] = E[E[X|Y]]
  variance decomposition  Var(X) = E[Var(X|Y)] + Var(E[X|Y])

The variance decomposition — the law of total variance — is worth internalizing because
it names the two sources of uncertainty in any hierarchical situation. The first term is
the average within-group variance, the second is the variance of the group means. In
statistics this is the ANOVA decomposition; in machine learning it separates aleatoric
from epistemic uncertainty; in hierarchical modelling it is exactly what determines how
much shrinkage a group receives. Whenever an analysis needs to attribute variability
between "noise within" and "differences between", this identity is the formal statement.

The Jacobian in the change-of-variables formula is where errors concentrate, and it has
a clean interpretation: a transformation that stretches a region must reduce the density
there proportionally, so that total probability is preserved. Forgetting it produces
densities that do not integrate to one, which is a useful self-check. This same
determinant is what normalizing flows track explicitly in order to remain valid
probability models under learned invertible transformations.

## Limit Theorems

The laws of large numbers state that sample averages converge to expectations. The weak
law gives convergence in probability: for any epsilon, the probability that the sample
mean deviates by more than epsilon tends to zero. The strong law gives almost-sure
convergence: the sample mean converges for every outcome outside a set of probability
zero. The distinction is about whether the sequence of averages settles down permanently
(strong) or merely becomes unlikely to deviate at any fixed time (weak). Both require
only a finite mean; neither requires finite variance, though the simplest proofs assume it.

The central limit theorem says that the standardized sample mean converges in
distribution to a standard normal, regardless of the underlying distribution, provided
the variance is finite. This universality is the reason the normal distribution appears
everywhere: any quantity that is a sum of many small independent contributions is
approximately normal, whatever those contributions look like individually. It also
explains why measurement error, biological variation, and aggregate financial returns
over short horizons all exhibit approximately normal behaviour.

The finite-variance condition is essential and its failure is not exotic. For
heavy-tailed distributions with infinite variance, sums converge instead to stable
distributions with power-law tails, and the scaling is n^{1/alpha} rather than
sqrt(n). The Cauchy distribution is the extreme case: the sample mean of n Cauchy
variables has exactly the same distribution as a single observation, so averaging
provides no improvement at all no matter how much data is collected. Since financial
returns, city sizes, network degrees, and file sizes all exhibit heavy tails, applying
CLT-based reasoning to them produces confidently wrong error bars.

Convergence modes, from strongest to weakest:
  almost sure          P(lim X_n = X) = 1;  the strong law
  in probability       P(|X_n - X| > eps) -> 0 for every eps;  the weak law
  in L^p               E|X_n - X|^p -> 0
  in distribution      F_n(x) -> F(x) at continuity points;  the CLT
  relationships        a.s. implies in probability implies in distribution;
                       L^p implies in probability; none of the converses hold
  Slutsky              if X_n converges in distribution and Y_n in probability to a
                       constant, then sums and products behave as expected
  delta method         sqrt(n)(g(Xbar) - g(mu)) -> Normal(0, g'(mu)^2 sigma^2)

The delta method is how asymptotic standard errors are obtained for functions of
estimates. It is a first-order Taylor expansion combined with the CLT: a smooth function
of an asymptotically normal estimator is itself asymptotically normal with variance
scaled by the squared derivative. This gives the standard error of a log-odds, of a
ratio, of a transformed correlation, and it fails exactly where the derivative vanishes,
in which case a second-order expansion gives a chi-square rather than a normal limit.

Q: The sample mean of n independent Cauchy random variables has:
A. Variance sigma^2/n
B. The same distribution as a single Cauchy variable
C. An approximately normal distribution for large n
D. Variance decreasing as 1/sqrt(n)
Reasoning: The Cauchy distribution has undefined mean and infinite variance, so neither
the law of large numbers nor the central limit theorem applies. It is stable with index
one, so the average of n copies is distributed exactly as one copy. Collecting more data
does not improve the estimate at all — a stark demonstration that the CLT's finite-variance
hypothesis is load-bearing. Answer: B.

## Concentration Inequalities

Concentration inequalities bound the probability that a random variable deviates from
its expectation, and they are the quantitative backbone of modern statistics, learning
theory, and randomized algorithms. Their appeal is that they hold at finite sample
sizes, unlike asymptotic results, and often require very weak assumptions.

Markov's inequality is the weakest and most general: for a non-negative random variable,
P(X >= a) <= E[X]/a. It requires only non-negativity and the existence of a mean.
Chebyshev's inequality follows by applying Markov to the squared deviation, giving
P(|X - mu| >= k sigma) <= 1/k^2, which requires finite variance and holds for any
distribution — the guarantee that at least 75% of any distribution lies within two
standard deviations, and 89% within three. These bounds are loose for well-behaved
distributions, where the normal gives 95% and 99.7%, but they cannot be improved without
further assumptions, and there exist distributions attaining them.

Chernoff bounds achieve exponential decay by applying Markov to the exponentiated
variable and optimizing over the free parameter. For sums of independent bounded
variables, Hoeffding's inequality gives P(|Xbar - mu| >= t) <= 2 exp(-2 n t^2 / (b-a)^2),
which decays exponentially in n rather than polynomially. This is the qualitative leap
that makes learning theory possible: it says that with n samples, the empirical mean is
within t of the truth except with probability exponentially small, so a union bound over
a large but finite hypothesis class still leaves high confidence.

Concentration toolkit:
  Markov            P(X >= a) <= E[X]/a;  X >= 0 only
  Chebyshev         P(|X - mu| >= k sigma) <= 1/k^2;  any distribution
  Chernoff          exponential bounds via the MGF; needs independence
  Hoeffding         bounded independent summands; exp(-2 n t^2 / range^2)
  Bernstein         uses the variance; sharper than Hoeffding when variance is
                    small relative to the range
  McDiarmid         bounded differences; applies to any function of independent
                    variables that changes little when one input changes
  Jensen            E[g(X)] >= g(E[X]) for convex g;  the source of many bounds
  Cauchy-Schwarz    E[XY]^2 <= E[X^2] E[Y^2]

McDiarmid's bounded-differences inequality is the most broadly applicable of these
because it does not require the quantity of interest to be a sum. Any function of
independent random variables whose value changes by at most c_i when the i-th input is
altered concentrates around its mean with a Hoeffding-type bound. This covers the
generalization error of a learning algorithm, the size of the largest connected component
in a random graph, and the length of the longest common subsequence — none of which are
sums, all of which are stable under single-coordinate changes.

Jensen's inequality is the source of a great deal of structure. For a convex function,
the expectation of the function is at least the function of the expectation. It gives
immediately that E[X^2] >= E[X]^2, which is variance non-negativity; that the arithmetic
mean dominates the geometric mean; that the Kullback-Leibler divergence is non-negative;
and that the evidence lower bound in variational inference is indeed a lower bound. When
an inequality between an expectation and a function of an expectation is needed, Jensen
is the first thing to try.

## Stochastic Processes and Markov Chains

A stochastic process is a collection of random variables indexed by time. The Markov
property — the future depends on the past only through the present — is the structural
assumption that makes analysis tractable and is far less restrictive than it first
appears, since any finite history can be absorbed into an enlarged state.

A discrete-time Markov chain is specified by a transition matrix P whose entry (i,j) is
the probability of moving from state i to j. Powers of P give multi-step transition
probabilities, so the distribution after n steps is the initial distribution times P^n.
The long-run behaviour is governed by the eigenstructure: the stationary distribution is
the left eigenvector with eigenvalue one, and the rate of approach to it is set by the
second-largest eigenvalue modulus. The spectral gap — one minus that modulus — determines
the mixing time, and a chain with a small gap mixes slowly, which is exactly the
diagnostic problem in Markov chain Monte Carlo.

Existence and uniqueness of the stationary distribution require conditions. Irreducibility
means every state is reachable from every other, ruling out disconnected components with
separate stationary distributions. Aperiodicity rules out deterministic cycling, which
would prevent convergence even with a unique stationary distribution — a chain
alternating between two states has a stationary distribution it never settles into.
Positive recurrence, automatic for finite chains, is needed in the infinite case.
Together these give the fundamental theorem: an irreducible aperiodic positive-recurrent
chain converges to its unique stationary distribution from any starting point.

Markov chain concepts:
  transition matrix     P_ij = P(next = j | current = i);  rows sum to 1
  n-step                P^n
  stationary            pi P = pi,  pi a probability vector
  detailed balance      pi_i P_ij = pi_j P_ji;  implies stationarity, and is what
                        Metropolis-Hastings constructs by design
  irreducible           all states communicate
  aperiodic             gcd of return times is 1
  ergodic               irreducible + aperiodic + positive recurrent
  mixing time           steps until within epsilon of stationary; ~1/spectral gap
  hitting time          expected steps to reach a target state
  absorbing             once entered, never left; the fundamental matrix gives
                        expected steps to absorption

Detailed balance deserves emphasis because it is the design principle of MCMC. Verifying
that a proposed chain has a given stationary distribution is hard in general and easy if
detailed balance holds, since detailed balance implies stationarity directly.
Metropolis-Hastings therefore constructs its acceptance probability specifically to
enforce detailed balance with respect to the target distribution, which is why the
algorithm works for any target known only up to a normalizing constant — the constant
cancels in the acceptance ratio, which is the entire reason Bayesian computation is
feasible.

Continuous-time processes add their own structure. The Poisson process counts events
occurring at a constant rate with independent increments, and its defining properties are
equivalent: exponential inter-arrival times, Poisson counts in any interval, and
memorylessness. Brownian motion is the continuous-time, continuous-state limit of a
random walk, with independent Gaussian increments whose variance is proportional to
elapsed time; its paths are continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere, which is
why stochastic calculus requires the Ito integral rather than ordinary calculus, and why
Ito's lemma carries a second-order term that has no classical analogue.

A martingale is a process whose conditional expected future value equals its present
value — a fair game. The optional stopping theorem states that under suitable conditions
the expected value at a stopping time equals the initial value, which resolves gambling
problems, gives the gambler's ruin probabilities in a line, and underlies sequential
analysis. The martingale convergence theorem states that a bounded martingale converges
almost surely, which is used constantly in proving convergence of stochastic algorithms.
Applications extend well beyond gambling: likelihood ratios form martingales under the
null hypothesis, which is what makes always-valid sequential testing possible, and the
prices of traded assets under a risk-neutral measure are martingales, which is the
foundational result of mathematical finance.
