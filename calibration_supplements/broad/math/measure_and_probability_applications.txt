## Markov Chains and the Memoryless Property

A stochastic process is a collection of random variables indexed by time, and the useful structure
comes from restricting how the future may depend on the past. The Markov property is the most
important such restriction: given the present state, the future is independent of the past. This
memorylessness is what makes a process analytically tractable, because the entire history collapses
into the current state.

A discrete Markov chain is specified by its transition probabilities, and its long-run behaviour
follows from the structure of that matrix. States communicate if each is reachable from the other,
which partitions the state space into classes. A class is recurrent if the process returns to it
with probability one and transient if there is positive probability of leaving forever. An
irreducible chain — one where every state communicates with every other — that is aperiodic and
positive recurrent has a unique stationary distribution to which it converges from any starting
point.

The stationary distribution is what most applications want. It describes the long-run fraction of
time spent in each state, and it is found by solving a linear system expressing that the
distribution is unchanged by one transition. Convergence to it is geometric, at a rate governed by
the second-largest eigenvalue of the transition matrix, which is why the spectral gap is the
quantity that determines how long a simulation must run before its samples are usable.

Q: A Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler produces samples whose statistics are stable across a long
run. Does this establish convergence?
A. Yes, stability is convergence
B. No — a chain trapped in one region of the state space produces stable statistics for that region
   indefinitely; stability is necessary and not sufficient, and multiple chains from dispersed
   starting points are the standard check
C. Only for discrete chains
D. Convergence cannot be assessed
Reasoning: Apparent stationarity within a mode is indistinguishable from global convergence when
observed from a single chain. Running several chains from overdispersed starts and comparing
between-chain and within-chain variance is the standard diagnostic precisely because it can detect
this. Answer: B.

## Poisson Processes and Counting

The Poisson process models events occurring randomly in time at a constant average rate, with
independent increments and no two events simultaneous. It is characterized equivalently by three
descriptions: the number of events in any interval is Poisson distributed with mean proportional to
the interval's length; the times between events are independent exponential variables; and given the
number of events in an interval, their positions are uniformly distributed within it.

The exponential inter-arrival time is memoryless, which is the property that makes the process
tractable and which is also its main modelling limitation. Memorylessness means that having waited
any amount of time tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait, and this is a strong
assumption that fails for many real processes — machine failures become more likely with age,
customer arrivals have daily patterns, and network traffic is bursty rather than smooth.

The generalizations relax specific assumptions. A non-homogeneous Poisson process allows a
time-varying rate, which handles daily and seasonal patterns while preserving independence. A
renewal process allows arbitrary inter-arrival distributions, losing the Markov property in
continuous time but retaining tractable long-run behaviour. A compound process attaches a random
magnitude to each event, which is the natural model for insurance claims and for aggregate demand.

## Martingales and Brownian Motion

A martingale is a process whose expected future value, given everything known so far, equals its
current value. It formalizes a fair game: no strategy based on available information changes the
expected outcome. The definition is deceptively simple and the consequences are powerful, because
martingales admit strong general theorems that apply regardless of the process's specific
distribution.

The optional stopping theorem is the most used of these. It states that under conditions preventing
unbounded play, the expected value at a stopping time equals the initial value. This immediately
resolves gambling questions that are otherwise messy — the expected outcome of a strategy that
doubles after losses is unchanged, and it fails in practice only because the conditions on bounded
resources are violated. The theorem's conditions are where all the content lies.

Brownian motion is the continuous-time process with independent, stationary, normally distributed
increments and continuous paths. It is the limit of a random walk under appropriate scaling, which
is why it appears whenever many small independent effects accumulate. Its paths are continuous
everywhere and differentiable nowhere, with unbounded variation on every interval — properties that
make ordinary calculus inapplicable and that motivated the development of a different calculus
entirely.

## Stochastic Calculus

Ordinary calculus fails for Brownian paths because they have unbounded variation, so the Riemann-
Stieltjes integral does not exist. The Ito integral is constructed differently: it is defined as a
limit of sums evaluating the integrand at the left endpoint of each subinterval, and that choice —
rather than the midpoint or right endpoint — is what makes the resulting integral a martingale.

The left-endpoint convention has a concrete interpretation: the integrand is decided before the
increment is observed, which is exactly the situation of a trading strategy or a control decision
that cannot see the future. This is why the Ito convention is the right one for finance and control,
while the midpoint convention, which yields ordinary calculus rules, is preferred in physics where
the noise is a limit of a smooth process.

Ito's lemma is the chain rule of this calculus and it contains an extra term that has no counterpart
in ordinary calculus. Because the quadratic variation of Brownian motion is nonzero, the
second-order term in a Taylor expansion does not vanish, and the derivative of a function of a
stochastic process acquires a term proportional to its second derivative. This term is the source of
essentially every result in the field that seems surprising, including why the expected growth rate
of a lognormal quantity differs from the drift of its logarithm.

## Queues, Applications, and Simulation

Queueing theory analyses systems where items arrive, wait, and are served, and its results are
surprisingly general. The standard notation specifies the arrival process, the service distribution,
and the number of servers, and the simplest case — Poisson arrivals, exponential service, one server
— yields closed-form expressions for queue length and waiting time as functions of the utilization.

The central result is that waiting time grows without bound as utilization approaches capacity, and
it does so hyperbolically rather than linearly. A system at fifty percent utilization has a modest
queue; at ninety percent the queue is roughly nine times longer; at ninety-nine percent it is
ninety-nine times. This is why systems operated near capacity behave so badly, why the last
increment of utilization is so expensive in latency, and why capacity planning targets a utilization
well below one hundred percent rather than treating headroom as waste.

Little's law is the other result worth knowing and it is remarkably general: the average number in a
system equals the arrival rate times the average time spent in it. It requires no assumptions about
the arrival or service distributions, applies to any stable system, and provides a consistency check
on any measurement of the three quantities. Two of them measured gives the third for free, which is
frequently the easiest route to the one that is hard to observe directly.
