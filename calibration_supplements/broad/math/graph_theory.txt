## Definitions and Representation

A graph consists of vertices and edges connecting pairs of them, and the abstraction is
powerful precisely because it retains only the relational structure. Road networks, molecular
bonds, social ties, web links, dependency relations, and neural connections are all graphs,
and a theorem about graphs applies to all of them.

The vocabulary distinctions matter for stating results correctly. A graph is simple if it has
no loops and no repeated edges, and the qualifier appears in most theorems because
multigraphs behave differently. A graph is directed when edges have orientation, which changes
connectivity notions substantially: reachability becomes asymmetric, and connectivity splits
into weak, ignoring direction, and strong, requiring mutual reachability. Weights on edges
represent distance, cost, capacity, or strength, and whether an algorithm handles negative
weights is a real distinction rather than a technicality.

Representation choice determines algorithmic cost. An adjacency matrix uses space quadratic in
the vertex count and answers edge queries in constant time, which suits dense graphs and
matrix-based algorithms. An adjacency list uses space proportional to vertices plus edges and
enumerates a vertex's neighbours in time proportional to their number, which suits the sparse
graphs that essentially all real networks are. For a social network with a billion vertices
and average degree a hundred, the matrix is impossible and the list is routine.

Basic definitions:
  order and size        vertex count and edge count
  degree                number of incident edges; in-degree and out-degree when
                        directed
  handshake lemma       the degree sum equals twice the edge count; hence the
                        number of odd-degree vertices is even
  simple graph          no loops, no multi-edges
  complete graph K_n    every pair adjacent; n(n-1)/2 edges
  bipartite             vertices split into two sets with edges only between them;
                        equivalently, no odd cycle
  subgraph              a subset of vertices with a subset of the induced edges
  induced subgraph      all edges among the chosen vertices
  walk, trail, path     repeated vertices allowed / repeated edges disallowed /
                        both disallowed
  cycle                 a closed path
  adjacency matrix      O(V^2) space, O(1) edge query; suits dense graphs
  adjacency list        O(V+E) space; suits sparse graphs, which is nearly
                        everything real
  degree sequence       the multiset of degrees; not sufficient to determine the
                        graph

The handshake lemma is trivial to state and useful more often than its simplicity suggests.
Because each edge contributes to exactly two degrees, the sum of degrees is even, and
therefore the number of vertices with odd degree must be even. This immediately rules out
many proposed degree sequences, proves that a party where everyone shakes hands has an even
number of people who shook an odd number of hands, and is the first step in the proof that an
Euler circuit requires all degrees to be even.

Q: Can a graph exist with degree sequence 4, 4, 3, 3, 2 ?
A. Yes   B. No, the degree sum is odd   C. No, it exceeds the maximum degree   D. Only if
directed
Reasoning: The degree sum is 4+4+3+3+2 = 16, which is even, so the handshake lemma is
satisfied and the count of odd-degree vertices is two, also even. Such a graph does exist —
the sum being even is necessary and the Erdos-Gallai conditions determine sufficiency, which
this sequence satisfies. Answer: A.

## Connectivity and Traversal

Connectivity asks whether vertices are reachable from one another. An undirected graph is
connected when a path exists between every pair; otherwise it decomposes into connected
components. A directed graph is strongly connected when every vertex reaches every other
following edge directions, and weakly connected when the underlying undirected graph is
connected — a distinction that matters enormously for the web graph, which is weakly connected
and decomposes into a large strongly connected core plus substantial regions that only reach
it or are only reached from it.

Cut vertices and bridges are the single points of failure. A cut vertex is one whose removal
disconnects the graph; a bridge is an edge with the same property. Identifying them locates
vulnerabilities in a network, and both are found in a single depth-first search using low-link
values, which track the earliest reachable ancestor from each subtree.

Traversal underlies most graph algorithms. Depth-first search explores as deeply as possible
before backtracking, and its recursion structure naturally yields connected components, cycle
detection, topological ordering of a directed acyclic graph, and strongly connected
components. Breadth-first search explores in layers and yields shortest paths in unweighted
graphs, which depth-first search does not — a distinction worth stating because the two are
otherwise interchangeable for reachability questions.

Connectivity and traversal:
  connected             a path between every pair
  component             a maximal connected subgraph
  strongly connected    mutual reachability in a directed graph
  cut vertex            removal disconnects; found by DFS low-link
  bridge                an edge whose removal disconnects
  vertex connectivity   minimum vertices whose removal disconnects
  Menger's theorem      the minimum cut equals the maximum number of disjoint paths
  DFS                   O(V+E); components, cycles, topological sort, SCC
  BFS                   O(V+E); shortest paths in unweighted graphs, level structure
  topological sort      a linear order respecting all edges; exists iff acyclic
  Dijkstra              shortest paths with non-negative weights, O(E log V)
  Bellman-Ford          handles negative weights, detects negative cycles, O(VE)
  Floyd-Warshall        all-pairs shortest paths, O(V^3); simple and effective for
                        small dense graphs
  A-star                Dijkstra with an admissible heuristic; optimal if the
                        heuristic never overestimates

The restriction of Dijkstra's algorithm to non-negative weights is not an implementation
detail but a correctness requirement. The algorithm finalizes a vertex's distance when it is
extracted from the priority queue, relying on the assumption that no shorter path can be found
later — which holds only if adding edges cannot decrease a path's length. With negative edges
that assumption fails and the algorithm returns wrong answers silently. Bellman-Ford relaxes
all edges repeatedly and handles negatives correctly, and it additionally detects negative
cycles, for which shortest paths are undefined because a path can be made arbitrarily short by
cycling.

## Trees, Matching, and Flow

A tree is a connected acyclic graph, and the equivalent characterizations are worth knowing
because different ones are convenient in different proofs: a tree on n vertices has exactly
n-1 edges, has a unique path between every pair, becomes disconnected on removing any edge, and
acquires a cycle on adding any edge. A spanning tree of a connected graph is a subgraph that
is a tree and includes every vertex, and a minimum spanning tree minimizes total edge weight.

Both standard minimum spanning tree algorithms are greedy and both are correct, which is
worth noting because greedy algorithms usually are not. Kruskal's sorts edges and adds each if
it does not create a cycle, using a disjoint-set structure to test that efficiently. Prim's
grows a single tree from an arbitrary start, repeatedly adding the cheapest edge leaving it.
The correctness of both follows from the cut property: for any partition of the vertices, the
minimum-weight edge crossing the partition belongs to some minimum spanning tree.

Matching pairs up vertices with no shared endpoints, and the bipartite case has a clean
theory with wide application — assigning workers to jobs, students to schools, organs to
recipients. Hall's marriage theorem gives the exact condition for a perfect matching in a
bipartite graph: one exists if and only if every subset of one side has at least as many
neighbours as it has members. The condition is elegant and, being over all subsets, is not
directly a practical algorithm; augmenting path methods provide that.

Trees, matching, and flow:
  tree characterizations  connected and acyclic; n-1 edges; unique paths
  spanning tree           includes all vertices, no cycles
  Kruskal                 sort edges, add if no cycle; union-find
  Prim                    grow from a start vertex; priority queue
  cut property            the minimum edge across any cut is in some MST
  matching                edges with no shared vertices
  maximum matching        the largest such set
  perfect matching        covers every vertex
  Hall's theorem          bipartite perfect matching iff every subset has enough
                          neighbours
  augmenting path         alternating path whose ends are unmatched; flipping it
                          increases the matching by one
  stable matching         Gale-Shapley; always exists, and the proposing side gets
                          its best stable outcome
  max-flow                the greatest rate from source to sink respecting
                          capacities
  min-cut                 the cheapest set of edges whose removal disconnects them
  max-flow min-cut        the two are equal; a duality result with many corollaries
  bipartite matching      reducible to max flow with unit capacities

The max-flow min-cut theorem is one of the most useful results in combinatorial
optimization because so many problems reduce to it. Bipartite matching, vertex-disjoint path
counting, project selection, image segmentation with binary labels, and baseball elimination
all become flow problems, and solving the flow gives the answer along with a certificate — the
minimum cut proves optimality, which is why the result is used as much for its dual
information as for the flow value itself.

## Colouring, Planarity, and Spectral Methods

Graph colouring assigns labels to vertices so that adjacent vertices differ, and the minimum
number needed is the chromatic number. The problem models conflict avoidance generally:
register allocation in compilers, frequency assignment in wireless networks, exam scheduling,
and Sudoku are all colouring problems. Determining the chromatic number is NP-hard, and
greedy colouring gives a bound of one more than the maximum degree, which is tight for
complete graphs and odd cycles and loose otherwise.

A graph is planar when it can be drawn without crossings. Euler's formula relates vertices,
edges, and faces of a planar drawing, and it yields the bound that a simple planar graph has
at most 3n-6 edges — which immediately shows that the complete graph on five vertices and the
complete bipartite graph on three plus three are not planar. Kuratowski's theorem states that
these two are the only obstructions: a graph is planar exactly when it contains no subdivision
of either.

The four colour theorem states that every planar graph is four-colourable. It was the first
major theorem proved with essential computer assistance, checking a large number of
unavoidable configurations, and the resulting debate about what constitutes a proof was
substantive. The theorem remains without a proof a human can verify unaided, and subsequent
formalization in a proof assistant has addressed the concern about the computational
component's reliability while not producing a shorter argument.

Colouring, planarity, and spectra:
  chromatic number       minimum colours for a proper vertex colouring
  greedy bound           at most maxdegree + 1
  bipartite              exactly the 2-colourable graphs
  four colour theorem    every planar graph is 4-colourable
  Euler's formula        V - E + F = 2 for a connected planar graph
  planar edge bound      E <= 3V - 6 for simple planar graphs
  Kuratowski             planar iff no K5 or K33 subdivision
  clique number          largest complete subgraph; a lower bound on chromatic
                         number, not generally equal
  independent set        no two adjacent; complement of a clique in the complement
                         graph
  adjacency spectrum     eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix
  Laplacian              degree matrix minus adjacency; positive semidefinite
  algebraic connectivity the second smallest Laplacian eigenvalue; zero exactly
                         when disconnected, and its magnitude measures how well
                         connected
  spectral partitioning  the corresponding eigenvector separates the graph into
                         weakly linked parts
  spectral clustering    embed with Laplacian eigenvectors, then cluster

Spectral methods connect graph structure to linear algebra and are the theoretical basis for
a great deal of practical network analysis. The Laplacian's eigenvalues encode connectivity:
the multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue equals the number of connected components, and the
smallest nonzero eigenvalue measures how difficult the graph is to cut. Its eigenvector, the
Fiedler vector, assigns values to vertices such that thresholding it produces a good
partition, which is the foundation of spectral clustering and of many graph partitioning
heuristics used in parallel computing and circuit layout.

## Random Graphs and Real Networks

The Erdos-Renyi random graph connects each pair of vertices independently with fixed
probability, and its most striking property is a phase transition. Below an average degree of
one the graph consists of small components; above it, a giant component containing a constant
fraction of all vertices appears abruptly. The transition is sharp in the limit of large size,
and it is the graph-theoretic version of percolation, with applications to epidemic thresholds
and network robustness.

Real networks depart from the random model in three consistent ways, and each has structural
consequences. Degree distributions are heavy-tailed rather than Poisson, so a few hubs have
degree orders of magnitude above the mean, and the mean degree is a poor summary. Clustering
is far higher than random, meaning that two neighbours of a vertex are likely to be connected
— triangles are common, which reflects that ties form through shared contacts. Path lengths
are nonetheless short, giving the small-world property.

The small-world result is that adding a small number of random long-range edges to a highly
clustered lattice collapses the average path length while barely reducing clustering. This
resolves the apparent tension between local structure and global reachability, and it explains
why social networks are simultaneously locally dense and globally shallow. The consequence for
dynamics is substantial: information and disease spread on small-world networks far faster
than on regular lattices, because the shortcuts bypass the local structure.

  Erdos-Renyi           independent edges with fixed probability
  giant component       emerges abruptly at average degree one
  small world           high clustering, short path lengths
  scale-free            a power-law degree distribution; hubs dominate
  preferential attachment  new nodes attach proportionally to existing degree;
                        one mechanism producing heavy tails
  clustering coefficient  fraction of a vertex's neighbour pairs that are adjacent
  betweenness centrality  fraction of shortest paths passing through a vertex;
                        identifies brokers and bottlenecks
  closeness centrality  inverse average distance to all others
  eigenvector centrality  importance proportional to neighbours' importance;
                        PageRank is a variant with damping
  assortativity         whether high-degree vertices attach to each other
  community detection   densely connected groups; modularity maximization is
                        standard and has a known resolution limit that hides small
                        communities
  robustness            heavy-tailed networks resist random failure and are
                        fragile to targeted attack on hubs

That last property is worth stating as the practical conclusion of the field. A network with
a heavy-tailed degree distribution retains its giant component after removing a large fraction
of randomly chosen vertices, because random removal almost always takes low-degree vertices
that carry little of the connectivity. Removing the highest-degree vertices in order
fragments the same network after removing a very small fraction. The identical structure is
therefore extremely robust to failure and extremely vulnerable to attack, which applies equally
to internet infrastructure, power grids, epidemic control by targeting super-spreaders, and
the removal of keystone species from an ecological network.
