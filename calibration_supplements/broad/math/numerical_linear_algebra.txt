## Floating Point and Conditioning

Numerical linear algebra computes with matrices in finite precision, and the discipline's central
concern is understanding when the computed answer resembles the true one. Floating point represents
numbers with a fixed relative precision, so every arithmetic operation introduces a small relative
error, and the question is how those errors propagate through a computation involving millions of
operations.

Conditioning is a property of the problem and stability is a property of the algorithm, and keeping
them separate resolves most confusion in the subject. The condition number measures how much the
solution can change in response to a perturbation in the data — a well-conditioned problem has a
unique answer that is robust, and an ill-conditioned one has an answer that moves substantially
under small input changes regardless of how it is computed. A stable algorithm produces an answer
that is exact for slightly perturbed input, which is the best any algorithm can do.

The two combine multiplicatively in the error estimate: a stable algorithm applied to an
ill-conditioned problem still yields a poor answer, and the poor answer is the problem's fault
rather than the algorithm's. This means diagnosing a bad result begins with computing or estimating
the condition number, because if the problem is ill-conditioned, changing the algorithm will not
help and the resolution must come from reformulating the problem or accepting the sensitivity.

Q: Solving a linear system yields a solution whose residual is tiny but which differs greatly from
the known true answer. What does this indicate?
A. The solver is broken
B. The system is ill-conditioned — a backward stable solver produces a small residual by
   construction, and in an ill-conditioned problem a small residual is compatible with a large error
   in the solution itself
C. The residual was computed incorrectly
D. More precision is needed
Reasoning: Backward stable methods guarantee a small residual, not a small forward error. The
relationship between them is mediated by the condition number, so an ill-conditioned system admits
solutions with tiny residuals and large errors. Higher precision helps somewhat and does not change
the conditioning. Answer: B.

## Factorizations

Most of numerical linear algebra reduces to computing a factorization and then solving with it,
because the factorizations expose structure that makes subsequent operations cheap and stable.
Triangular systems are solved directly by substitution, orthogonal matrices are inverted by
transposition, and diagonal matrices are trivial, so the useful factorizations express a general
matrix in terms of these.

Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting produces a lower and upper triangular factorization and
is the standard method for general square systems. Pivoting — exchanging rows to place the largest
available element on the diagonal — is what makes it usable in practice; without it the method fails
on matrices that are perfectly well-conditioned, because a small pivot amplifies subsequent errors
enormously. For symmetric positive definite matrices, the Cholesky factorization exploits the
structure, requires no pivoting, costs half as much, and is unconditionally stable.

Orthogonal factorizations are the tool for problems where stability matters most. The QR
factorization expresses a matrix as an orthogonal matrix times an upper triangular one, and because
orthogonal transformations preserve norms, they do not amplify errors. The singular value
decomposition goes furthest, expressing any matrix as a rotation, a scaling, and another rotation,
and it reveals the rank, the condition number, the ranges and null spaces, and the best low-rank
approximation. It costs more than the alternatives and it answers questions none of them can.

## Least Squares and Regularization

Overdetermined systems — more equations than unknowns — generally have no exact solution, and the
least squares solution minimizes the residual norm. The mathematically clean formulation multiplies
by the transpose to give the normal equations, and this is precisely the formulation to avoid
numerically, because forming that product squares the condition number and can turn a solvable
problem into an unsolvable one.

The stable approach uses an orthogonal factorization directly. QR applied to the original matrix
solves least squares with the original conditioning rather than its square, and the singular value
decomposition handles the rank-deficient case where QR alone is ambiguous. The cost difference is
modest and the accuracy difference can be the difference between a meaningful answer and noise,
which is why every serious library implements least squares this way and not through the normal
equations.

Regularization addresses the ill-conditioned and rank-deficient cases by modifying the problem. Adding
a penalty on the solution norm shifts the singular values away from zero, which bounds the
amplification of noise at the cost of biasing the solution toward zero. The regularization parameter
controls this tradeoff, and choosing it is the substantive decision: too little leaves noise
amplified, too much biases the answer, and the standard selection methods use cross-validation or
the shape of the residual-versus-solution-norm curve.

## Eigenvalue Computation and Iterative Methods

Eigenvalue problems cannot be solved by a finite algorithm, because that would amount to solving a
general polynomial by radicals, which is impossible above degree four. Every eigenvalue method is
therefore iterative, and the practical methods converge quickly enough that this distinction is a
theoretical point rather than an obstacle.

The standard dense approach reduces the matrix to a simpler form by orthogonal similarity
transformations — Hessenberg for a general matrix, tridiagonal for a symmetric one — and then applies
an iteration that converges to the eigenvalues. The symmetric case is much better behaved: its
eigenvalues are real, its eigenvectors orthogonal, and the problem is well-conditioned. The
nonsymmetric case can have eigenvalues that are extremely sensitive to perturbation, and computed
eigenvectors near a defective matrix are essentially meaningless.

For large sparse problems, only a few eigenvalues are usually needed and the matrix is available only
as a function computing its product with a vector. Krylov subspace methods build a subspace from
repeated products and extract approximate eigenvalues from it, converging fastest to the extremes of
the spectrum. The same framework solves linear systems: conjugate gradients for symmetric positive
definite systems and generalized minimal residual methods for the rest, with convergence governed by
the spectrum's distribution rather than by the matrix size.

## Sparse, Structured, and Practical Matters

A sparse matrix has enough zeros that storing and operating on only the nonzeros is worthwhile, and
sparsity changes the algorithmic landscape entirely. Direct factorization of a sparse matrix produces
fill-in — entries that were zero becoming nonzero during elimination — and the amount depends
dramatically on the elimination order. Reordering to minimize fill is a combinatorial problem solved
heuristically, and a good ordering can be the difference between a factorization that fits in memory
and one that does not.

Structured matrices — Toeplitz, circulant, banded, low-rank plus sparse, hierarchical — admit
specialized algorithms that beat general methods by large factors. Recognizing structure is
therefore worth more than any general optimization, and the structures that appear most often arise
from the underlying problem: translation invariance gives Toeplitz structure, local interactions give
banded structure, and smooth kernels give hierarchical low-rank structure.

The practical guidance is consistent across the field. Use established libraries rather than
implementing these algorithms, because the implementations encode decades of accumulated numerical
care that is not visible in the mathematical description. Never form an explicit inverse to solve a
system — factor and solve, which is faster and more accurate. Exploit every structural property the
problem has. And check the result: the residual, the conditioning estimate, and where possible a
known property of the true solution, because a numerical computation that silently produces a wrong
answer is the characteristic failure of the field and it does not announce itself.
