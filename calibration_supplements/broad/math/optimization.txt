## Formulation and Convexity

An optimization problem consists of an objective function to minimize or maximize, a set
of decision variables, and constraints defining the feasible region. Formulation — writing
the real problem in this form — is usually harder and more consequential than solving it,
because a well-formulated problem in a tractable class solves in seconds while a poorly
formulated equivalent may not solve at all.

The single most important structural property is convexity. A set is convex if the
segment between any two of its points lies entirely within it. A function is convex if the
chord between any two points on its graph lies on or above the graph, equivalently if its
Hessian is positive semidefinite where it exists. A convex optimization problem minimizes
a convex function over a convex set, and it has a property no other class shares: every
local minimum is a global minimum. This is what makes convex problems reliably solvable —
a descent method that stops cannot be stuck in a suboptimal basin, because there are
none.

The practical dividing line in optimization is therefore not linear versus nonlinear but
convex versus non-convex. Convex problems of enormous size solve routinely with
guaranteed optimality and verifiable certificates. Non-convex problems of modest size may
have exponentially many local minima and no way to certify that any solution is global.
Recognizing convexity, or reformulating a problem to expose it, is the highest-leverage
skill in the subject.

Convexity-preserving operations, which is how convexity is recognized in practice:
  nonnegative weighted sum      sum of convex functions is convex
  composition with affine       f(Ax + b) is convex if f is
  pointwise maximum             max of convex functions is convex
  composition rules             g(f(x)) convex if g convex nondecreasing, f convex
  partial minimization          inf over one variable of a jointly convex function
  perspective                   t f(x/t) for t > 0
  norms                         every norm is convex
  indicator of a convex set     0 inside, infinity outside; how constraints enter
  log-sum-exp                   convex; the smooth maximum
  negative entropy, negative log  convex on the positive orthant

Common convex functions worth recognizing: affine functions (both convex and concave),
quadratics with positive semidefinite Hessian, exponentials, negative logarithms, all
norms, the maximum eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix, and the log-determinant's negative on
positive definite matrices. Common non-convex functions that trip people up: products of
variables, the zero-norm counting nonzeros, most rank constraints, and anything involving
an equality constraint that is not affine — a nonlinear equality constraint makes the
feasible set non-convex almost automatically, which is why equality constraints are
linearized when possible.

Q: A problem minimizes a convex function subject to the constraint that a variable takes
integer values. The problem is:
A. Convex, since the objective is convex
B. Non-convex, because the integer feasible set is not convex
C. Convex if the objective is also linear
D. Always solvable in polynomial time
Reasoning: Convexity is a property of the problem, requiring both a convex objective and a
convex feasible set. The integers are a discrete set and the segment between two integer
points contains non-integer points, so the feasible set is not convex. This is why integer
programming is NP-hard even with a linear objective. Answer: B.

## Unconstrained Optimization

Gradient descent moves against the gradient, which is the direction of steepest local
decrease. Its convergence rate on a convex quadratic depends on the condition number of
the Hessian — the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue — and degrades badly when that
ratio is large, because the method oscillates across the narrow directions while creeping
along the shallow ones. This is why preconditioning, feature scaling, and adaptive methods
matter so much: they are all ways of improving the effective conditioning.

Newton's method uses second-order information, solving the Newton system to obtain a step
that accounts for curvature. Near a minimum it converges quadratically — the number of
correct digits roughly doubles per iteration — which is dramatically faster than gradient
descent's linear rate. The costs are that forming and factoring the Hessian is O(n^3) in
the dimension, that the Hessian must be positive definite for the step to be a descent
direction, and that far from the optimum the pure Newton step can be terrible. Practical
implementations therefore modify the Hessian to ensure positive definiteness and use a
line search or trust region to control the step.

Quasi-Newton methods build an approximation to the Hessian inverse from successive
gradients, obtaining superlinear convergence without ever computing second derivatives.
BFGS is the standard choice and is the default in most general-purpose optimizers.
Limited-memory BFGS stores only a handful of recent update vectors instead of the full
matrix, reducing memory from O(n^2) to O(n), which makes it the workhorse for
high-dimensional smooth problems.

Method comparison:
  gradient descent      O(n) per step, linear rate, sensitive to conditioning
  momentum / heavy ball damps oscillation across steep directions
  Nesterov acceleration optimal first-order rate for smooth convex problems
  conjugate gradient    exact in n steps for a quadratic, no matrix storage
  Newton                quadratic rate, O(n^3) per step, needs a positive definite
                        Hessian
  Gauss-Newton          for least squares; approximates the Hessian by J^T J,
                        dropping the second-derivative term
  Levenberg-Marquardt   Gauss-Newton with adaptive damping; interpolates toward
                        gradient descent when the model is poor
  BFGS                  superlinear, O(n^2) memory
  L-BFGS                superlinear, O(n) memory; the default for large smooth
                        problems
  trust region          bounds the step where the local model is trusted; more
                        robust than line search on badly behaved problems

Line search and trust region are two answers to the same question of how far to move.
Line search picks a direction then chooses a step length, typically requiring the Wolfe
conditions: sufficient decrease in the objective, and sufficient decrease in the
directional derivative so the step is not too short. Trust region picks a radius within
which the quadratic model is believed accurate, minimizes the model within that radius,
and adjusts the radius based on how well the model predicted the actual decrease. Trust
region methods handle indefinite Hessians and highly nonlinear objectives more gracefully,
which is why they dominate in derivative-free and noisy settings.

Non-smooth problems require different tools. Subgradient methods generalize gradient
descent to functions with kinks, converging slowly at a rate of one over the square root
of the iteration count. Proximal methods split the objective into a smooth part handled by
a gradient step and a non-smooth part handled by a proximal operator, which for the L1
norm is soft thresholding — this is exactly the structure of the lasso and the reason
proximal gradient methods are ubiquitous in sparse learning. The alternating direction
method of multipliers decomposes problems with separable structure into subproblems that
may each be easy, and is the standard approach for large distributed convex problems.

## Constrained Optimization and Duality

Constraints change the character of the problem. At an unconstrained minimum the gradient
vanishes; at a constrained minimum it need not, and instead must be balanced by the
constraint gradients. The Lagrangian formalizes this by adding each constraint multiplied
by a multiplier to the objective, and stationarity of the Lagrangian gives the
first-order conditions.

The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions extend Lagrange multipliers to inequality constraints
and are the central first-order optimality conditions of the subject. Stationarity
requires the objective gradient to be a nonnegative combination of the active constraint
gradients. Primal feasibility requires the constraints to hold. Dual feasibility requires
inequality multipliers to be nonnegative. Complementary slackness requires each
multiplier to be zero unless its constraint is active — a constraint that is not binding
exerts no force. For convex problems satisfying a constraint qualification, KKT is both
necessary and sufficient for global optimality, which turns verification of a solution
into an algebraic check.

Duality is the deepest structural idea here. Every optimization problem has an associated
dual whose variables are the multipliers of the primal constraints, and whose optimal
value lower-bounds the primal minimum. This is weak duality and holds always. Strong
duality — the two values being equal — holds for convex problems under mild conditions
such as Slater's, and it is enormously useful: it provides a certificate of optimality,
often a computationally easier problem to solve, and an economic interpretation where the
multipliers are shadow prices measuring how much the optimum would improve per unit
relaxation of each constraint.

Constrained optimization concepts:
  Lagrangian           L = f(x) + sum lambda_i g_i(x) + sum mu_j h_j(x)
  KKT stationarity     grad_x L = 0
  complementary slack  lambda_i g_i(x) = 0 for each inequality
  dual function        inf over x of the Lagrangian; always concave, whatever the
                       primal
  weak duality         dual optimum <= primal optimum, always
  strong duality       equality; holds for convex problems under Slater's condition
  duality gap          the difference; zero certifies optimality
  shadow price         the multiplier equals the rate of change of the optimum with
                       respect to relaxing that constraint
  active set           constraints holding with equality at the optimum
  penalty method       add a penalty for violation, increase its weight
  barrier / interior   add a term diverging at the boundary; the basis of modern
                       interior-point solvers
  augmented Lagrangian penalty plus multipliers; avoids the ill-conditioning that
                       pure penalty methods suffer as the weight grows

The shadow price interpretation is what makes duality practically valuable outside
mathematics. In a production planning problem, the multiplier on a resource constraint is
the marginal value of an additional unit of that resource, which tells you exactly how
much it would be worth paying for more. A constraint with a zero multiplier is not
binding and more of that resource is worthless. This turns the dual solution into
directly actionable economic information, obtained for free alongside the primal answer.

## Linear and Integer Programming

Linear programming minimizes a linear objective subject to linear constraints. The
feasible region is a polyhedron, the objective's level sets are hyperplanes, and the
optimum — if one exists — is attained at a vertex. This geometric fact is the basis of the
simplex method, which walks from vertex to adjacent vertex along improving edges until no
improvement is available, at which point optimality is certified. Simplex has exponential
worst-case complexity on contrived instances and is extremely fast in practice.
Interior-point methods have polynomial worst-case complexity and traverse the interior;
both are available in every serious solver, and which is faster depends on the problem's
structure and size.

Linear programming is remarkably expressive once a few modelling tricks are known.
Minimizing a maximum is linearized by introducing an auxiliary variable bounded below by
each term. Absolute values are linearized by splitting a variable into positive and
negative parts, or by bounding an auxiliary variable above and below. Piecewise linear
convex functions are represented as maxima of affine pieces. Ratios can sometimes be
handled by the Charnes-Cooper transformation. Recognizing that a problem can be cast as an
LP is often the difference between an instant solution and an intractable one.

Integer programming adds the requirement that some variables take integer values, which
is what allows modelling of discrete decisions — build or do not build, assign or do not
assign, sequence in this order or that. This expressiveness comes at a cost: integer
programming is NP-hard, and the feasible set is not convex, so the reliable machinery of
convex optimization does not apply. Solvers use branch and bound, solving the continuous
relaxation to obtain a bound, branching on a fractional variable, and pruning subtrees
whose bound cannot beat the incumbent, augmented by cutting planes that tighten the
relaxation and by heuristics that find good incumbents early.

Modelling patterns with binary variables:
  either-or             big-M constraints activated by a binary; keep M as tight as
                        possible, since loose M produces weak relaxations and slow
                        solves
  fixed charge          a setup cost incurred only if production is positive
  at most k of n        sum of binaries <= k
  implication           x implies y becomes y >= x for binaries
  logical and / or      products of binaries linearized with auxiliary variables
  piecewise nonconvex   SOS2 constraints or explicit binary segment selection
  assignment            one-to-one matching; the LP relaxation is integral, so
                        this special case solves as an LP
  network flow          totally unimodular constraint matrices give integral LP
                        solutions automatically

Total unimodularity is worth knowing because it identifies the integer problems that are
secretly easy. When the constraint matrix is totally unimodular and the right-hand side is
integral, every vertex of the polyhedron is integral, so solving the linear relaxation
already yields an integer solution. Network flow problems, assignment problems, and
bipartite matching all have this structure, which is why they are solvable in polynomial
time while general integer programming is not.

That is a weak-relaxation problem, and the diagnostics point to a specific cause.

Your largest big-M value is one million. Big-M constraints with loose M produce very weak
LP relaxations — the relaxation can satisfy the constraint by setting the binary to a tiny
fractional value, so it carries almost no information, and branch and bound has nothing to
prune with. Tightening M to the smallest value that is still valid is usually the single
highest-impact change to a MIP model. Compute a genuine upper bound for each constrained
quantity from the data rather than using a safe round number. A reduction from 1e6 to a
realistic bound frequently cuts the root gap by more than half.

Second, scheduling models are usually riddled with symmetry — interchangeable machines,
identical time slots, equivalent job orderings. Symmetric solutions mean branch and bound
explores many equivalent subtrees, each of which must be separately excluded. Adding
symmetry-breaking constraints (lexicographic ordering on identical resources, for example)
prunes those duplicates. Most solvers detect some symmetry automatically, and explicit
constraints usually do better.

Third, and worth doing immediately regardless: set a MIP gap tolerance. If a 2% gap is
acceptable for the business decision, setting that tolerance lets the solver stop when it
reaches it instead of chasing proof of optimality that has no operational value. Six hours
of compute to close a gap nobody will act on is not a good trade.

Two more if those are insufficient. Provide your known-good incumbent as a warm start so
the solver begins with a strong bound. And check whether a reformulation exists — many
scheduling problems have a time-indexed formulation and an alternative
sequence-based or flow-based one with much tighter relaxations, and switching formulation
often beats any amount of solver tuning.

## Stochastic and Global Optimization

Stochastic gradient descent replaces the full gradient with an unbiased estimate from a
random subset of the data. Each step is far cheaper, and the noise it introduces is not
purely harmful: it helps escape saddle points, which dominate high-dimensional non-convex
landscapes, and it acts as an implicit regularizer. Convergence requires the step size to
decrease appropriately — the Robbins-Monro conditions require the step sizes to sum to
infinity while their squares sum to a finite value, which is why a constant step size
converges only to a neighbourhood of the optimum rather than to the optimum itself.

Adaptive methods scale the step per coordinate using accumulated gradient statistics.
AdaGrad divides by the square root of the accumulated squared gradients, which handles
sparse features well and monotonically shrinks the effective step until learning stalls.
RMSProp replaces the accumulation with an exponential moving average, fixing that decay.
Adam adds momentum and bias correction and is the practical default for non-convex deep
learning, though on convex problems well-tuned SGD with momentum often generalizes
slightly better.

Global optimization confronts problems with many local minima and no structural
guarantees. There is no method that both finds the global optimum and terminates quickly
in general — the difficulty is fundamental rather than a gap in current technique. The
practical approaches trade guarantees for usefulness.

Global and derivative-free methods:
  multi-start local     run a local method from many random starts; simple, often
                        surprisingly effective, embarrassingly parallel
  simulated annealing   accept worsening moves with a temperature-dependent
                        probability, cooling over time; converges in theory under
                        impractically slow cooling schedules
  genetic algorithms    population, selection, crossover, mutation; general and
                        usually outperformed by problem-specific methods
  particle swarm        population following personal and global bests
  CMA-ES                adapts a covariance matrix of the search distribution; the
                        strongest general-purpose derivative-free method for
                        continuous problems of moderate dimension
  Bayesian optimization builds a surrogate (usually a Gaussian process) and
                        selects the next evaluation by an acquisition function
                        balancing exploration and exploitation; the right choice
                        when each evaluation is very expensive
  Nelder-Mead           simplex reflection and contraction; no theory, widely used
                        for low-dimensional smooth problems
  branch and bound      exact global optimality for problems with computable bounds

Bayesian optimization deserves particular note because it targets a specific and common
situation: the objective is expensive to evaluate, has no derivatives, and the budget is
perhaps fifty to a few hundred evaluations. Hyperparameter tuning, physical experiment
design, and simulation-based engineering all fit. The surrogate model captures both the
predicted value and the uncertainty, and the acquisition function — expected improvement
being the standard choice — decides where to sample next by weighing likely improvement
against the value of reducing uncertainty. Under a few hundred evaluations it
substantially outperforms random search; beyond a few thousand the surrogate's own cost
and the curse of dimensionality erode its advantage, and random or evolutionary search
becomes competitive again.

The recurring practical advice across all of these is to spend effort on formulation
before algorithm selection. Reformulating a non-convex problem into a convex one,
tightening a relaxation, exploiting separable structure, reducing dimensionality, or
finding a special structure like total unimodularity typically yields improvements of
orders of magnitude, while switching between comparable algorithms yields factors of two
or three. The most common cause of an intractable optimization problem is a formulation
that discarded structure the problem actually had.
