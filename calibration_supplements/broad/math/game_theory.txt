## Representation and Equilibrium

Game theory studies decisions whose outcomes depend on the choices of others, and this
interdependence is what distinguishes it from ordinary optimization. A decision-maker facing a
fixed environment maximizes against it; a decision-maker facing other decision-makers must reason
about what they will do, knowing that they are reasoning about the same thing.

A game is specified by its players, the strategies available to each, and the payoffs each
receives for every combination of strategies. The normal form presents this as a matrix and suits
simultaneous decisions; the extensive form presents it as a tree and represents the sequence of
moves and what each player knows when moving. The same game can be represented either way, and the
extensive form carries information about timing and knowledge that the normal form compresses.

Nash equilibrium is the central solution concept: a strategy profile in which no player can improve
by changing their own strategy while the others hold theirs fixed. It is a consistency condition
rather than a prediction of what will happen — it identifies the outcomes that could be stable
given mutual best responses, and a game may have several equilibria, or an equilibrium may be
worse for everyone than an achievable alternative.

Q: A game has a Nash equilibrium in which both players receive less than they would under a
different strategy profile. This indicates:
A. The equilibrium is computed incorrectly
B. Equilibrium concerns stability against unilateral deviation, not efficiency; the better outcome
   is not stable because at least one player gains by deviating from it
C. The players are irrational
D. The game has no solution
Reasoning: Nash equilibrium requires only that no player improves by changing their own strategy
alone. A jointly better outcome that is not an equilibrium fails because someone gains by
deviating from it, so it cannot be sustained without a mechanism for commitment or enforcement.
The prisoner's dilemma is the canonical instance. Answer: B.

## Classic Games and What They Model

A small number of games recur because they capture recurring strategic structures, and
recognizing which structure a situation has is most of applying the theory.

The prisoner's dilemma has mutual cooperation better for both than mutual defection, and
defection dominant for each individually. The equilibrium is therefore mutual defection, which both
players prefer less than mutual cooperation — a socially inferior outcome produced by individually
rational choices. It models arms races, overfishing, price competition, environmental degradation,
and contributions to a shared resource, and its lesson is that the outcome results from the
incentive structure rather than from any failure of the participants.

Stag hunt has two equilibria: both players cooperating for a high payoff, and both defecting for a
lower but safe one. Unlike the prisoner's dilemma, cooperation is an equilibrium, so the problem is
coordination and trust rather than incentive. The efficient outcome is achievable if each is
confident the other will cooperate, which makes communication, reputation, and assurance mechanisms
effective here where they fail in the prisoner's dilemma.

Chicken has two asymmetric equilibria in which one player yields, and the worst outcome occurs when
neither does. It models brinkmanship and standoffs, and its distinctive feature is that commitment
helps — a player who can credibly remove their own option to yield forces the other to.

Ostrom's finding deserves emphasis because it corrects a conclusion the model was widely taken to
establish. The tragedy of the commons predicts overexploitation absent privatization or external
enforcement, and empirical study of actual commons — fisheries, irrigation systems, forests,
pastures — found many that had been managed sustainably for centuries by the communities using
them. The mechanisms involved clearly defined boundaries, rules matched to local conditions,
participation in rule-making by those affected, monitoring, graduated sanctions, and accessible
conflict resolution. The model is not wrong; it omits the institutions people build, and those
institutions are what change the game.

## Mixed Strategies and Sequential Games

Some games have no equilibrium in pure strategies, and the canonical case is pure conflict where
one player wants to match and the other to mismatch. Whatever either does, the other prefers to
change, so no combination is stable. The resolution is mixed strategies, in which a player
randomizes over their options according to a probability distribution.

The equilibrium mixing probability has a property that is initially counterintuitive: each player
mixes so as to make the other indifferent among their options. This is required for stability — if
one option were better for the opponent, they would take it purely and the first player would
respond, so no equilibrium. The consequence is that a player's equilibrium mixture depends on the
opponent's payoffs rather than on their own, which inverts the usual intuition about optimization.

Sequential games introduce timing, and the solution concept refines Nash equilibrium to require
that the strategies be optimal at every point in the game rather than only overall. Backward
induction computes this by solving the last decision first, substituting its outcome, and working
backward. Subgame perfect equilibrium is the resulting concept, and it eliminates equilibria
sustained by threats that would not actually be carried out.

The credibility of threats is where sequential reasoning most changes conclusions. A threat that
would be costly to execute is not credible, so an opponent reasoning correctly ignores it. Making
a threat credible requires changing the payoffs — a commitment that removes the option not to
follow through, a reputation whose loss is costly, or a contract with enforcement — which is why
apparently irrational commitments can be strategically advantageous.

## Repeated Games, Information, and Signalling

Repeating a game changes it fundamentally because future interaction permits current behaviour to
be conditioned on past behaviour. Cooperation that is unsustainable in a single interaction becomes
sustainable when defection can be punished later, and the folk theorem states this generally: in an
infinitely repeated game with sufficiently patient players, a wide range of outcomes including
cooperative ones can be sustained as equilibria.

The discount factor is what determines whether cooperation is sustainable. A player weighing future
payoffs heavily loses more from triggering punishment than they gain from defecting once, so
cooperation is individually rational. A player weighing them lightly gains more from the immediate
defection. This is why cooperation is more achievable in long-term relationships, why the
prospect of ending a relationship undermines it, and why a known final period unravels
cooperation by backward induction.

Information structure is the other dimension that transforms games. Complete information means the
payoffs are known to all; incomplete information means at least one player has private information
about payoffs or types. The latter is the realistic case and it produces the phenomena that make
strategic interaction interesting — signalling, screening, reputation, and adverse selection.

Signalling occurs when an informed party takes a costly action to convey their private information,
and the signal is credible only if it is differentially costly. An action that a low type could
imitate as cheaply as a high type conveys nothing, since both would take it. Education as a signal
of ability works, in the signalling model, precisely because it is more costly for a less able
person — regardless of whether it makes them more productive.

The tournament findings on repeated prisoner's dilemma deserve mention because they identified
which properties make a strategy successful rather than merely which strategy won. The strategies
that performed well were nice, never defecting first; retaliatory, responding to defection;
forgiving, returning to cooperation after the opponent does; and clear, so the opponent could
recognize the pattern and adapt. The last property is the least obvious and it matters — a strategy
too complex to be recognized cannot elicit cooperation, because the opponent cannot learn that
cooperating is rewarded.

## Mechanism Design and Cooperative Theory

Mechanism design inverts the usual question. Rather than analysing a given game, it asks what game
to construct so that self-interested participants produce a desired outcome. This is the theory
underlying auction design, voting systems, matching markets, and contract structure, and it treats
the rules as the variable.

The revelation principle simplifies the search substantially. It states that any outcome
achievable by some mechanism is achievable by a mechanism in which participants truthfully report
their private information, so the search can be restricted to truthful mechanisms without loss.
This converts an unbounded design space into a tractable one.

Incentive compatibility is the property that truthful reporting is optimal, and designing for it is
what makes a mechanism robust. A mechanism requiring participants to act against their interest
will be gamed, and a mechanism where honesty is optimal does not require enforcement. The second
price auction is the standard illustration: a bidder pays the second-highest bid, and bidding one's
true value is optimal regardless of what others do, so no strategic estimation is needed.

Impossibility results bound what mechanisms can achieve, and they are as informative as the
possibility results. Arrow's theorem establishes that no voting system satisfying a small set of
reasonable conditions exists for three or more options. The Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem
establishes that any non-trivial voting system is manipulable. These do not mean voting systems are
worthless; they mean every system involves a tradeoff, and the choice is which properties to
sacrifice.

Deferred acceptance deserves emphasis because it is a designed mechanism operating at scale with
demonstrable properties. It produces a stable matching — no pair would both prefer each other to
their assigned partners — it is strategy-proof for the proposing side, and it terminates. It is
used for assigning medical graduates to residency programmes, students to schools, and in kidney
exchange chains, and the last of these is a case where mechanism design created a market that did
not previously exist: direct exchange between incompatible donor-recipient pairs is impossible, and
a designed matching mechanism finds chains that make transplants possible that otherwise would not
occur.

The applications of game theory extend across economics, political science, biology, and computer
science, and the biological application is worth noting because it removes the rationality
assumption. Evolutionary game theory replaces deliberate optimization with differential
reproduction: strategies that do better become more common, and an evolutionarily stable strategy
is one that a rare mutant cannot invade. The equilibrium concept survives without any assumption
that the players reason at all, which is a substantial extension of the theory's scope and which
explains why the same mathematics describes animal conflict, signalling, and cooperation as
describes markets.
