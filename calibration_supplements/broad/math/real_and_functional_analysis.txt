## Metric Foundations and Convergence

Analysis is the rigorous study of limits, and the machinery exists because intuitive reasoning
about infinite processes fails in ways that are not visible from the outside. A metric space
abstracts distance to its essential properties — non-negativity with equality only for identical
points, symmetry, and the triangle inequality — and everything about convergence, continuity, and
compactness can be phrased in that language.

Completeness is the property that separates the rationals from the reals and it is the reason
analysis works on the latter. A space is complete when every Cauchy sequence converges within it —
when sequences whose terms become arbitrarily close to each other actually have a limit present. The
rationals fail this: a sequence of rational approximations to the square root of two is Cauchy and
has no rational limit. Completeness is what licenses constructing objects as limits of
approximations, which is the fundamental technique of the subject.

Compactness is the other structural property that does most of the work in proofs. In a metric
space it is equivalent to every sequence having a convergent subsequence, and in finite-dimensional
Euclidean space it coincides with being closed and bounded. In infinite dimensions that equivalence
fails, and the failure is the central difficulty of functional analysis: the closed unit ball is
compact exactly when the space is finite-dimensional, so the extraction of convergent subsequences
that makes finite-dimensional arguments work is simply unavailable.

Q: A sequence of continuous functions converges pointwise to a discontinuous function. This shows:
A. The functions were not really continuous
B. Pointwise convergence does not preserve continuity; uniform convergence is the stronger notion
   that does, and the distinction is precisely why it exists
C. The limit does not exist
D. An error in the calculation
Reasoning: Pointwise convergence controls each point independently with no uniformity in the rate,
so continuity can be lost in the limit. The standard example is powers of x on the unit interval
converging to a function that jumps at the endpoint. Uniform convergence forbids this.
Answer: B.

## Measure and Integration

The Riemann integral partitions the domain and is adequate for continuous functions on intervals.
Its limitations are structural: it handles badly discontinuous functions poorly, it behaves badly
under limits, and the space of Riemann-integrable functions is not complete under the natural
distance. The Lebesgue integral partitions the range instead, asking how much of the domain maps
into each range slice, and this reorganization fixes all three problems.

Making that precise requires measure theory. A measure assigns sizes to sets, countably additively,
on a sigma-algebra — a collection of sets closed under complement and countable union. The
restriction to a sigma-algebra rather than all sets is not a technicality that better mathematics
would remove: non-measurable sets provably exist under the axiom of choice, and any translation-
invariant, countably additive measure defined on all subsets of the line is impossible.

The convergence theorems are what make the Lebesgue theory worth the setup cost. Monotone
convergence permits passing the limit inside the integral for increasing sequences. Dominated
convergence permits it whenever the sequence is bounded by a fixed integrable function. Fatou's
lemma gives an inequality in general. These have no adequate Riemann counterparts, and their
availability is why probability theory, Fourier analysis, and partial differential equations are all
built on Lebesgue integration.

## Banach and Hilbert Spaces

Functional analysis studies spaces whose points are functions, with the linear structure and a norm
compatible with it. A Banach space is a complete normed vector space, and completeness again is
what permits constructing solutions as limits. The characteristic difficulty is that the
finite-dimensional intuitions fail: not all norms are equivalent, bounded sets need not have
convergent subsequences, and linear maps need not be continuous.

A Hilbert space adds an inner product inducing the norm, and this single addition recovers most of
the geometry. Orthogonality becomes meaningful, projection onto a closed subspace exists and is
unique, and every element has an expansion in an orthonormal basis with coefficients given by
inner products. The space of square-integrable functions is the canonical example, and Fourier
series are exactly the orthonormal expansion in it, which is why the Hilbert space framework
explains rather than merely accommodates classical Fourier theory.

The three foundational theorems of the subject are all consequences of completeness and each
converts a pointwise hypothesis into a uniform conclusion. The uniform boundedness principle says a
family of operators bounded at each point is uniformly bounded. The open mapping theorem says a
surjective bounded operator is open, from which the bounded inverse theorem follows. The closed
graph theorem says an operator with a closed graph is bounded. Their common character — local
information forced into global conclusions by completeness — is worth recognizing.

## Operators and Spectral Theory

A linear operator between normed spaces is bounded exactly when it is continuous, and the two words
are used interchangeably for this reason. The operator norm measures the largest factor by which
the operator can stretch a vector, and the bounded operators on a space form an algebra under
composition — a structure that carries much of the theory.

The spectrum generalizes the eigenvalue set. In infinite dimensions an operator can fail to be
invertible without having an eigenvector, so the spectrum is defined as the set of scalars for
which the operator minus that scalar is not invertible, and it decomposes into point, continuous,
and residual parts. For a compact operator the spectrum is discrete apart from zero and the
finite-dimensional intuition largely survives, which is why compact operators are the tractable
class.

The spectral theorem is the central structural result. A self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space
has real spectrum and admits a representation as multiplication by a real function on an
appropriate measure space, which is the infinite-dimensional statement of diagonalization. For
compact self-adjoint operators the statement is concrete: an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
exists with real eigenvalues accumulating only at zero. This underlies the mode decompositions of
vibrating systems, the eigenfunction expansions solving partial differential equations, and the
observable structure of quantum mechanics.

## Applications and Practical Consequences

The abstract machinery pays off in specific places, and knowing where prevents the subject from
seeming like formalism for its own sake. Existence proofs for differential equations are the
clearest case: the contraction mapping principle on a complete space gives existence and uniqueness
for ordinary differential equations directly, and variational methods in Sobolev spaces give weak
solutions for elliptic partial differential equations where classical solutions may not exist.

The notion of a weak solution is itself an important conceptual move. Rather than requiring an
equation to hold pointwise with all derivatives existing, it is required to hold after integration
against smooth test functions. This admits solutions with shocks, corners, and discontinuities that
the classical formulation excludes, and it matches the physical situations — a shock wave is a real
phenomenon and the classical formulation simply has nothing to say about it.

In applied and computational work the relevant consequences are about approximation and stability.
Choosing a function space determines what convergence means for a numerical method, and a method
converging in one norm may diverge in another. Ill-posedness — where the solution does not depend
continuously on the data — is an operator-theoretic statement about the inverse being unbounded,
and regularization is the standard response: replace the unbounded inverse with a family of bounded
approximations and choose the parameter by the noise level.
