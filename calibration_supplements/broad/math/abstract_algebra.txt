## Groups

Abstract algebra studies structures defined by operations satisfying axioms, and the payoff is
generality: a theorem proved from the axioms applies to every structure satisfying them,
however different they appear. The integers under addition, the nonzero rationals under
multiplication, the symmetries of a square, the invertible matrices, and the permutations of a
set are all groups, and every group theorem applies to all of them at once.

A group is a set with an associative binary operation, an identity element, and inverses for
every element. Commutativity is not required, and its absence is where most of the subject's
interest lies — abelian groups are well understood and classified, while non-abelian groups
support the rich structure that makes group theory a deep field. The symmetries of a physical
object form a group precisely because composing symmetries gives a symmetry, doing nothing is a
symmetry, and every symmetry can be undone.

The order of a group is its cardinality, and the order of an element is the least positive
power giving the identity. Lagrange's theorem states that the order of any subgroup divides the
order of the group, which is one of the most useful facts in the subject because it immediately
constrains what subgroups can exist. A group of prime order has no proper nontrivial subgroups
at all and is therefore cyclic, generated by any non-identity element.

Cayley's theorem is worth noting for what it says about the subject's scope. Every group,
however abstractly defined, is isomorphic to a group of permutations of some set. This means
group theory is in a precise sense the study of symmetry — the axioms capture exactly the
structure of reversible transformations composing associatively, and nothing that satisfies
them fails to be realizable that way.

Q: A group has order 15. What can be concluded about its subgroups without further
information?
A. It has subgroups of every order
B. Any proper nontrivial subgroup has order 3 or 5, since a subgroup's order must divide 15
C. It has no subgroups
D. It must be non-abelian
Reasoning: Lagrange's theorem restricts subgroup orders to divisors of the group order, which
for 15 are 1, 3, 5, and 15. Cauchy's theorem further guarantees that subgroups of order 3 and 5
exist, since 3 and 5 are primes dividing 15. In fact every group of order 15 is cyclic, which
follows from the Sylow theorems. Answer: B.

## Subgroups, Homomorphisms, and Quotients

A homomorphism is a map between groups preserving the operation, and it is the right notion of
structural comparison because it transports the algebraic structure rather than merely the
elements. An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism, and two isomorphic groups are the same
group in different notation — every group-theoretic statement true of one is true of the other.

The kernel of a homomorphism is the set of elements mapping to the identity, and it measures
how far the map is from injective: the map is injective exactly when the kernel is trivial. The
image is what the map reaches, and it is a subgroup of the target. The kernel is not merely a
subgroup but a normal one, meaning it is invariant under conjugation, and this turns out to
characterize kernels completely — a subgroup is the kernel of some homomorphism exactly when it
is normal.

That characterization is what makes quotient groups possible. The cosets of a normal subgroup
can be given a group structure in a well-defined way, producing the quotient group, and the
construction fails for non-normal subgroups because the operation on cosets is not well defined.
The quotient collapses the subgroup to a point and retains the structure that survives, which is
the algebraic analogue of ignoring a distinction that does not matter.

The first isomorphism theorem states that the image of a homomorphism is isomorphic to the
domain modulo the kernel, which is the central structural result of the subject. It says that
every homomorphism factors as a quotient followed by an inclusion, so understanding
homomorphisms out of a group reduces to understanding its normal subgroups.

  injective iff        the kernel is trivial

  normal subgroup      invariant under conjugation; exactly the possible kernels
  quotient group       cosets of a normal subgroup with induced operation

  composition series   successive quotients that are simple; the Jordan-Holder

  classification of finite simple groups  complete; eighteen infinite families

                       Monster

The classification of finite simple groups deserves mention as one of mathematics' largest
collaborative achievements. Simple groups are those with no nontrivial normal subgroups, and
every finite group decomposes into them in a sense made precise by the Jordan-Holder theorem —
so classifying them classifies the building blocks of all finite groups. The proof spans tens
of thousands of pages across hundreds of papers by over a hundred authors, and the effort to
produce a coherent verified second-generation proof continues decades after the classification
was first announced.

## Group Actions

A group action formalizes a group operating on a set, assigning to each group element a
permutation of the set in a way compatible with the group operation. This is where group theory
connects to everything else, because symmetry in any context is a group acting on the objects
being symmetric.

The orbit of a point is everything it can be moved to, and the stabilizer is the subgroup fixing
it. The orbit-stabilizer theorem states that the orbit's size times the stabilizer's order
equals the group's order, which converts counting problems into structural ones and is the
workhorse of combinatorial applications. Counting the rotational symmetries of a cube, for
instance, follows immediately: the group acts transitively on the six faces, and the stabilizer
of a face is the four rotations about the axis through it, giving twenty-four.

Burnside's lemma counts orbits by averaging the number of fixed points over the group, which
answers the recurring question of how many configurations there are up to symmetry. How many
distinct necklaces can be made from beads of two colours, counting rotations as identical? How
many distinct ways can a cube's faces be coloured? Each is an orbit count under a symmetry group,
and the lemma provides it without enumeration.

  orbit                everything a point can be moved to
  stabilizer           the subgroup fixing a point
  orbit-stabilizer     orbit size times stabilizer order equals group order
  transitive           one orbit; every point reachable from every other
  faithful             only the identity acts trivially
  conjugation          the group acting on itself; orbits are conjugacy classes
  class equation       the group order as a sum over conjugacy classes; proves

  Burnside's lemma     orbit count is the average number of fixed points
  Polya enumeration    the generating-function refinement; counts colourings by
                       composition
  Cayley graph         the group as a graph; vertices are elements, edges are
                       generators
  representation       an action on a vector space by linear maps; converts group
                       theory into linear algebra

Representation theory deserves emphasis because it is how group theory is applied in physics
and chemistry. A representation is an action on a vector space by invertible linear maps, which
turns abstract group elements into matrices and group-theoretic questions into linear algebra.
The irreducible representations of a symmetry group determine which quantum transitions are
allowed, which molecular vibrational modes exist and how they transform, and how a degenerate
energy level splits when a symmetry is broken — results obtained from the group structure alone
without solving any equation of motion.

## Rings and Fields

A ring has two operations, addition making it an abelian group and multiplication that is
associative and distributes over addition. The integers are the archetype, and the ring axioms
capture what is needed to do arithmetic. Multiplication need not commute and need not have
inverses, and adding those requirements gives progressively more structured objects.

An integral domain is a commutative ring with identity and no zero divisors, meaning a product
of nonzero elements is nonzero. This is exactly the condition that permits cancellation, which
is why it can be assumed in ordinary algebra with integers and cannot in the integers modulo a
composite number — where two times three is zero modulo six, so cancelling a factor is invalid.

A field is a commutative ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse,
which is what permits division. The rationals, reals, complexes, and integers modulo a prime are
fields; the integers are not, because most elements lack inverses. Fields are where linear
algebra lives, since the construction of vector spaces requires dividing by scalars.

Ideals play the role in ring theory that normal subgroups play in group theory: they are
exactly the kernels of ring homomorphisms and exactly the subsets by which one can quotient. The
quotient of a ring by an ideal is a ring, and the character of the quotient reflects the ideal's
properties — quotienting by a prime ideal gives an integral domain, and quotienting by a maximal
ideal gives a field.

  integral domain      no zero divisors; cancellation is valid
  field                every nonzero element is invertible

                       the converse fails

  maximal ideal        quotient is a field

  transcendental       not algebraic; pi and e over the rationals

The uniqueness of finite fields is worth stating as a result because it is unusually strong.
For every prime power there is exactly one field of that order up to isomorphism, and there is no
field whose order is not a prime power. This complete classification, with no exceptions and no
families to enumerate, is rare in mathematics and it is what makes finite fields so usable in
applications — a construction can rely on the field of a given order being the field of that
order.

## Galois Theory and Applications

Galois theory establishes a correspondence between field extensions and groups, and it is the
subject's most celebrated achievement. To a polynomial one associates the group of symmetries
of its roots — permutations preserving all algebraic relations among them — and the structure of
that group determines what can be done with the polynomial.

The fundamental theorem of Galois theory states that for a suitable extension there is an
inclusion-reversing bijection between intermediate fields and subgroups of the Galois group.
Questions about fields, which are infinite objects, become questions about finite groups, which
are combinatorial. This is a template that recurs throughout modern mathematics: translate a
problem into a category where the tools are stronger.

The consequences settled questions that had been open for centuries. A polynomial is solvable by
radicals exactly when its Galois group is solvable in the group-theoretic sense, and the
symmetric group on five elements is not solvable, which is why no general formula exists for the
roots of a quintic. The classical construction problems — trisecting an angle, doubling a cube,
squaring a circle — are impossible with compass and straightedge because such constructions
generate only field extensions of degree a power of two, and the required extensions have degree
three or are transcendental.

The applications deserve a closing observation about why they work. Abstract algebra studies
structure independent of what the elements are, so a theorem about groups applies wherever
group structure appears — and it appears wherever there is symmetry, reversibility, or
composition. That is why the same theory constrains which polynomials can be solved, which
geometric constructions are possible, which crystal structures exist, which particle interactions
are permitted, and which error-correcting codes can be built. The generality that makes the
subject feel abstract is precisely what makes it applicable, and the applications were in every
case discovered after the abstraction rather than motivating it.
