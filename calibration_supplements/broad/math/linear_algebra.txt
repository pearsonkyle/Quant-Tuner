## Vector Spaces and Linear Maps

A vector space over a field is a set closed under addition and scalar multiplication
satisfying the eight axioms — associativity and commutativity of addition, an additive
identity and inverses, and the compatibility and distributivity conditions for scalars.
The abstraction is worth the trouble because it applies far beyond arrows in space:
polynomials of degree at most n form a vector space of dimension n+1, continuous
functions on an interval form an infinite-dimensional one, and the solutions of a
homogeneous linear differential equation form a subspace. Everything proved about
vector spaces applies verbatim to all of them.

A basis is a linearly independent spanning set, and every basis of a given space has
the same cardinality, which is the dimension. Coordinates are always relative to a
basis: the vector exists independently, but its list of numbers does not. This
distinction is the source of most confusion about change of basis and about tensors.
If B is the matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors expressed in the old
coordinates, then a vector's new coordinates are B^{-1} x, and a linear map's matrix
transforms as B^{-1} A B — a similarity transformation. Quantities that are invariant
under similarity, such as the trace, determinant, rank, and eigenvalues, are
properties of the map itself rather than of any coordinate choice, which is exactly
why they matter.

Every matrix A of size m by n induces four fundamental subspaces, and their
relationships are the core structural theorem of the subject. The column space (range)
lives in R^m and has dimension r, the rank. The null space (kernel) lives in R^n and
has dimension n - r. The row space lives in R^n with dimension r, and the left null
space lives in R^m with dimension m - r. The rank-nullity theorem, rank + nullity = n,
is just the statement that the domain splits into the part that survives the map and
the part that is annihilated. The orthogonality relations are equally important: the
row space and null space are orthogonal complements in R^n, and the column space and
left null space are orthogonal complements in R^m.

The four subspaces summarized:
  column space C(A)      subset of R^m,  dim r,      spanned by pivot columns
  null space N(A)        subset of R^n,  dim n - r,  solutions of Ax = 0
  row space C(A^T)       subset of R^n,  dim r,      orthogonal complement of N(A)
  left null space N(A^T) subset of R^m,  dim m - r,  orthogonal complement of C(A)

The solvability of Ax = b follows immediately: a solution exists if and only if b lies
in the column space, and it is unique if and only if the null space is trivial.
Overdetermined systems (m > n) usually have no exact solution and are handled by least
squares; underdetermined systems (m < n) have infinitely many and are usually resolved
by choosing the minimum-norm one. Both are special cases of the pseudoinverse.

Q: A is a 7 by 4 matrix with rank 3. The dimension of its null space and the
dimension of its left null space are:
A. 1 and 4   B. 3 and 3   C. 1 and 3   D. 4 and 1
Reasoning: Null space dimension is n - r = 4 - 3 = 1. Left null space dimension is
m - r = 7 - 3 = 4. Answer: A.

Q: Ax = b has a 7-by-4 coefficient matrix of rank 4 and b is not in the column space.
The system has:
A. Exactly one solution
B. Infinitely many solutions
C. No exact solution, but a unique least-squares solution
D. No solution and no least-squares solution
Reasoning: Since b is not in C(A) there is no exact solution. Full column rank means
A^T A is invertible, so the normal equations have a unique solution — the unique point
minimizing the residual norm. Answer: C.

## Matrix Operations and Structure

Matrix multiplication composes linear maps, and its non-commutativity is a feature
rather than a defect: rotating then translating differs from translating then
rotating. The associativity AB(C) = A(BC) is what makes the order of operations a
performance decision. Multiplying a 1000-by-1 vector chain in the wrong order can cost
orders of magnitude more arithmetic — computing (A B) x for square A and B costs
O(n^3), while A (B x) costs O(n^2). This is the entire content of matrix chain
optimization and it appears constantly in machine learning code, where a low-rank
factor should always be applied to the activation rather than reconstituted into a
full matrix first.

Special structures each buy computational savings. A diagonal matrix scales each
coordinate independently, so multiplication is O(n) and inversion is elementwise
reciprocal. A triangular matrix is solved by forward or back substitution in O(n^2)
rather than O(n^3), which is why decompositions aim at triangular factors. An
orthogonal matrix satisfies Q^T Q = I, so its inverse is its transpose, it preserves
lengths and angles, and its condition number is exactly one — numerically the best
possible object to multiply by. A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an
orthonormal eigenbasis. A positive definite matrix has all eigenvalues positive,
admits a Cholesky factorization at half the cost of LU, and defines a genuine inner
product.

The determinant measures signed volume scaling. It is zero exactly when the map
collapses dimension, which is exactly when the matrix is singular. Useful properties:
det(AB) = det(A)det(B), det(A^T) = det(A), det(A^{-1}) = 1/det(A), and the determinant
of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal. The last of these is how
determinants are actually computed — factor into triangular form and multiply the
diagonal — because cofactor expansion costs O(n!) and is unusable beyond about n = 4.
For numerical work the log-determinant is preferred, since determinants of large
matrices overflow or underflow catastrophically; the log of a positive definite
matrix's determinant is twice the sum of the logs of the Cholesky diagonal.

The trace is the sum of the diagonal, and its cyclic property tr(ABC) = tr(BCA) =
tr(CAB) is the single most useful identity for matrix calculus. It converts scalar
expressions into forms where derivatives are readable, and it lets you evaluate
tr(A B) for a tall-thin A and short-wide B in O(mn) without ever forming the m-by-m
product. The trace equals the sum of the eigenvalues, just as the determinant equals
their product, which gives two cheap sanity checks on any eigen-computation.

Matrix calculus identities, with the denominator-layout convention:
  d(a^T x)/dx        = a
  d(x^T A x)/dx      = (A + A^T) x,  and 2Ax when A is symmetric
  d(tr(AX))/dX       = A^T
  d(tr(X^T A X))/dX  = (A + A^T) X
  d(log det X)/dX    = X^{-T}
  d(X^{-1})/dt       = -X^{-1} (dX/dt) X^{-1}
  d||Ax - b||^2/dx   = 2 A^T (Ax - b)

That last line is the gradient of the least-squares objective, and setting it to zero
gives the normal equations A^T A x = A^T b directly. It is worth deriving once and
remembering, because it appears in linear regression, in the Gauss-Newton method, in
Kalman filtering, and in the inner loop of essentially every optimization over linear
models.

## Decompositions

Matrix decompositions factor a matrix into structured pieces that make a problem easy.
Choosing the right one is most of applied linear algebra.

LU decomposition writes A = LU with L unit lower triangular and U upper triangular,
computed by Gaussian elimination. With partial pivoting for stability it becomes PA =
LU. It costs about (2/3)n^3 operations and is the standard method for solving general
square systems: factor once at O(n^3), then each additional right-hand side costs only
O(n^2) via two triangular solves. This is why you never compute an explicit inverse to
solve a system — the inverse costs more, is less accurate, and the subsequent
multiplication is no cheaper than a triangular solve.

Cholesky decomposition writes a symmetric positive definite A as LL^T, at half the
cost of LU and with guaranteed stability requiring no pivoting. It is the workhorse
for covariance matrices, Gaussian process kernels, normal equations, and the Hessian
systems inside second-order optimizers. Its failure is informative: the algorithm
breaks when it tries to take the square root of a non-positive pivot, so a failed
Cholesky is a positive-definiteness test. In practice near-singular kernel matrices are
regularized by adding a small multiple of the identity — jitter, or ridge, or
Tikhonov damping depending on the field — which lifts every eigenvalue and restores
factorability.

QR decomposition writes A = QR with Q orthogonal and R upper triangular. Computed by
Householder reflections (stable, the default) or Gram-Schmidt (intuitive, numerically
poor in its classical form, acceptable when modified and reorthogonalized). QR is the
numerically sound way to solve least squares: rather than forming A^T A, which squares
the condition number, solve R x = Q^T b directly. For an ill-conditioned design matrix
this is the difference between a usable answer and numerical noise.

Eigendecomposition writes A = V Lambda V^{-1} when a full set of independent
eigenvectors exists. For symmetric matrices this specializes to A = Q Lambda Q^T with
orthogonal Q and real eigenvalues — the spectral theorem — which is the well-behaved
case that covers covariance matrices, graph Laplacians, and Hessians. Non-symmetric
matrices may be defective, lacking a complete eigenbasis, in which case the Jordan
form is the theoretical answer and is numerically useless because it is discontinuous
in the entries; the Schur decomposition A = Q T Q^T with upper triangular T always
exists and is what numerical libraries actually compute.

The singular value decomposition writes any m-by-n matrix as A = U Sigma V^T with U
and V orthogonal and Sigma diagonal with non-negative entries in decreasing order. It
always exists, for any shape and any rank, which makes it the most general and most
useful decomposition. The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of
A^T A; the number of nonzero ones is the rank; the ratio of largest to smallest is the
condition number. The Eckart-Young theorem states that truncating to the top k
singular triplets gives the best rank-k approximation in both the Frobenius and
spectral norms, which is the mathematical foundation of principal component analysis,
latent semantic analysis, low-rank adaptation of neural networks, and image
compression.

Decomposition selection guide:
  general square solve                   LU with partial pivoting
  symmetric positive definite solve      Cholesky
  least squares, well-conditioned        QR (Householder)
  least squares, rank-deficient          SVD or column-pivoted QR
  symmetric eigenproblem                 QR algorithm / divide-and-conquer
  general eigenproblem                   Schur (Hessenberg + QR iteration)
  low-rank approximation                 truncated SVD, or randomized SVD at scale
  a few extreme eigenpairs, sparse A     Lanczos (symmetric) / Arnoldi (general)
  nonneg data, parts-based factors       NMF (no unique solution; not a rotation)

The algorithm is short. Draw a random Gaussian matrix Omega of shape 4000 by 60 (your
50 components plus 10 oversampling, which is what makes the tail accurate). Form Y =
A Omega, a 200,000 by 60 sketch. Orthonormalize it with a QR factorization to get Q,
then form the small matrix B = Q^T A of shape 60 by 4,000 and take its exact SVD,
which is trivial at that size. The left singular vectors of A are Q times those of B.

Two practical notes. Add two power iterations — replace Y with A (A^T Y) before the QR
— because the singular value spectrum of real data usually decays slowly, and without
them the subspace captured by the sketch is contaminated by the tail. Reorthonormalize
between power iterations or you will lose the gains to round-off.

Also centre the columns before you start, since PCA is the SVD of the *centred*
matrix; skipping that gives you the components of the uncentred second-moment matrix,
whose first component is essentially the mean vector. If memory is tight, centring
implicitly by subtracting the mean inside the matrix-vector products avoids
materializing a second copy. Expect around 3 GB peak instead of 13 GB.

## Eigenstructure and Its Applications

An eigenvector is a direction the map preserves, scaled by its eigenvalue: Av =
lambda v. The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial det(A -
lambda I) = 0, which is a fine definition and a terrible algorithm — polynomial
rooting is ill-conditioned, and the practical methods (QR iteration on a Hessenberg
form) never form the polynomial at all.

Diagonalization is what makes eigenstructure useful. If A = V Lambda V^{-1}, then A^k
= V Lambda^k V^{-1}, so raising a matrix to a power reduces to raising numbers to a
power. This immediately solves linear recurrences, discrete dynamical systems, and
Markov chains: the long-run behaviour is governed by the largest-magnitude eigenvalue,
and the system converges, oscillates, or diverges according to whether the spectral
radius is below, on, or above the unit circle. The same idea gives the matrix
exponential exp(At) = V exp(Lambda t) V^{-1}, which solves the linear system of
differential equations dx/dt = Ax in closed form and underlies stability analysis in
control theory — the system is stable exactly when every eigenvalue has negative real
part.

For symmetric matrices the picture is especially clean. The spectral theorem
guarantees real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis, so a symmetric matrix is a
pure rescaling along perpendicular axes. The Rayleigh quotient x^T A x / x^T x is
bounded by the smallest and largest eigenvalues and attains them at the corresponding
eigenvectors, which turns eigenvalue computation into an optimization problem and is
the basis of both the power method and of many spectral bounds. The Courant-Fischer
min-max theorem generalizes this to all the intermediate eigenvalues.

Positive definiteness — all eigenvalues strictly positive — is the matrix analogue of
a positive number, and it appears wherever a quadratic form must be a genuine measure.
A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite by construction, since x^T Sigma x is the
variance of a projection and variances are non-negative. A Hessian that is positive
definite at a critical point certifies a local minimum; indefinite means a saddle,
which is the generic situation in high-dimensional non-convex optimization and the
reason saddle points, not local minima, are the real obstacle in deep learning. Tests
for positive definiteness in order of practicality: attempt a Cholesky factorization,
check that all eigenvalues are positive, or check that all leading principal minors
are positive.

Principal component analysis is the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix, or
equivalently the SVD of the centred data. The eigenvectors are the directions of
maximal variance, mutually orthogonal, and the eigenvalues are the variance captured
along each. The proportion of total variance explained by the top k is the ratio of
their eigenvalue sum to the trace. Two cautions that catch people repeatedly: PCA is
not scale-invariant, so features in different units must be standardized first or the
components will simply track whichever variable has the largest numerical range; and
the components are directions of variance, not of predictive relevance, so discarding
low-variance components can discard exactly the signal that predicts the target.

D. The product of all three
Reasoning: A stochastic matrix always has eigenvalue 1, whose eigenvector is the

governed by the second-largest eigenvalue in absolute value, here 0.7, so the error
decays like 0.7^n. This quantity, the spectral gap 1 - 0.7 = 0.3, is what determines

A. Positive definite   B. Positive semidefinite   C. Indefinite   D. Negative definite
Reasoning: Positive definite requires every eigenvalue strictly positive; semidefinite
allows zeros but not negatives. With both positive and negative eigenvalues the
quadratic form takes both signs, so the matrix is indefinite — geometrically a saddle.

## Least Squares, Conditioning, and Numerical Reality

The least-squares problem minimizes ||Ax - b||^2 over x. Setting the gradient to zero
gives the normal equations A^T A x = A^T b, whose solution is x = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b
when A has full column rank. Geometrically, the residual is orthogonal to the column
space, so Ax is the orthogonal projection of b onto C(A), and the projection matrix is
P = A(A^T A)^{-1}A^T, which satisfies P^2 = P and P^T = P — the defining properties of
an orthogonal projector.

The normal equations are correct mathematics and poor numerics. Forming A^T A squares
the condition number, so a design matrix with condition number 10^8 — entirely
ordinary when predictors are correlated or badly scaled — yields a normal-equation
system with condition number 10^16, which is the reciprocal of double-precision
machine epsilon. All accuracy is gone. Solving via QR keeps the conditioning at
kappa(A) rather than kappa(A)^2 and costs only about twice as much. Every serious
library does this, which is why `numpy.linalg.lstsq` does not implement the textbook
formula.

Condition number quantifies how much a relative perturbation in the input can be
amplified in the output: kappa(A) = sigma_max / sigma_min for the 2-norm. A system
with kappa = 10^k loses roughly k digits of accuracy. Since double precision carries
about 16 digits, kappa above 10^12 or so means the answer is mostly noise. The
diagnosis matters more than the number: ill-conditioning usually signals that the
columns are nearly linearly dependent, meaning the data does not actually determine
the parameters, and no algorithm can invent information the data lacks. The honest
responses are to regularize (ridge adds lambda I, lifting sigma_min and capping the
condition number), to reduce dimension (truncated SVD discards the directions the data
does not constrain), or to collect data that breaks the degeneracy.

The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse A^+ = V Sigma^+ U^T, where Sigma^+ inverts the nonzero
singular values and transposes, unifies all these cases. It gives the exact solution
when one exists, the least-squares solution when the system is overdetermined, and the
minimum-norm least-squares solution when it is rank-deficient. Truncating the small
singular values before inverting — treating anything below a tolerance as zero — is
regularization by another name, and choosing that tolerance is the same decision as
choosing a ridge parameter.

Numerical rules of thumb:
  never invert to solve            factor and do triangular solves
  never form A^T A                 use QR, or SVD if rank-deficient
  prefer orthogonal transforms     Householder/Givens have condition number 1
  scale columns to similar norms   before any decomposition or penalty
  use log-determinant              via Cholesky diagonal, not det()
  check residuals, not just x      ||Ax - b|| is what you minimized
  fp32 gives ~7 digits             fp64 ~16; kappa consumes them directly
  iterative refinement             one extra residual solve can recover digits

For large sparse systems, direct factorization is often infeasible because the factors
fill in — L and U acquire nonzeros where A had none, sometimes catastrophically —
and iterative methods take over. Conjugate gradients solves symmetric positive
definite systems using only matrix-vector products, converging in a number of
iterations governed by sqrt(kappa), which is why preconditioning matters so much:
a preconditioner M approximating A but cheap to invert transforms the problem into one
with a far smaller effective condition number. GMRES handles general nonsymmetric
systems. In both cases the matrix never needs to be stored explicitly, only applied,
which is what makes them the methods of choice for discretized PDEs and for the
implicit Hessian products in large-scale optimization.
