## Estimation and the Properties of Estimators

Statistical theory studies procedures for drawing conclusions from data under a probability model,
and its central objects are estimators — functions of the data used to infer something about the
model. The theory's value is that it says which estimators are good, in what sense, and under what
conditions, which converts a menu of plausible-seeming procedures into an ordered comparison.

The basic properties are stated relative to repeated sampling from the model. An estimator is
unbiased when its expected value equals the quantity estimated. It is consistent when it converges
to that quantity as the sample grows. It is efficient when its variance is as small as achievable
among a class. These are distinct and none implies the others: an unbiased estimator can be
inconsistent, a consistent estimator can be badly biased in small samples, and the estimator with
the smallest mean squared error is frequently biased.

That last point deserves emphasis because it contradicts a widespread intuition. Mean squared error
decomposes into squared bias plus variance, so accepting some bias in exchange for a larger variance
reduction lowers the total error. Shrinkage estimators exploit this deliberately, and the striking
theoretical result is that in three or more dimensions the obvious unbiased estimator of a normal
mean vector is inadmissible — a shrunken estimator has smaller risk everywhere. Unbiasedness is a
property, not a virtue.

Estimation properties:
  estimator            a function of the data used to infer a quantity
  bias                 the difference between expectation and truth
  consistency          convergence to the truth as the sample grows
  efficiency           minimal variance within a class
  the independence     none of these implies the others
  mean squared error   squared bias plus variance
  the bias-variance tradeoff  accepting bias to reduce variance lowers total error
  shrinkage            deliberately biased toward a target; often better
  the inadmissibility result  in three or more dimensions, the obvious unbiased
                       estimator of a normal mean is dominated everywhere
  the lesson           unbiasedness is a property to be traded, not preserved
  standard error       the estimator's own standard deviation
  robustness           sensitivity to model misspecification and to outliers
  breakdown point      the fraction of contaminated data an estimator tolerates;
                       the mean has zero, the median has one half
  influence function   how much a single observation can move the estimate

Q: An estimator with small bias and large variance is compared with one having moderate bias and
very small variance. The second has lower mean squared error. The correct conclusion is:
A. The first estimator is better because it is less biased
B. The second is better for this criterion; mean squared error combines squared bias and variance,
   and reducing variance can more than compensate for added bias
C. Bias makes an estimator invalid
D. They cannot be compared
Reasoning: Unbiasedness is one desirable property among several and it is not the objective unless
the loss function makes it so. Under squared error loss, the decomposition makes the tradeoff
explicit, and shrinkage methods exploit it systematically. Answer: B.

## Likelihood, Sufficiency, and Information

The likelihood function is the probability of the observed data viewed as a function of the
parameter, and it is the central object of parametric inference. It is not a probability
distribution over the parameter — it does not integrate to one in general — and treating it as one
is the most common conceptual error in the area. What it does is rank parameter values by how well
they explain the data.

Maximum likelihood estimation selects the parameter maximizing this function, and it has excellent
properties under regularity conditions: consistency, asymptotic normality, asymptotic efficiency,
and invariance to reparameterization. The regularity conditions matter and are frequently violated
in practice — at boundary values, when the parameter count grows with the sample, and when the
support depends on the parameter — and in those cases the standard asymptotic results simply do not
apply.

Sufficiency identifies when a statistic captures all the information in the data about a parameter.
A sufficient statistic is one such that the conditional distribution of the data given it does not
depend on the parameter, so nothing further can be learned from the raw data once it is known. The
factorization criterion makes sufficiency checkable directly from the likelihood's form, and the
exponential family is precisely the class where a sufficient statistic of fixed dimension exists
regardless of sample size, which is why that family recurs throughout the theory.

  likelihood           the data's probability as a function of the parameter
  not a distribution over parameters  the standard conceptual error
  maximum likelihood   the maximizing parameter value
  the good properties  consistency, asymptotic normality and efficiency,

  regularity conditions  interior optimum, fixed dimension, support independent
                       of the parameter
  the failure cases    boundaries, growing dimension, parameter-dependent support
  score function       the derivative of the log likelihood; zero at the maximum
  Fisher information   the expected curvature; how sharply the data distinguishes
                       parameter values
  Cramér-Rao bound     a lower bound on unbiased estimator variance, given by the
                       inverse information
  observed information  the curvature at the estimate; often preferred for
                       standard errors in practice
  sufficiency          a statistic capturing all parameter information
  factorization criterion  sufficiency checkable from the likelihood's form
  minimal sufficiency  the coarsest sufficient statistic

                       the theory of when is genuinely subtle

## Hypothesis Testing Theory

A hypothesis test partitions the sample space into regions where a null hypothesis is rejected and
where it is not, and the theory characterizes which partitions are good. The two error types are
asymmetric by construction: type one is rejecting a true null, controlled at a chosen level; type
two is failing to reject a false null, whose complement is power. The asymmetry encodes a decision
that false positives and false negatives have different costs, which is a modelling choice rather
than a mathematical necessity.

The Neyman-Pearson lemma establishes that for testing one simple hypothesis against another, the
likelihood ratio test is most powerful at any level. This is the theoretical anchor of the subject —
it says the optimal test is determined, not chosen — and extending it to composite hypotheses is
what most of testing theory is about. Uniformly most powerful tests exist for some families with
monotone likelihood ratios and frequently do not exist at all, in which case restricted optimality
criteria such as unbiasedness or invariance are imposed to select a test.

The p-value is the probability, under the null, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one
observed. It is not the probability that the null is true, it is not the probability the result
occurred by chance, and it does not measure effect size. Its correct use requires that the test was
specified before seeing the data, since selecting a hypothesis after examining the data invalidates
the calculation entirely — the sampling distribution being referred to is not the one that was
actually sampled from.

Testing theory:
  null and alternative  the partition being tested
  type one error       rejecting a true null; controlled at a chosen level
  type two error       failing to reject a false null
  power                one minus the type two error rate
  the asymmetry        a decision about relative costs, not a mathematical fact
  Neyman-Pearson lemma  the likelihood ratio test is most powerful for simple
                       hypotheses
  uniformly most powerful  optimal across the whole alternative; exists only
                       sometimes
  monotone likelihood ratio  the condition under which it does
  restricted optimality  unbiasedness or invariance imposed when no uniform
                       optimum exists
  likelihood ratio test  the general-purpose construction
  Wald and score tests  asymptotically equivalent alternatives with different
                       computational and finite-sample behaviour
  p-value              the null probability of a result at least as extreme
  what it is not       the probability the null is true, or that the result was
                       chance, or a measure of effect size
  the pre-specification requirement  selecting the hypothesis after seeing the
                       data invalidates the reference distribution
  multiple testing     the error rate across a family; family-wise control versus

                       questions and should be chosen deliberately
  confidence interval  the set of parameter values not rejected; the duality with
                       testing is exact and is the clearest way to understand it

## Asymptotics and Large Sample Theory

Exact finite-sample distributions are available only for a few models, so most inference relies on
approximations that become accurate as the sample grows. Asymptotic theory characterizes these
approximations and, importantly, indicates when they fail, which is the part most often neglected in
application.

The two foundational results carry most of the weight. The law of large numbers says sample averages
converge to expectations, which justifies estimating population quantities by their sample analogues.
The central limit theorem says the standardized sample mean converges to a normal distribution
regardless of the underlying distribution's shape, provided the variance is finite, which is why
normal-based procedures apply so broadly.

The finite-variance condition is essential and its violation is not exotic. Heavy-tailed
distributions with infinite variance do not satisfy the central limit theorem, and their sample
means converge to stable distributions with entirely different behaviour — including the property
that averaging more observations does not reduce variability in the usual way. Financial returns,
network traffic, and city sizes are all commonly modelled with such distributions, and applying
normal-based inference to them produces confidence intervals that are far too narrow.

Asymptotic theory:
  the motivation       exact distributions exist for few models
  law of large numbers  sample averages converge to expectations
  central limit theorem  the standardized mean converges to normal
  the finite variance requirement  essential, and violated in real applications
  heavy tails          convergence to stable laws instead; averaging does not
                       help as expected
  the practical consequence  normal-based intervals are far too narrow for such
                       data
  delta method         the asymptotic distribution of a smooth function of an
                       estimator
  continuous mapping   limits pass through continuous functions
  Slutsky's theorem    combining convergence in distribution and in probability
  rates of convergence  the standard root-n rate and the situations that are
                       slower
  the convergence speed question  asymptotic validity says nothing about the
                       sample size needed, which depends on skewness and tail
                       weight and can be far larger than the usual rules of thumb
  Edgeworth expansion  higher-order corrections quantifying the approximation
                       error
  contiguity and local alternatives  the framework for comparing tests'
                       asymptotic power meaningfully

## Decision Theory, Bayes, and Resampling

Decision theory frames inference as choosing an action under uncertainty with a specified loss
function, and it unifies estimation and testing as special cases. Risk is expected loss, and the
comparison of procedures by risk clarifies what optimality means. A procedure is admissible when no
other has risk at least as low everywhere and lower somewhere, and inadmissible procedures can be
discarded — though admissibility is a weak criterion, since many poor procedures are admissible for
trivial reasons.

The Bayesian formulation places a prior distribution on the parameter and computes the posterior by
conditioning on the data. It yields direct probability statements about parameters, incorporates
prior information explicitly, and handles nuisance parameters by integration rather than by
approximation. Its costs are that the prior must be specified and affects the answer, and that
computation was historically prohibitive — the latter largely resolved by simulation methods. The
theoretical connection is that Bayes procedures are essentially the complete class of admissible
procedures, which means good frequentist procedures are Bayes procedures for some prior whether or
not they were derived that way.

Resampling methods estimate sampling distributions computationally rather than analytically. The
bootstrap resamples the data with replacement and treats the variability across resamples as an
estimate of the estimator's sampling variability, which works because the empirical distribution
approximates the true one. It applies where analytical results are unavailable and it is not
universal — it fails for estimators that are not smooth functionals of the distribution, including
the sample maximum, and for dependent data unless the resampling scheme is adapted to preserve the
dependence.
