## Limits and Continuity

The limit is the foundational construction of analysis, and the epsilon-delta
definition is what makes it rigorous rather than intuitive. To say lim_{x->a} f(x) = L
is to say that for every epsilon > 0 there exists a delta > 0 such that 0 < |x - a| <
delta implies |f(x) - L| < epsilon. The quantifier order is the whole content:
epsilon is given by an adversary, delta is your response, and delta may depend on
epsilon. Reversing the order gives a different and usually false statement. The
condition 0 < |x - a| explicitly excludes the point itself, which is why a limit can
exist where the function is undefined — the entire basis of the derivative, where the
difference quotient is undefined at exactly the point of interest.

Continuity at a point requires three things: the function is defined there, the limit
exists there, and the two agree. Failures are classified accordingly. A removable
discontinuity has a limit that disagrees with, or exists in the absence of, the
function value — sin(x)/x at zero, which has limit 1 and no value. A jump
discontinuity has one-sided limits that both exist and differ, as in a step function.
An essential discontinuity has at least one one-sided limit failing to exist, as in
sin(1/x) at zero, which oscillates infinitely fast.

Uniform continuity strengthens continuity by requiring a single delta to work across
the whole domain for a given epsilon, rather than a possibly different delta at each
point. The function 1/x is continuous on (0, 1) but not uniformly so, because the delta
required shrinks without bound as x approaches zero; f(x) = x^2 is continuous on all
of R but not uniformly, because the delta shrinks as x grows. The Heine-Cantor theorem
says continuity on a compact set implies uniform continuity, which is why the
distinction rarely surfaces on a closed bounded interval and becomes essential on open
or unbounded ones. Uniform continuity is what guarantees Riemann integrability and
what makes numerical approximation schemes converge at a predictable rate.

The three great theorems of continuous functions on a closed interval [a, b]:
  Intermediate Value Theorem   f continuous, f(a) < c < f(b) implies f(x) = c for
                               some x in (a, b). Basis of bisection root-finding.
  Extreme Value Theorem        f continuous on a compact set attains a maximum and
                               a minimum. Why optimization on closed bounded sets
                               is well posed.
  Mean Value Theorem           f continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b),
                               implies f'(c) = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a) for some c.
                               The bridge from local derivative to global change.

The mean value theorem is the workhorse behind most error bounds in numerical
analysis. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder is its direct generalization,
and it is what converts "the derivative is bounded by M" into "the function changes by
at most M times the interval length" — a statement about global behaviour deduced from
purely local information, which is the recurring pattern of the entire subject.

Q: f(x) = sin(1/x) for x nonzero and f(0) = 0. At x = 0 the function has:
A. A removable discontinuity
B. A jump discontinuity
C. An essential discontinuity
D. No discontinuity; it is continuous
Reasoning: As x approaches zero, 1/x runs to infinity and sin oscillates between -1
and 1 infinitely often, so neither one-sided limit exists. That is the definition of
an essential discontinuity. Note that x*sin(1/x) with value 0 at the origin *is*
continuous there, because the factor of x squeezes the oscillation to zero — a useful
contrast. Answer: C.

Q: A function is continuous on the open interval (0, 1) but unbounded. This is:
A. Impossible; continuity implies boundedness
B. Possible, because the interval is not compact
C. Possible only if the function is discontinuous somewhere
D. Possible only for non-differentiable functions
Reasoning: The Extreme Value Theorem requires a compact — closed and bounded — domain.
The open interval is bounded but not closed, so the theorem does not apply, and 1/x on
(0, 1) is a smooth counterexample. Compactness, not continuity alone, is what
guarantees boundedness. Answer: B.

## Differentiation

The derivative is the limit of the difference quotient, f'(a) = lim_{h->0} (f(a+h) -
f(a))/h, and it measures the instantaneous rate of change. Geometrically it is the
slope of the tangent line; analytically it is the coefficient of the best linear
approximation, f(a+h) = f(a) + f'(a)h + o(h). That second reading generalizes cleanly
to higher dimensions where the geometric picture becomes unwieldy, and it is the right
one to carry forward: differentiation is linearization.

Differentiability implies continuity but not conversely. The absolute value function is
continuous everywhere and fails to be differentiable at zero, where the left and right
difference quotients disagree. More strikingly, the Weierstrass function is continuous
everywhere and differentiable nowhere, which destroyed the nineteenth-century intuition
that continuous curves must have tangents except at isolated points, and motivated
much of the rigour that followed.

Differentiation rules, and the ones people misremember:
  power           d/dx x^n = n x^{n-1}                for all real n, x > 0
  product         (fg)' = f'g + fg'
  quotient        (f/g)' = (f'g - fg')/g^2            note the order in the numerator
  chain           (f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x)) g'(x)
  inverse         (f^{-1})'(y) = 1 / f'(f^{-1}(y))
  exponential     d/dx e^x = e^x,   d/dx a^x = a^x ln a
  logarithm       d/dx ln|x| = 1/x
  trigonometric   sin' = cos, cos' = -sin, tan' = sec^2
  inverse trig    arcsin' = 1/sqrt(1-x^2), arctan' = 1/(1+x^2)
  implicit        differentiate both sides in x, solve for dy/dx
  logarithmic     for y = f^g, take ln of both sides first

Critical points, where the derivative vanishes or fails to exist, are candidates for
local extrema, and the second derivative classifies them: positive means a local
minimum, negative a local maximum, zero is inconclusive and requires either higher
derivatives or a sign analysis of the first derivative. The inconclusive case is not
exotic — x^4 and x^3 both have vanishing first and second derivatives at the origin,
and one is a minimum while the other is an inflection point.

Taylor series expand a function as an infinite polynomial about a point, f(x) = sum
f^{(n)}(a)(x-a)^n / n!. The remainder term controls the error of truncation, and for
the Lagrange form it is f^{(n+1)}(c)(x-a)^{n+1}/(n+1)! for some c between a and x. Two
cautions carry real weight. First, a function can be infinitely differentiable and
have a Taylor series that converges to something else entirely: exp(-1/x^2) extended by
zero at the origin has every derivative zero there, so its Taylor series is identically
zero while the function is not. Smooth does not imply analytic. Second, the radius of
convergence is governed by the nearest singularity in the *complex* plane, which is why
1/(1+x^2) has a Taylor series about zero that diverges beyond |x| = 1 despite the
function being perfectly well behaved on the whole real line — the poles at plus and
minus i are invisible from the real axis and control everything.

Essential Taylor expansions about zero:
  e^x        = 1 + x + x^2/2! + x^3/3! + ...           all x
  sin x      = x - x^3/3! + x^5/5! - ...               all x
  cos x      = 1 - x^2/2! + x^4/4! - ...               all x
  ln(1+x)    = x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - ...                 |x| < 1
  1/(1-x)    = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + ...                 |x| < 1
  (1+x)^a    = 1 + ax + a(a-1)x^2/2! + ...             |x| < 1
  arctan x   = x - x^3/3 + x^5/5 - ...                 |x| <= 1

The first-order approximations from these — sin x is approximately x, ln(1+x) is
approximately x, e^x is approximately 1 + x, (1+x)^a is approximately 1 + ax — are
worth internalizing because they turn up constantly in physics, finance, and numerical
work. The small-angle approximation is the pendulum's linearization; the log
approximation is why log returns and simple returns agree for small moves; the binomial
one is behind every "a 1% change in x produces an a% change in y" statement.

## Integration

The Riemann integral is the limit of sums over partitions, and the fundamental theorem
of calculus links it to differentiation in two directions. The first part says that
d/dx of the integral of f from a to x equals f(x), so integration and differentiation
undo each other. The second says the integral of f' from a to b equals f(b) - f(a), so
a definite integral is evaluated by finding any antiderivative and taking a difference.
This is the result that converts an infinite limiting process into arithmetic, and it
is why calculus is a practical tool rather than a theoretical one.

Techniques of integration are, in essence, the differentiation rules read backwards.
Substitution reverses the chain rule and is the first thing to try. Integration by
parts reverses the product rule, giving the integral of u dv as uv minus the integral
of v du; the art is choosing u to be something that simplifies under differentiation,
and the LIATE heuristic (logarithmic, inverse trig, algebraic, trigonometric,
exponential) orders the usual preference. Partial fractions decompose a rational
function into terms with linear or irreducible quadratic denominators, each of which
integrates to a logarithm or an arctangent. Trigonometric substitution handles
expressions containing sqrt(a^2 - x^2), sqrt(a^2 + x^2), and sqrt(x^2 - a^2) by turning
them into trigonometric identities.

Improper integrals extend the definition to unbounded intervals or unbounded
integrands, defined as limits. The p-integrals are the reference cases everyone should
know: the integral of 1/x^p from 1 to infinity converges exactly when p > 1, and from
0 to 1 converges exactly when p < 1. The boundary case p = 1 diverges at both ends,
which is the harmonic series' continuous analogue. Comparison against these settles
most convergence questions without any evaluation.

Numerical integration when no antiderivative exists:
  midpoint rule       error O(h^2), exact for linear
  trapezoid rule      error O(h^2), exact for linear, easy on tabulated data
  Simpson's rule      error O(h^4), exact for cubics, the standard workhorse
  Gauss-Legendre      n nodes exact for degree 2n-1; superb for smooth integrands
  adaptive quadrature refines only where the estimated error is large
  Monte Carlo         error O(n^{-1/2}) independent of dimension; the only
                      practical option above ~8 dimensions
  quasi-Monte Carlo   low-discrepancy sequences, error near O(n^{-1}) for smooth
                      integrands in moderate dimension

The dimensional crossover in that list is worth stating explicitly, because it governs
practical choices in physics and finance. Grid-based rules have error scaling like
h^k, and with n points in d dimensions h is n^{-1/d}, so the error goes as n^{-k/d} —
the curse of dimensionality. Monte Carlo error is n^{-1/2} regardless of d. Below
roughly four to eight dimensions the deterministic rules win handily; above that Monte
Carlo wins and the gap grows without bound. This is the entire reason path integrals
and high-dimensional Bayesian posteriors are attacked by sampling rather than by
quadrature.

[user] I need to integrate a smooth function over [0, 1] to 1e-10 accuracy and my
adaptive Simpson call is taking forever.
[assistant] tool_call numerics.quadrature_plan({"interval": [0, 1], "tolerance": 1e-10,
"smoothness": "analytic", "current_method": "adaptive_simpson", "evals_used": 2100000})
[tool] {"simpson_evals_needed": 3.2e6, "gauss_legendre_nodes": 24,
"clenshaw_curtis_nodes": 40, "recommended": "gauss_legendre", "expected_error": 4e-14}
[assistant] Switch to Gauss-Legendre quadrature. For an analytic integrand this is not
a marginal improvement — it is roughly a hundred-thousand-fold reduction in function
evaluations.

The reason is the convergence rate. Simpson's rule is exact for cubics and has error
O(h^4), so reaching 1e-10 requires refining until h is small enough, and adaptive
subdivision spends most of its budget in regions the error estimator is merely
uncertain about. Gauss-Legendre with n nodes is exact for polynomials up to degree
2n-1, and for an analytic function the error decays geometrically in n rather than
polynomially in h. Twenty-four nodes should put you at around 4e-14, comfortably past
your tolerance.

Practically: take the standard Gauss-Legendre nodes and weights on [-1, 1], map them
to [0, 1] with x = (t+1)/2, and scale the weights by the Jacobian of 1/2. Sum w_i
f(x_i) and you are done — 24 evaluations, no adaptivity, no error estimator.

The one thing that would break this: if your function is not actually analytic — a
kink, a square-root branch point at an endpoint, or a near-singularity just off the
interval — the geometric convergence disappears and you are back to algebraic rates.
If Gauss-Legendre stalls around 1e-6, that is what happened, and the fix is either a
substitution that removes the singularity or a splitting of the interval at the
offending point.

## Sequences and Series

A sequence converges if its terms approach a limit; a series converges if its sequence
of partial sums does. The necessary condition that terms go to zero is not sufficient,
and the harmonic series is the standing counterexample: 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... diverges
despite terms shrinking to zero, because the partial sums grow like ln n. This single
example prevents more errors than any other in the subject.

Convergence tests, in the order worth trying them:
  term test           if a_n does not go to 0, the series diverges. Necessary only.
  geometric           sum r^n converges iff |r| < 1, to 1/(1-r)
  p-series            sum 1/n^p converges iff p > 1
  comparison          bound termwise against a known series
  limit comparison    if a_n/b_n tends to a positive finite limit, both do the same
  ratio test          L = lim |a_{n+1}/a_n|; converges if L < 1, diverges if L > 1,
                      inconclusive at 1. Best for factorials and exponentials.
  root test           L = lim |a_n|^{1/n}; same conclusion. Best for nth powers.
  integral test       for positive decreasing a_n, the series and the integral agree
  alternating series  decreasing terms going to zero converge; error bounded by the
                      first omitted term

Absolute convergence, where the series of absolute values converges, is much stronger
than conditional convergence. An absolutely convergent series can be rearranged
freely without changing its sum. A conditionally convergent one cannot: the Riemann
rearrangement theorem states that a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged
to converge to *any* prescribed real number, or to diverge. The alternating harmonic
series sums to ln 2 in its natural order and can be rearranged to sum to seventeen.
This is not a curiosity — it is why term reordering in numerical summation is not
innocent, and it is a good intuition for why floating-point summation order matters.

Power series converge on a disc whose radius is determined by the ratio or root test,
and within that radius they may be differentiated and integrated term by term, which
is the standard method for deriving new series from known ones and for solving
differential equations. Uniform convergence is the condition that licenses swapping
limits with integrals and derivatives: pointwise convergence alone is not enough, and
the standard counterexample is a sequence of functions each with integral one whose
pointwise limit is identically zero — a spike that grows taller and narrower. The
Weierstrass M-test is the practical tool for establishing uniform convergence of a
series of functions.

Q: The series sum of (-1)^n / n converges to ln 2. A rearrangement of its terms:
A. Must also converge to ln 2
B. Can be made to converge to any real number
C. Must diverge
D. Converges only if the rearrangement is finite
Reasoning: The series converges conditionally — the terms 1/n do not have a convergent
sum of absolute values. Riemann's rearrangement theorem then applies: the positive and
negative terms each form a divergent series, so they can be interleaved to steer the
partial sums toward any target. Answer: B.

Q: For which p does sum 1/(n (ln n)^p) from n = 2 converge?
A. All p   B. p > 1   C. p >= 1   D. No p
Reasoning: The integral test with substitution u = ln n turns this into the integral of
1/u^p, which converges at the upper limit exactly when p > 1. The case p = 1 gives
ln(ln n), which diverges — slowly, but it diverges. Answer: B.

## Multivariable and Vector Calculus

In several variables the derivative becomes the gradient, a vector of partial
derivatives pointing in the direction of steepest increase, with magnitude equal to
that rate. The directional derivative in a unit direction u is the dot product of the
gradient with u, which is maximized when u aligns with the gradient — the fact that
makes gradient descent a sensible algorithm. Crucially, the existence of all partial
derivatives does not imply differentiability in the multivariable sense; the function
must be well approximated by its linearization in every direction simultaneously, and
continuity of the partials is a convenient sufficient condition.

The second derivative becomes the Hessian, the matrix of second partials, symmetric
whenever those partials are continuous (Clairaut's theorem). Its eigenvalues classify
critical points: all positive gives a local minimum, all negative a local maximum,
mixed signs a saddle. In high dimensions mixed signs are overwhelmingly the generic
case — a random symmetric matrix has positive and negative eigenvalues with
probability approaching one — which is why saddle points rather than spurious local
minima are the dominant obstacle in high-dimensional non-convex optimization.

Constrained optimization uses Lagrange multipliers. To extremize f subject to g = 0,
set the gradient of f equal to lambda times the gradient of g and solve alongside the
constraint. The geometric content is that at a constrained optimum the level sets of
objective and constraint are tangent, so their gradients are parallel; if they were
not, one could move along the constraint surface and improve. The multiplier itself is
not a bookkeeping artifact — it equals the rate of change of the optimal value with
respect to relaxing the constraint, which is the shadow price in economics and the
force of constraint in mechanics. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions extend this to
inequality constraints, adding complementary slackness: each multiplier is zero unless
its constraint is active.

The vector calculus operators and their meanings:
  gradient    grad f = (df/dx, df/dy, df/dz)      scalar -> vector; steepest ascent
  divergence  div F = dFx/dx + dFy/dy + dFz/dz    vector -> scalar; source density
  curl        curl F = del cross F                vector -> vector; circulation
  Laplacian   lap f = div grad f                  scalar -> scalar; diffusion
  identities  curl(grad f) = 0,  div(curl F) = 0  always, for smooth fields

Those two identities carry physical content. That the curl of a gradient vanishes
means conservative fields — those derived from a potential — have no circulation, so
the work done around any closed loop is zero, which is conservation of energy. That
the divergence of a curl vanishes means fields expressible as a curl have no sources,
which is why magnetic monopoles are absent from Maxwell's equations given that B is the
curl of a vector potential.

The integral theorems unify into a single statement. Green's theorem relates a line
integral around a plane curve to a double integral over the enclosed region. Stokes'
theorem relates the circulation of a field around a closed curve to the flux of its
curl through any surface bounded by that curve. The divergence theorem relates the flux
of a field through a closed surface to the integral of its divergence over the enclosed
volume. All three are instances of the generalized Stokes theorem, which says the
integral of a differential form over the boundary of a region equals the integral of
its exterior derivative over the region — the fundamental theorem of calculus,
restated in arbitrary dimension.

## Measure and the Lebesgue Integral

The Riemann integral partitions the domain; the Lebesgue integral partitions the
range. That single change of viewpoint resolves most of the Riemann integral's
deficiencies. The Dirichlet function, equal to one on the rationals and zero
elsewhere, is not Riemann integrable because every partition contains both rationals
and irrationals in each subinterval, so the upper and lower sums are one and zero
regardless of refinement. Lebesgue integration handles it immediately: the rationals
form a countable set and therefore have measure zero, so the function equals zero
almost everywhere and its integral is zero.

Measure formalizes size. Lebesgue measure on the real line assigns each interval its
length and extends this consistently to a much larger collection of sets, the Lebesgue
measurable sets. Countable sets have measure zero, since each point has measure zero
and countable additivity applies. More surprisingly, uncountable sets can also have
measure zero — the Cantor set is the standard example, having the same cardinality as
the reals while occupying no length. And the axiom of choice yields non-measurable
sets, such as the Vitali set, on which no consistent notion of size exists; their
existence is why measure theory restricts to a sigma-algebra rather than to all
subsets.

The convergence theorems are what make the Lebesgue integral the tool of choice for
analysis and probability, because they license exchanging a limit with an integral
under weak hypotheses where the Riemann theory demands uniform convergence.

The three convergence theorems:
  Monotone Convergence   0 <= f_1 <= f_2 <= ... pointwise to f
                         implies the integrals converge to the integral of f
  Fatou's Lemma          integral of liminf f_n <= liminf of integral of f_n
                         for non-negative f_n; an inequality, always available
  Dominated Convergence  f_n -> f pointwise with |f_n| <= g for an integrable g
                         implies the integrals converge to the integral of f

Dominated convergence is the one used constantly. The dominating function g is the
essential hypothesis and its absence is exactly what the sliding-spike counterexample
exploits: functions that are n on the interval [0, 1/n] and zero elsewhere converge
pointwise to zero, each has integral one, and no integrable function dominates them
all. Whenever an interchange of limit and integral is asserted, the honest question is
what dominates.

The connection to probability is direct rather than analogical. A probability space is
a measure space with total measure one, a random variable is a measurable function,
and expectation is the Lebesgue integral against the probability measure. Almost-sure
convergence is pointwise convergence off a null set, convergence in probability is
convergence in measure, and convergence in distribution is weak convergence of the
induced measures. The dominated convergence theorem becomes the statement that
expectations converge when the variables are uniformly bounded by something with
finite mean, which is the workhorse behind most limit theorems in the subject. Seen
this way, probability is not an application of measure theory so much as a
specialization of it with its own vocabulary.
