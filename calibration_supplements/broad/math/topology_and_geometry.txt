## Metric and Topological Spaces

Topology studies properties preserved under continuous deformation — stretching and bending
but not tearing or gluing. The motivating observation is that a great many results depend not on
distances but only on which points are near which, and abstracting away the metric reveals which
results those are.

A metric space equips a set with a distance function satisfying non-negativity, symmetry, the
identity of indiscernibles, and the triangle inequality. From the metric one obtains open balls,
and from open balls one obtains open sets, and it turns out that the open sets alone suffice to
define continuity, convergence, compactness, and connectedness. A topological space therefore
dispenses with the metric and specifies the open sets directly, subject to axioms: the empty set
and the whole space are open, arbitrary unions of open sets are open, and finite intersections
are open.

The asymmetry between arbitrary unions and finite intersections is not arbitrary and is worth
understanding. An infinite intersection of open sets need not be open — the intersection of the
intervals from minus one over n to one over n, over all n, is the single point zero, which is
not open. Restricting to finite intersections is exactly what is needed for the axioms to
produce the intended notion of nearness.

Different metrics can induce the same topology, which is the point of the abstraction. The
Euclidean, taxicab, and maximum metrics on the plane give different distances and identical open
sets, so every topological statement about one holds for the others. Conversely some
topological spaces are not metrizable at all, so the topological setting is strictly more
general.

Q: A continuous bijection between topological spaces is not necessarily a homeomorphism.
The additional requirement is:
A. The spaces must be metric spaces
B. The inverse map must also be continuous
C. The map must preserve distances
D. The spaces must be compact
Reasoning: A homeomorphism requires continuity in both directions, and a continuous bijection
can have a discontinuous inverse — mapping the half-open interval [0, 1) onto a circle by
wrapping is the standard example, continuous and bijective with a discontinuous inverse at the
join. Continuity of the inverse is automatic when the domain is compact and the codomain
Hausdorff, which is why option D is close and not the general answer. Answer: B.

## Continuity, Compactness, and Connectedness

Continuity in the topological setting is defined by preimages: a map is continuous when the
preimage of every open set is open. This is equivalent to the epsilon-delta definition in metric
spaces and is more general, and its direction — preimages rather than images — is the part that
initially surprises. The image of an open set under a continuous map need not be open, as the
constant map demonstrates.

Compactness is the topological property doing the most work in analysis, and its definition —
every open cover has a finite subcover — appears technical and is exactly right. In Euclidean
space the Heine-Borel theorem identifies compact sets with those that are closed and bounded,
which is the familiar characterization, and in general spaces the two notions diverge and
compactness is the useful one.

What compactness buys is the ability to pass from local to global. A continuous function on a
compact set attains its maximum and minimum, is uniformly continuous, and is bounded — each
proved by covering the set with local statements and extracting a finite subcover. These are
exactly the theorems that fail on a non-compact set such as an open interval, where a continuous
function may be unbounded and may fail to attain its bounds. Compactness is what makes the
extreme value theorem true.

Connectedness formalizes being in one piece: a space is connected when it cannot be written as
the union of two disjoint nonempty open sets. Path connectedness is the stronger and more
intuitive condition that any two points are joined by a continuous path, and it implies
connectedness while the converse fails — the topologist's sine curve is connected and not path
connected, which is the standard counterexample.

The intermediate value theorem's topological content is worth stating because it explains why
the theorem is true. A continuous image of a connected set is connected, an interval is
connected, and the connected subsets of the real line are exactly the intervals — so the image
of an interval under a continuous function is an interval, which contains every value between
any two of its values. The familiar theorem is a corollary of a structural statement about
connectedness, which is the pattern topology repeatedly reveals.

## Surfaces and Their Classification

Surfaces are two-dimensional manifolds — spaces locally resembling the plane — and the compact
ones without boundary admit a complete classification, which is unusual and instructive. Every
such surface is either a sphere with some number of handles attached, or a sphere with some
number of cross-caps, and the number determines it up to homeomorphism.

The genus counts the handles, so the sphere has genus zero, the torus genus one, the two-holed
torus genus two, and so on. Orientability distinguishes the two families: a surface is
orientable if a consistent notion of clockwise can be defined globally, and non-orientable if
not. The Möbius band and Klein bottle are the standard non-orientable examples, and the Möbius
band demonstrates the phenomenon concretely — a small clock face transported around the band
returns reversed.

The Euler characteristic is the invariant that makes classification computable. For a surface
divided into vertices, edges, and faces, the alternating sum of their counts is independent of
how the division was made, which is a striking result. It equals two for the sphere, zero for the
torus, and two minus twice the genus in general for orientable surfaces. Because it is a
topological invariant, two surfaces with different Euler characteristics cannot be homeomorphic,
and computing it from any convenient triangulation settles the question.

The Gauss-Bonnet theorem deserves emphasis because it connects a geometric quantity to a
topological one in a way that seems too strong to be true. The total Gaussian curvature
integrated over a closed surface equals two pi times its Euler characteristic — so however a
sphere is deformed, stretched, or dented, the total curvature is unchanged. Bending the surface
redistributes curvature and cannot change its total, because the total is determined by the
topology alone. This is the archetype of a rigidity result linking local geometry to global
topology, and it has descendants throughout modern mathematics and theoretical physics.

## Homotopy, Homology, and Invariants

Topological classification requires invariants — quantities preserved by homeomorphism — because
proving two spaces are homeomorphic requires exhibiting a map, while proving they are not
requires showing some invariant differs. Algebraic topology constructs invariants by assigning
algebraic objects to spaces in a way that respects continuous maps.

Homotopy formalizes continuous deformation of maps. Two maps are homotopic if one can be
continuously deformed into the other, and two spaces are homotopy equivalent if maps between
them compose to something homotopic to the identity. Homotopy equivalence is coarser than
homeomorphism — a disc is homotopy equivalent to a point, and a solid torus to a circle — which
makes it easier to compute and sufficient for many purposes.

The fundamental group is the first and most intuitive algebraic invariant. It consists of loops
based at a point, considered up to homotopy, with concatenation as the group operation. A space
in which every loop contracts to a point has trivial fundamental group and is called simply
connected — the sphere is, the torus is not, and the circle's fundamental group is the integers,
counting how many times a loop winds around.

Homology groups generalize this to higher dimensions and are more computable than higher
homotopy groups. They detect holes of each dimension: the zeroth counts connected components,
the first counts loops that do not bound a surface, the second counts voids enclosed by
surfaces. Their computability is what makes them the practical tool, since higher homotopy groups
are notoriously difficult to compute even for the sphere.

Brouwer's fixed point theorem deserves note because its proof illustrates the method. The
theorem states that any continuous map from a disc to itself has a fixed point, which is
intuitive and not obviously provable. The standard argument is by contradiction: if no fixed
point existed, a retraction from the disc onto its boundary circle could be constructed, and such
a retraction would induce a homomorphism from the trivial fundamental group of the disc onto the
integers of the circle, which is impossible. An algebraic impossibility establishes a geometric
fact, which is what algebraic topology exists to do.

## Differential Geometry and Applications

Differential geometry studies smooth manifolds with additional structure permitting
differentiation, and its central concept is curvature — a local measurement of how a space
departs from being flat. For a surface, Gaussian curvature is the product of the principal
curvatures, and Gauss's theorema egregium establishes that it is intrinsic: it can be determined
by measurements made entirely within the surface, without reference to how the surface sits in
space.

The theorem's practical consequence is that a surface with nonzero Gaussian curvature cannot be
flattened without distortion. A sphere has positive curvature everywhere and a plane has zero, so
no map projection can preserve both distances and angles — every world map distorts something,
and which distortion is accepted is a choice rather than a technical limitation. A cylinder, by
contrast, has zero Gaussian curvature despite appearing curved, which is why a sheet of paper
rolls into a cylinder without stretching.

Geodesics generalize straight lines as the locally shortest paths within a surface, and they are
what a particle follows in the absence of force. On a sphere they are great circles, which is why
long-distance flight paths appear curved on a flat map — they are straight in the geometry that
matters and the map's distortion makes them look otherwise. In general relativity, spacetime is
a curved manifold and free particles follow geodesics, so gravity is not a force acting on
trajectories but the curvature of the space the trajectories are straight in.

Topological data analysis deserves the closing note because it applies these ideas
computationally. Data is treated as a sampled point cloud, and building a simplicial complex by
connecting points within a distance threshold produces a space whose homology reflects the data's
shape. Varying the threshold gives a family of complexes, and persistent homology tracks which
topological features appear and persist across scales — features persisting over a wide range of
thresholds are structural, and short-lived ones are noise. This provides a description of shape
that is invariant to the coordinate system, robust to noise, and able to detect structure such
as loops and voids that clustering and dimensionality reduction do not represent at all.

The recurring lesson across the subject is that ignoring information reveals structure. Topology
discards distance and finds what depends only on nearness; homotopy discards more and becomes
computable; homology discards the group structure of loops and becomes computable in every
dimension. Each abstraction loses detail and gains generality and tractability, and the results
obtained apply to every space satisfying the weaker assumptions — which is why theorems about
abstract topological spaces constrain map projections, weather patterns, robot motion planning,
and the geometry of spacetime alike.
