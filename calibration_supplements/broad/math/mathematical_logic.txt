## Propositional and Predicate Logic

Mathematical logic studies reasoning itself as a mathematical object, which means formalizing
what a statement is, what a proof is, and what it means for a statement to be true. The
distinction between syntax and semantics is the subject's organizing separation: syntax concerns
symbol manipulation according to rules, semantics concerns meaning and truth, and the central
results relate the two.

Propositional logic combines atomic statements with connectives whose truth is determined by the
truth of their components. Its semantics is given by truth tables, and its central computational
question — whether a formula has a satisfying assignment — is the canonical NP-complete problem,
which is a striking connection between a foundational logical question and computational
complexity.

Predicate logic adds quantifiers and predicates, which permits statements about objects and their
properties rather than only about whole propositions. This makes it expressive enough for
mathematics, and the price is that its satisfiability is undecidable rather than merely
intractable. Quantifier order is not commutative, and the difference between a statement holding
for every object with a witness depending on it, and a single witness working for every object, is
the source of a great deal of mathematical content — uniform convergence versus pointwise
convergence is exactly that distinction.

Q: The statements "for every x there exists y such that P(x,y)" and "there exists y such that for
every x, P(x,y)" differ in that:
A. They are logically equivalent
B. The second is strictly stronger, since it requires one witness working for all x rather than
   permitting the witness to depend on x
C. The first is stronger
D. Neither implies the other
Reasoning: In the first, y may depend on x, so a different witness may serve each x. In the
second, a single y serves every x, which is a stronger requirement and implies the first. The
distinction is exactly that between pointwise and uniform properties throughout analysis.
Answer: B.

## Proof Systems and Model Theory

A proof system specifies which derivations count as proofs, and several formulations exist with
different characteristics. Hilbert systems have many axioms and few inference rules, which makes
them convenient for metamathematical proofs and painful for constructing actual derivations.
Natural deduction has introduction and elimination rules for each connective, which mirrors
informal mathematical reasoning closely. Sequent calculus makes the structure of derivations
explicit and supports the cut elimination theorem, which is central to proof theory.

Soundness and completeness relate the syntactic and semantic notions. A proof system is sound if
everything provable is true in every model, which is the minimum requirement — an unsound system
proves falsehoods. It is complete if everything true in every model is provable, which is a
substantive result rather than a definitional one. Gödel's completeness theorem establishes that
first-order logic is complete in this sense, so semantic entailment and syntactic provability
coincide.

Model theory studies the relationship between formal theories and the structures satisfying
them, and its results are frequently counterintuitive. The compactness theorem states that if
every finite subset of a set of sentences has a model, the whole set does, which has the immediate
consequence that any first-order theory with arbitrarily large finite models has an infinite one.
The Löwenheim-Skolem theorems establish that a theory with an infinite model has models of every
infinite cardinality, so no first-order theory can pin down the size of its models.

Proof systems and models:
  Hilbert system       many axioms, few rules; convenient for metatheory
  natural deduction    introduction and elimination rules; mirrors informal
                       practice
  sequent calculus     explicit derivation structure; cut elimination
  soundness            everything provable is true; the minimum requirement
  completeness         everything true is provable; Gödel's completeness theorem
                       for first-order logic
  compactness          finite satisfiability implies satisfiability; forces
                       unintended infinite models
  Löwenheim-Skolem     a theory with an infinite model has models of every
                       infinite size
  Skolem's paradox     a countable model of set theory contains a set the theory
                       calls uncountable; resolved by noting that the bijection
                       witnessing countability is not in the model
  categoricity         all models isomorphic; only achievable for finite
                       structures in first-order logic
  non-standard models  unintended structures satisfying the axioms; unavoidable by
                       compactness, and the basis of non-standard analysis with
                       genuine infinitesimals
  decidability of a theory  whether provability is algorithmically determinable;
                       real closed fields are decidable, arithmetic is not

Skolem's paradox deserves attention because its resolution clarifies what a formal theory
actually captures. Set theory proves the existence of uncountable sets, and by Löwenheim-Skolem it
has a countable model — so within that model there is a set the theory calls uncountable, which
from outside has countably many elements. The resolution is that uncountability means the absence
of a bijection with the naturals, and the bijection exists outside the model but not within it.
Formal theories constrain their models from the inside, and properties that appear absolute are
relative to what the model contains.

## Set Theory and Foundations

Set theory became the standard foundation because sets are expressive enough to encode all
mathematical objects — numbers, functions, relations, structures — so a single axiomatic theory
suffices for the whole subject. Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice is the
standard framework, and its axioms are constraints on set formation designed to permit ordinary
mathematics while avoiding paradox.

Russell's paradox is what forced the axiomatization. The naive principle that any property
defines a set produces contradiction: the set of all sets not containing themselves both contains
and does not contain itself. The resolution restricts comprehension so that sets are formed only
as subsets of existing sets, which blocks the construction while retaining everything mathematics
requires.

The axiom of choice states that a choice function exists for any collection of nonempty sets, and
it is independent of the other axioms — neither provable nor refutable from them. It is equivalent
to several statements that are used constantly, including that every vector space has a basis and
that every set can be well-ordered, and it also implies results that many find unacceptable,
including the decomposition of a sphere into finitely many pieces reassembling into two spheres of
the same size. The paradoxical consequence is genuine and the pieces are non-measurable, so no
physical construction is implied.

The independence of the continuum hypothesis deserves emphasis because of what it establishes
about mathematical truth. Gödel showed it cannot be disproved from ZFC and Cohen showed it cannot
be proved, so the axioms simply do not determine it. This is not ignorance awaiting resolution by
a cleverer argument; it is a demonstration that the question is not settled by the assumptions.
Whether it has a determinate answer that the axioms fail to capture, or whether the question is
genuinely indeterminate, is a philosophical position rather than a mathematical result.

## Computability and Incompleteness

Computability theory formalizes what can be computed, and the remarkable finding is that
several independently proposed formalizations — Turing machines, lambda calculus, recursive
functions, register machines — define exactly the same class of computable functions. The
Church-Turing thesis asserts that this class captures the informal notion of effective
computation, and it is a thesis rather than a theorem because it relates a formal definition to an
informal concept.

The halting problem is the canonical undecidable problem: no algorithm determines, for an
arbitrary program and input, whether the program halts. The proof is diagonalization — assume such
an algorithm exists, construct a program that halts exactly when the algorithm says it does not,
and apply it to itself. Rice's theorem generalizes this substantially: every non-trivial semantic
property of programs is undecidable, so no algorithm decides whether a program computes a
particular function, whether two programs are equivalent, or whether a program has a particular
behaviour.

Gödel's incompleteness theorems apply the same self-referential technique to formal systems. The
first theorem states that any consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic
contains true statements it cannot prove. The construction encodes statements as numbers, which
permits a system to make statements about its own provability, and constructs a statement
asserting its own unprovability — which is true if unprovable, so the system is incomplete if
consistent.

The second theorem is the more consequential for foundations: such a system cannot prove its own
consistency. This defeated Hilbert's programme of establishing mathematics on a finitary
foundation whose consistency could be proved by unobjectionable means, since the consistency proof
would require assumptions stronger than the system being proved consistent.

The scope of the incompleteness theorems deserves the emphasis it usually does not receive,
because they are misapplied more often than applied. They concern formal systems strong enough to
express arithmetic, and they establish that such systems have unprovable true statements — not
that any particular interesting statement is unprovable, not that mathematics is unreliable, and
not that human reasoning transcends formal systems. Weaker systems can be complete and decidable,
which shows the phenomenon depends on the specific strength required, and the unprovable
statements the theorems construct are self-referential rather than the ordinary statements
mathematicians care about. Natural independent statements do exist and finding them was
substantial work.

## Type Theory and Formalization

Type theory offers an alternative foundation to set theory, in which every object has a type and
expressions must be well-typed. Its original motivation was avoiding paradox — Russell's paradox
is blocked because a set cannot be a member of itself when membership must respect type levels —
and its modern importance comes from a different property: the correspondence between types and
propositions.

The Curry-Howard correspondence identifies propositions with types and proofs with programs. A
proof of an implication is a function from proofs of the antecedent to proofs of the consequent; a
proof of a conjunction is a pair; a proof of a disjunction is a tagged value. This is an exact
correspondence rather than an analogy, and it means that checking a proof is type-checking a
program, which makes proof verification mechanical.

This is the foundation of proof assistants, which have moved from a curiosity to a working tool.
Substantial theorems have been formally verified — the four colour theorem, the Feit-Thompson odd
order theorem, the Kepler conjecture — and formal verification is used in industry for hardware,
for compilers, and for security-critical software. The effort remains large, typically an order of
magnitude or more over an informal proof, and it is falling as libraries accumulate and automation
improves.

The constructive versus classical distinction deserves a closing note because it has practical
consequences. Classical logic accepts the law of excluded middle, so a proof that the negation of
a statement leads to contradiction establishes the statement. Constructive logic does not, so an
existence proof must produce the object. The difference matters computationally because a
constructive proof of an existence statement is an algorithm producing the witness, by
Curry-Howard, while a classical one may establish existence without any means of finding it. This
is why constructive proofs are preferred in settings where the proof is intended to yield a
program, and why the distinction is engineering-relevant rather than merely philosophical.

The broader significance of formalization is a shift in what a proof is for. An informal proof
persuades a reader; a formal proof is checked by a program whose correctness rests on a small
auditable kernel. The two serve different purposes — informal proofs communicate the idea and
formal ones establish the fact beyond dispute — and the growing practice is to have both, with the
informal exposition carrying the understanding and the formalization carrying the guarantee. That
combination addresses a genuine problem: published proofs contain errors at a measurable rate, and
for results that others build on, mechanical checking removes a source of accumulated risk that
peer review does not reliably catch.
