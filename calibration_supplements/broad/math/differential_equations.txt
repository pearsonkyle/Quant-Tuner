## First-Order Equations

A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives, and it is the natural language
for any system whose rate of change depends on its state. Newton's laws, population dynamics,
chemical kinetics, heat flow, circuit behaviour, and epidemic spread are all differential
equations, which is why the subject is the most broadly applicable in the whole of applied
mathematics.

Classification determines which methods apply, so it is the first step rather than a formality.
The order is the highest derivative appearing. An equation is ordinary if it involves one
independent variable and partial if several. It is linear if the unknown function and its
derivatives appear only to the first power and are not multiplied together, which is a strong
condition with strong consequences — linear equations obey superposition, so solutions can be
combined, and they admit systematic solution methods that nonlinear equations do not.

Separable equations, in which the variables can be arranged on opposite sides, are solved by
direct integration and cover a surprising range of applications including exponential growth
and decay, Newton's law of cooling, and many chemical rate laws. Linear first-order equations
are solved by an integrating factor that converts the left side into an exact derivative, which
is a technique worth knowing because it generalizes and because the integrating factor is
always available in closed form for the first-order linear case.

First-order methods:
  separable            g(y) dy = f(x) dx;  integrate both sides
  linear               y' + p(x)y = q(x);  multiply by exp(integral of p)
  exact                M dx + N dy = 0 with dM/dy = dN/dx;  find the potential
  integrating factor   converts an inexact equation to exact
  substitution         homogeneous, Bernoulli, and other reducible forms
  autonomous           y' = f(y);  no explicit x; equilibria at f(y) = 0
  direction field      sketch slopes at grid points; reveals qualitative behaviour
                       without solving
  existence            Picard-Lindelof: a continuous f with a Lipschitz condition
                       in y guarantees a unique local solution
  uniqueness failure   without the Lipschitz condition; y' = sqrt(y) with y(0) = 0
                       has infinitely many solutions
  initial value problem  one condition at a point; well posed for first order
  equilibrium stability  for autonomous equations, f'(y*) < 0 is stable

Direction fields are worth using more than they typically are, because they deliver
qualitative understanding without any solution. Sketching the slope at a grid of points shows
where solutions increase and decrease, where equilibria lie, and whether nearby solutions
converge toward or diverge from them. For an autonomous equation this reduces to a phase line,
on which the sign of the derivative between equilibria determines the entire long-term
behaviour of every solution — information that the closed-form solution, where one exists,
presents less directly.

Q: The logistic equation y' = ry(1 - y/K) with r and K positive has equilibria at y = 0 and
y = K. Their stability is:
A. Both stable   B. Both unstable   C. y = 0 unstable, y = K stable   D. y = 0 stable, y = K
unstable
Reasoning: The derivative of the right side is r(1 - 2y/K). At y = 0 this is r, which is
positive, so perturbations grow and the equilibrium is unstable. At y = K it is -r, negative,
so perturbations decay and the equilibrium is stable. Any positive initial population
therefore approaches the carrying capacity. Answer: C.

## Linear Equations and Systems

Second-order linear equations with constant coefficients are the workhorse of applied
mathematics because they describe every oscillator, every simple circuit, and the linearization
of nearly every system near an equilibrium. The solution method is to substitute an
exponential trial function, obtaining a characteristic polynomial whose roots determine the
behaviour.

The three cases of the characteristic equation correspond directly to physical regimes.
Distinct real roots give a sum of decaying exponentials, the overdamped case, with no
oscillation. A repeated real root gives an exponential and an exponential times the
independent variable, the critically damped case, which returns to equilibrium fastest without
overshoot. Complex conjugate roots give an exponentially modulated oscillation, the
underdamped case. Recognizing which case a given system falls into from its coefficients is
what allows the qualitative behaviour to be stated without solving.

Nonhomogeneous equations require a particular solution added to the general homogeneous one.
Undetermined coefficients guesses a form matching the forcing term and works when that term is
a polynomial, exponential, sine, cosine, or product of these — which covers most applications.
Variation of parameters is general and requires integration. The resonance case, where the
forcing matches a homogeneous solution, requires multiplying the trial form by the independent
variable, and it corresponds physically to driving a system at its natural frequency, where the
response grows without bound in the undamped case.

Linear equation methods:
  characteristic equation  substitute exponential; roots determine behaviour
  distinct real roots      overdamped; sum of exponentials
  repeated root            critically damped; includes a factor of x
  complex roots            underdamped; exponentially modulated oscillation
  superposition            linear combinations of solutions are solutions
  Wronskian                nonzero indicates linear independence
  undetermined coefficients  guess a form matching the forcing; simple and limited
  variation of parameters  general; requires integration
  resonance                forcing matches a natural frequency; multiply the trial
                           form by x, and the response is unbounded without damping
  reduction of order       find a second solution given one
  Cauchy-Euler             variable coefficients of a special form; substitute a
                           power

Systems of first-order equations subsume higher-order equations, since any nth-order equation
becomes a system of n first-order ones by introducing the derivatives as new variables. This is
why numerical solvers take first-order systems as their input format and why the theory is
developed there. For linear systems with constant coefficients, the solution is governed by the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the coefficient matrix, so the entire behaviour follows from an
eigenvalue problem.

The phase plane is the geometric representation for two-dimensional systems, plotting one
variable against the other with trajectories showing how the state evolves. Equilibria are
classified by the eigenvalues at that point: both negative real gives a stable node, both
positive an unstable node, opposite signs a saddle, complex with negative real part a stable
spiral, complex with positive real part an unstable spiral, and purely imaginary a centre. This
classification is what makes qualitative analysis of nonlinear systems possible — linearize at
each equilibrium and read off the local behaviour.

## Series, Transforms, and Special Functions

Not every equation has an elementary solution, and series methods extend reach substantially.
The power series method assumes a solution expressible as a series about a point, substitutes,
and derives a recurrence for the coefficients. At an ordinary point this works directly. At a
regular singular point the Frobenius method generalizes by including a leading power that need
not be an integer, and it is what produces the special functions of mathematical physics.

Bessel functions, Legendre polynomials, Hermite polynomials, and the rest arise as solutions
of specific equations that recur because they come from separating variables in the standard
coordinate systems. Bessel functions appear whenever cylindrical geometry is involved — a
vibrating drumhead, a waveguide, heat flow in a pipe. Legendre polynomials appear in spherical
geometry, which is why they underlie the spherical harmonics used in quantum mechanics,
geodesy, and computer graphics. Their properties are tabulated precisely because they recur so
often.

The Laplace transform converts differential equations in time into algebraic equations in a
complex variable, solves them algebraically, and inverts. Its practical advantage is that
initial conditions enter automatically rather than being applied afterward, and that it handles
discontinuous and impulsive forcing that would require piecewise treatment otherwise. This is
why it is standard in control theory, where step and impulse responses are the objects of
interest and where the transfer function — the transform of the impulse response — is the
central representation.

Transform and series methods:
  power series          about an ordinary point; recurrence for the coefficients
  Frobenius             about a regular singular point; a non-integer leading power
  Bessel functions      cylindrical geometry; drumheads, waveguides, pipes
  Legendre polynomials  spherical geometry; the basis of spherical harmonics
  Laplace transform     differential to algebraic; initial conditions built in
  transfer function     the transform of the impulse response; the control theory
                        representation
  convolution theorem   convolution in time is multiplication in the transform
                        domain
  Fourier series        periodic functions as sums of sinusoids; the basis for
                        separation of variables on bounded domains
  Fourier transform     for problems on infinite domains
  Green's function      the response to a point source; the general solution
                        follows by superposition
  step and impulse      Heaviside and Dirac; handled naturally by transforms

The Green's function deserves emphasis because it captures the structure of linear problems
compactly. If the response to a point source is known, the response to any distributed source
follows by superposition — integrating the Green's function against the source distribution.
This turns a differential equation into an integral, and it is the basis of boundary element
methods, of the propagator formalism in quantum field theory, and of the influence function
approach in structural analysis. Its existence depends entirely on linearity, which is another
instance of why the linear-nonlinear distinction matters so much.

## Partial Differential Equations

Partial differential equations involve several independent variables and describe fields
rather than functions of one variable. Second-order linear equations in two variables classify
into three types by the sign of a discriminant, and the classification determines the character
of the solutions and the appropriate methods — a classification that turns out to correspond to
three physically distinct kinds of behaviour.

Elliptic equations, of which Laplace's equation is the archetype, describe equilibrium states.
Their solutions are smooth, they satisfy a maximum principle placing extrema on the boundary,
and information propagates instantly throughout the domain, so the solution at every point
depends on the entire boundary. They are boundary value problems.

Parabolic equations, of which the heat equation is the archetype, describe diffusion. They
smooth initial data immediately — an initial discontinuity becomes infinitely differentiable
for any positive time — and they have infinite propagation speed in the mathematical model,
which is a known unphysical artifact. They are irreversible: running the heat equation backward
is ill-posed, which is why deblurring and inverse heat conduction are hard problems requiring
regularization.

Hyperbolic equations, of which the wave equation is the archetype, describe propagation. They
preserve discontinuities, propagating them along characteristics at finite speed, and they are
reversible in time. The domain of dependence is finite, so the solution at a point depends only
on the initial data within the backward characteristic cone — which is the mathematical
expression of a finite signal speed and is what the CFL condition enforces numerically.

Classification and canonical equations:
  elliptic               Laplace, Poisson; equilibrium, smooth, maximum principle,
                         boundary value problems
  parabolic              heat, diffusion; smoothing, irreversible, infinite
                         propagation speed in the model
  hyperbolic             wave; propagation at finite speed, preserves
                         discontinuities, reversible
  separation of variables  assume a product solution; reduces to ODEs; requires
                         a suitable geometry and boundary conditions
  eigenfunction expansion  expand in the eigenfunctions of the spatial operator
  Dirichlet condition    value specified on the boundary
  Neumann condition      derivative specified; pure Neumann determines the solution
                         only up to a constant and requires a compatibility
                         condition on the sources
  Robin condition        a combination; convective heat transfer
  method of characteristics  for first-order and hyperbolic equations; follows the
                         curves along which information travels
  well-posedness         existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on data;
                         a problem lacking the third is ill-posed and requires
                         regularization

Separation of variables is the classical technique and its scope is worth stating precisely.
Assuming a solution that is a product of functions of each variable, substituting, and dividing
gives an equation in which each side depends on different variables and must therefore equal a
constant. This reduces the partial differential equation to ordinary ones, and the boundary
conditions turn the spatial problem into an eigenvalue problem whose eigenfunctions form a
basis. The method requires a coordinate system in which the domain boundaries are coordinate
surfaces, which is why it works beautifully for rectangles, discs, and spheres and not at all
for an arbitrary shape.

## Nonlinear Dynamics

Nonlinear equations lack superposition, so the systematic methods for linear problems do not
apply and closed-form solutions are exceptional. The productive approach is qualitative:
determine the equilibria, linearize about each to establish local behaviour, and assemble a
global picture from the local ones plus additional structure such as conserved quantities or
Lyapunov functions.

Linearization about an equilibrium replaces the nonlinear system with its Jacobian, and the
Hartman-Grobman theorem justifies this: near a hyperbolic equilibrium, one whose eigenvalues
have no zero real part, the nonlinear system is topologically equivalent to its linearization.
The theorem's condition matters, because at a non-hyperbolic equilibrium the linearization is
inconclusive and the nonlinear terms determine the behaviour — which is precisely the situation
at a bifurcation.

Bifurcations are qualitative changes in the structure of solutions as a parameter varies, and
a small number of types account for most cases. A saddle-node bifurcation creates or destroys a
pair of equilibria. A transcritical bifurcation exchanges the stability of two equilibria that
cross. A pitchfork bifurcation replaces one stable equilibrium with two, and is characteristic
of symmetric systems undergoing symmetry breaking. A Hopf bifurcation converts a stable
equilibrium into an unstable one surrounded by a limit cycle, which is how sustained
oscillation arises in a system that previously settled.

Nonlinear concepts:
  equilibrium            where all derivatives vanish
  linearization          the Jacobian; valid near hyperbolic equilibria
  Hartman-Grobman        topological equivalence to the linearization
  phase portrait         the global picture of trajectories
  limit cycle            an isolated closed orbit; sustained oscillation with
                         amplitude independent of initial conditions
  Poincare-Bendixson     a bounded trajectory in two dimensions approaches an
                         equilibrium or a limit cycle; hence no chaos in the plane
  Lyapunov function      a scalar decreasing along trajectories; proves stability
                         without solving
  conserved quantity     constrains trajectories to level sets
  saddle-node            equilibria created or destroyed in pairs
  transcritical          two equilibria exchange stability
  pitchfork              symmetry breaking; one equilibrium becomes three
  Hopf                   an equilibrium loses stability, giving a limit cycle
  period doubling        successive bifurcations leading to chaos; Feigenbaum's
                         universal constant governs the spacing
  strange attractor      bounded, aperiodic, fractal; the geometry of chaos
  sensitive dependence   exponential divergence of nearby trajectories; the
                         defining property of chaos

The Poincare-Bendixson theorem is worth knowing because it establishes a hard limit on what
two-dimensional continuous systems can do. A bounded trajectory in the plane must approach
either an equilibrium or a closed orbit, so chaos requires at least three dimensions in a
continuous autonomous system. Discrete maps are not so constrained — the one-dimensional
logistic map is chaotic — because a map can jump rather than moving continuously, and it is the
continuity of the flow together with the planar topology that forces the restriction.

Numerical solution is how nonlinear systems are actually studied, and the practical guidance
follows from the equation's character. Non-stiff problems are handled by explicit
Runge-Kutta methods with adaptive stepping. Stiff problems, with widely separated timescales,
require implicit methods, since the explicit step size is bounded by stability rather than
accuracy and becomes impractically small. Hamiltonian systems integrated over long times require
symplectic methods that preserve phase-space structure, so that conserved quantities oscillate
within a bound rather than drifting. And for any of these, verifying the result by refining the
tolerance and confirming the answer stops changing is the minimum standard, because a plausible
trajectory produced by an inappropriate method is indistinguishable from a correct one without
that check.
