## Complex Numbers and Analytic Functions

Complex numbers extend the reals by adjoining a square root of minus one, and the resulting
field is algebraically closed — every non-constant polynomial has a root, which the reals do not
provide. That closure is the fundamental theorem of algebra, and it is the first indication that
the complex numbers are a more natural setting than the reals for many questions, including
questions whose statement mentions only real quantities.

The geometric interpretation is what makes complex arithmetic intuitive. A complex number is a
point in the plane, addition is vector addition, and multiplication scales by the modulus and
rotates by the argument. Euler's formula connects the exponential to the trigonometric functions,
so the exponential of an imaginary argument traces the unit circle, and multiplication by a
complex number of unit modulus is exactly a rotation. This is why complex numbers are the natural
language for anything involving rotation, oscillation, or phase.

Complex differentiability is far more restrictive than real differentiability, and this
restriction is the source of the subject's power. The derivative is defined as a limit of a
difference quotient as in the real case, and the limit must be the same along every direction of
approach in the plane. Requiring this yields the Cauchy-Riemann equations relating the partial
derivatives of the real and imaginary parts, and a function satisfying them with continuous
partials is analytic.

  complex conjugate    reflection across the real axis

The consequences of analyticity are extraordinary and have no real-variable analogue. An analytic
function is infinitely differentiable automatically, whereas a real function can be differentiable
once and not twice. It equals its Taylor series in any disc where it is analytic, whereas a real
infinitely differentiable function need not. It is determined on an entire connected region by its
values on any set with a limit point, so knowing it on a tiny arc determines it everywhere —
analytic continuation. And it cannot attain a maximum modulus in the interior of a region unless
it is constant.

Q: A function is complex differentiable at every point of an open disc. What follows about its
higher derivatives?
A. Nothing; differentiability once does not imply more
B. It is infinitely differentiable and equals its Taylor series throughout the disc
C. It is twice differentiable
D. Only that the second derivative exists
Reasoning: Complex differentiability in a neighbourhood is far stronger than the real analogue,
and Cauchy's integral formula expresses all derivatives as contour integrals of the function
itself, establishing that they all exist and are analytic. Convergence of the Taylor series
throughout the disc follows. This is the property that makes complex analysis so rigid.
Answer: B.

## Contour Integration and Cauchy's Theorems

Integration in the complex plane is along a path rather than over an interval, and the value
depends on the path in general. For analytic functions it does not, and that independence is
Cauchy's theorem: the integral of an analytic function around any closed contour in a simply
connected domain is zero. The consequence is that the integral between two points depends only on
the endpoints, so analytic functions have antiderivatives and the fundamental theorem of calculus
carries over.

Simple connectivity is the essential hypothesis and its failure is where the subject becomes
interesting. In a domain with a hole — the plane minus a point, for instance — a contour
enclosing the hole cannot be shrunk to a point within the domain, and the integral around it need
not vanish. The integral of one over z around a circle enclosing the origin is two pi i,
independent of the circle's radius, and this single computation underlies everything that follows.

Cauchy's integral formula expresses an analytic function's value at an interior point as a
contour integral of its values on the boundary. This is a remarkable statement: the values on a
closed curve determine the values everywhere inside. Differentiating under the integral gives the
same for all derivatives, which is how the infinite differentiability is established, and it
yields bounds on the derivatives in terms of the function's size on the boundary.

  the key computation  the integral of 1/z around a circle enclosing the origin is
                       2 pi i, independent of radius

  Liouville's theorem  a bounded entire function is constant

  Morera's theorem     the converse of Cauchy; vanishing integrals imply analytic
  deformation          a contour may be deformed within the domain of analyticity

Liouville's theorem deserves emphasis because of how much it delivers from how little. A
function analytic on the entire plane and bounded must be constant, which follows from the
derivative bound: the estimate on the derivative involves the maximum modulus divided by the
radius, and letting the radius grow with the modulus bounded forces the derivative to zero
everywhere. The fundamental theorem of algebra follows in three lines — if a polynomial had no
root, its reciprocal would be entire and bounded, hence constant, hence the polynomial is
constant. A theorem about real polynomials is proved by a detour through complex analysis, which
is the pattern the subject repeats.

## Series, Singularities, and Residues

An analytic function has a Taylor series converging in the largest disc within which it is
analytic, so the radius of convergence is the distance to the nearest singularity. This explains
a phenomenon that is inexplicable from the real perspective: the function one over one plus x
squared is perfectly well behaved on the entire real line, and its Taylor series about zero
diverges beyond a radius of one. The reason is the singularities at plus and minus i, which are
invisible from the real axis and which nonetheless control the convergence.

Laurent series extend Taylor series to include negative powers, which permits expansion about a
singularity in an annulus. The coefficient of the inverse first power is the residue, and it is
the single most useful number in the subject because the entire contour integral reduces to it.

Singularities classify by the Laurent expansion. A removable singularity has no negative powers
and the function extends analytically. A pole of order n has finitely many negative powers with
the lowest being the inverse nth, and the function grows without bound as the point is
approached. An essential singularity has infinitely many negative powers, and the behaviour near
it is wild — Picard's theorem states that in any neighbourhood the function takes every complex
value, with at most one exception, infinitely often.

The residue theorem states that the integral around a closed contour is two pi i times the sum
of the residues at the enclosed singularities. This converts contour integration into a
computation of residues, which for poles is a matter of differentiation and limits rather than of
integration at all. It is the subject's central computational tool.

The argument principle deserves note because it turns a counting problem into an integral. The
integral of the logarithmic derivative around a contour equals two pi i times the number of zeros
minus the number of poles inside, counted with multiplicity. This is what underlies the Nyquist
stability criterion in control theory: plotting the open-loop transfer function around a contour
enclosing the right half plane and counting encirclements of a critical point determines the
number of unstable closed-loop poles, without computing any of them.

## Conformal Mapping and Transforms

An analytic function with nonvanishing derivative is conformal, meaning it preserves angles
between curves. The reason is that near a point it acts as multiplication by the derivative,
which is a rotation and a scaling, and both preserve angles. This makes analytic functions the
natural tool for transforming geometric regions while preserving the structure of the equations
defined on them.

The application follows from harmonicity being preserved. The real and imaginary parts of an
analytic function satisfy Laplace's equation, and composing a harmonic function with a conformal
map yields another harmonic function. A boundary value problem on a complicated region can
therefore be transformed to the same problem on a simple region — a disc or a half plane — solved
there, and mapped back. This was the primary method for two-dimensional potential problems before
numerical computation and remains a source of exact solutions.

The Riemann mapping theorem establishes when this is possible and its strength is striking: any
simply connected proper subset of the plane can be conformally mapped onto the unit disc, and the
map is unique given normalizing conditions. Whatever the region's shape, provided it is simply
connected and not the whole plane, a conformal map to the disc exists. The theorem is
non-constructive, and specific maps for specific regions are catalogued or computed numerically.

The connection between pole locations and stability is the subject's most widely applied
consequence. A linear time-invariant system's response is determined by its transfer function,
whose poles correspond to the natural modes. A pole in the left half plane corresponds to a
decaying mode and a pole in the right half plane to a growing one, so stability is precisely the
condition that all poles have negative real part. The discrete analogue places them inside the
unit circle. Every technique in classical control — root locus, Nyquist, Bode — is a method for
reasoning about where the poles are and how feedback moves them.

## Applications

The applications of complex analysis extend well beyond mathematics, and they share a common
form: a real problem is embedded in the complex plane, solved using tools available only there,
and the answer read back.

Definite integrals over the real line that resist elementary methods frequently yield to contour
integration. Closing the real axis with a large semicircular arc in the upper half plane, showing
that the arc's contribution vanishes as its radius grows, and applying the residue theorem
evaluates the integral by summing residues in the upper half plane. Integrals of rational
functions, of rational functions times trigonometric functions, and of many other classes are
handled this way.

Two-dimensional potential theory is where conformal mapping earns its place. Electrostatic
potential, steady heat flow, and incompressible irrotational fluid flow all satisfy Laplace's
equation in two dimensions, so a single complex potential describes both the potential and its
conjugate — the streamlines in fluid flow, the field lines in electrostatics. Solving a flow
around a complicated shape reduces to finding a conformal map from a simple shape, and the
Joukowski transform mapping a circle to an aerofoil is the classical demonstration.

The Riemann zeta function deserves the closing note because it illustrates analytic continuation
at its most consequential. The function is defined by a series converging only where the real part
of the argument exceeds one, and analytic continuation extends it uniquely to the whole plane
apart from a single pole. That extension is not a formal manoeuvre — it is the unique analytic
function agreeing with the series where the series converges, and it encodes genuine information.
Its zeros determine the error term in the prime counting function, and the conjecture that all
non-trivial zeros lie on a single vertical line is equivalent to the sharpest possible statement
about how primes are distributed. A question about integers is answered by the analytic
properties of a function on the complex plane, which is the subject's deepest instance of the
pattern it repeats throughout.
