## Logic and Proof

Propositional logic combines statements with connectives whose truth is determined entirely
by the truth of their components. The one that causes persistent confusion is implication:
"if P then Q" is false only when P is true and Q is false, so an implication with a false
antecedent is vacuously true. This is a definition rather than a discovery, chosen because it
makes the rules of inference work, and the mismatch with ordinary language — where "if" often
carries a causal claim — is the source of the confusion.

The relationships between an implication and its variants are worth having exact. The
converse swaps antecedent and consequent and is a different statement with no logical
relation to the original. The inverse negates both and is likewise independent. The
contrapositive negates and swaps and is logically equivalent to the original, which is why
proof by contrapositive is valid and is frequently much easier than the direct route.
Confusing an implication with its converse is probably the most common reasoning error
outside mathematics, appearing constantly in interpretations of test results, legal
arguments, and statistical claims.

Predicate logic adds quantifiers, and quantifier order is not commutative. "For every
person there exists someone they love" and "there exists someone whom every person loves" are
different claims, and the second is far stronger. Negating a quantified statement flips each
quantifier and negates the body, so the negation of "for all x, P(x)" is "there exists x with
not P(x)" — which is why disproving a universal claim requires exactly one counterexample and
proving it requires an argument covering every case.

Proof techniques and when each applies:
  direct              assume the hypothesis, derive the conclusion
  contrapositive      prove not-Q implies not-P; often easier when the conclusion
                      is a negation
  contradiction       assume the negation, derive an absurdity; powerful and easy
                      to misuse by hiding a direct proof inside
  induction           base case plus inductive step; for statements over integers
  strong induction    assume all smaller cases; needed when the step depends on
                      more than the immediate predecessor
  structural induction  over recursively defined structures such as trees and
                      expressions
  construction        exhibit an object with the property
  counterexample      one suffices to disprove a universal claim
  pigeonhole          n+1 objects in n boxes forces a repeat; trivially stated,
                      surprisingly powerful
  double counting     count the same set two ways and equate; proves many
                      combinatorial identities immediately

Induction requires both parts and the base case is where errors hide. The inductive step
establishes that the property propagates, and without a verified base case it propagates from
nothing. The classic fallacious proofs — that all horses are the same colour — fail precisely
because the inductive step tacitly assumes a case size that the base does not supply. Checking
that the step's argument is valid at the smallest case it claims to cover is the standard
defence.

Q: The statement "if it rains, the ground is wet" is given. Which is logically equivalent?
A. If the ground is wet, it rained
B. If it does not rain, the ground is not wet
C. If the ground is not wet, it did not rain
D. It rains and the ground is wet
Reasoning: The contrapositive negates and swaps, giving "if not wet, then not rain," which is
equivalent. Option A is the converse and B is the inverse; both are independent statements
that happen to be equivalent to each other and not to the original. Answer: C.

## Sets, Relations, and Functions

A set is an unordered collection of distinct elements, specified by listing or by a defining
property. The naive notion of forming a set from any property leads to Russell's paradox —
the set of all sets not containing themselves both must and must not contain itself — which
is why axiomatic set theory restricts comprehension to subsets of existing sets. The practical
consequence for most work is nil, and the historical importance is that it forced the
foundations to be made precise.

Relations generalize functions by dropping the requirement of a unique output. A relation on
a set may be reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, or transitive, and specific combinations
give structures that recur constantly. An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and
transitive, and it partitions the set into disjoint equivalence classes — which is the
formal content of any statement that two things are "the same for present purposes". A partial
order is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive, and it captures precedence, containment,
and divisibility, with the crucial feature that some pairs may be incomparable.

Cardinality extends counting to infinite sets and produces genuinely counterintuitive
results. Two sets have the same cardinality when a bijection exists between them, and by that
criterion the integers, the even integers, and the rationals all have the same cardinality —
countably infinite — despite one being a proper subset of another. Cantor's diagonal argument
shows the reals are strictly larger, so infinities come in different sizes, and the same
argument technique proves the halting problem undecidable and Gödel's incompleteness theorems.

Sets and relations:
  power set            all subsets; |P(S)| = 2^|S|, always strictly larger
  Cartesian product    ordered pairs; |A x B| = |A| |B|
  equivalence relation reflexive, symmetric, transitive; partitions the set
  partial order        reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive; allows incomparability
  total order          a partial order with all pairs comparable
  injective            distinct inputs to distinct outputs
  surjective           every output attained
  bijective            both; establishes equal cardinality
  countable            in bijection with the naturals; integers and rationals are
  uncountable          the reals, by diagonalization
  pigeonhole           if |A| > |B| there is no injection from A to B

## Counting

Counting problems reduce to a handful of patterns, and the difficulty is recognizing which
applies. The two foundational rules are the sum rule, that disjoint alternatives add, and the
product rule, that independent sequential choices multiply. Nearly every counting formula is
built from these plus corrections for overcounting.

The central distinction is between arrangements where order matters and selections where it
does not, and between drawing with and without replacement. Permutations count ordered
arrangements; combinations count unordered selections and are permutations divided by the
number of orderings of the selected items, which is exactly the correction for overcounting.
Multiset problems — selections with repetition allowed — map to the stars and bars
construction, where distributing identical items into distinct boxes is equivalent to
arranging items and dividers.

Inclusion-exclusion handles overlapping sets by adding the individual sizes, subtracting the
pairwise intersections, adding back the triple intersections, and alternating. It is the
correction for the sum rule when alternatives are not disjoint, and it generalizes to any
number of sets with alternating signs.

Counting formulas:
  permutations of n         n!
  k-permutations of n       n! / (n-k)!
  combinations              C(n,k) = n! / (k! (n-k)!)
  with repetition, ordered  n^k
  with repetition, unordered  C(n+k-1, k);  stars and bars
  multiset permutations     n! / (n1! n2! ... nm!)
  circular arrangements     (n-1)!;  rotations are equivalent
  inclusion-exclusion       |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| - |A ∩ B|, alternating in general
  binomial theorem          (x+y)^n = sum C(n,k) x^k y^(n-k)
  Pascal's identity         C(n,k) = C(n-1,k-1) + C(n-1,k)
  derangements              D(n) ≈ n!/e;  permutations with no fixed point
  Catalan numbers           C(2n,n)/(n+1);  balanced parentheses, binary trees,
                            triangulations, and many equivalent structures

Double counting is the proof technique that makes combinatorial identities transparent
rather than algebraic. Pascal's identity, for instance, follows immediately by counting
k-subsets of an n-set two ways: either the subsets containing a designated element, of which
there are C(n-1,k-1), or those excluding it, of which there are C(n-1,k). The algebraic
verification is a factorial manipulation that proves the identity and explains nothing; the
combinatorial argument shows why it is true.

The Catalan numbers deserve mention because they appear in an unreasonable number of
contexts: the count of balanced parenthesizations, of binary trees with n nodes, of
triangulations of a polygon, of monotonic lattice paths staying below the diagonal, and of
ways to associate a product. Recognizing a Catalan structure in a problem immediately supplies
the answer, and the fact that so many superficially unrelated problems share the same count
reflects underlying bijections between them.

## Recurrences, Generating Functions, and Number Theory

Recurrence relations define a sequence in terms of earlier terms, and they arise naturally
from recursive algorithms and from combinatorial decompositions. Linear homogeneous
recurrences with constant coefficients are solved by the characteristic equation: substitute
a geometric trial solution, obtain a polynomial, and the general solution is a combination of
terms from its roots — with a correction of polynomial factors for repeated roots. The
Fibonacci sequence solved this way yields the golden ratio, which is why Fibonacci numbers
grow geometrically at that rate.

The master theorem handles the divide-and-conquer recurrences that dominate algorithm
analysis, giving the asymptotic solution by comparing the work of the recursive calls against
the work of combining. Its three cases correspond to the recursion dominating, the combining
dominating, or both contributing equally, which is why merge sort and binary search have the
complexities they do.

Generating functions encode a sequence as the coefficients of a formal power series, turning
recurrence manipulation into algebra. Convolution of sequences becomes multiplication of
series, which is why generating functions handle problems involving combining independent
choices so cleanly. The technique is: write the generating function from the recurrence,
solve for it algebraically, then extract coefficients by partial fractions or by recognizing
a known expansion.

Recurrences and number theory:
  linear homogeneous     characteristic equation; roots give the general solution
  master theorem         T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n); compare f(n) with n^(log_b a)
  generating function    sum a_n x^n;  convolution becomes multiplication
  Fibonacci              F(n) = F(n-1) + F(n-2);  grows as phi^n / sqrt(5)
  divisibility           a | b if b = ak for some integer k
  gcd via Euclid         gcd(a,b) = gcd(b, a mod b);  O(log) steps
  Bezout                 gcd(a,b) = ax + by for some integers x, y
  modular arithmetic     congruence classes; addition and multiplication respect it
  modular inverse        exists iff gcd(a,m) = 1; found by extended Euclid
  Fermat's little        a^(p-1) ≡ 1 mod p for prime p not dividing a
  Euler's theorem        a^phi(n) ≡ 1 mod n when gcd(a,n) = 1
  Chinese remainder      congruences with coprime moduli have a unique joint
                         solution modulo the product
  prime factorization    unique; the fundamental theorem of arithmetic

Modular arithmetic underpins public-key cryptography, and the reason is an asymmetry in
computational difficulty. Multiplying two large primes is fast; factoring their product is
believed to be hard. Modular exponentiation is fast by repeated squaring; the discrete
logarithm — recovering the exponent — is believed to be hard. RSA rests on the first pair and
Diffie-Hellman on the second, and the security of both rests on the word "believed": no proof
of hardness exists, and Shor's algorithm breaks both on a sufficiently large quantum computer.

## Graphs and Computational Complexity

A graph is a set of vertices and a set of edges connecting them, and the abstraction is
useful precisely because so many problems are relational. Directed edges represent asymmetric
relationships; weights represent cost or capacity. The representation choice matters for
algorithms: an adjacency matrix gives constant-time edge queries at quadratic space, and an
adjacency list gives linear space proportional to edges, which is the right choice for the
sparse graphs that occur in practice.

Traversal is the foundation of most graph algorithms. Depth-first search explores as far as
possible before backtracking and naturally computes connected components, detects cycles,
produces topological orderings, and identifies strongly connected components. Breadth-first
search explores level by level and finds shortest paths in unweighted graphs, which
depth-first search does not.

Complexity classes formalize what is efficiently computable. P is the class of problems
solvable in polynomial time, and it is the standard proxy for tractable. NP is the class
whose solutions are verifiable in polynomial time — a distinction worth stating precisely,
since NP does not mean not polynomial. NP-complete problems are the hardest in NP, in that
every NP problem reduces to them, so a polynomial algorithm for any one would give one for
all. Whether P equals NP is the central open question of theoretical computer science, and
the consensus expectation is that it does not.

Graph and complexity essentials:
  degree sum            equals twice the edge count; every graph has an even number
                        of odd-degree vertices
  tree                  connected and acyclic; n vertices, n-1 edges, unique paths
  bipartite             two-colourable; equivalently, no odd cycle
  Euler circuit         uses every edge once; exists iff connected with all even
                        degrees
  Hamiltonian cycle     visits every vertex once; NP-complete to decide
  planar                drawable without crossings; V - E + F = 2
  four colour theorem   every planar graph is 4-colourable
  BFS                   shortest paths in unweighted graphs, O(V+E)
  DFS                   components, cycles, topological order, O(V+E)
  Dijkstra              shortest paths with non-negative weights, O(E log V)
  Bellman-Ford          handles negative weights, detects negative cycles, O(VE)
  minimum spanning tree Kruskal or Prim
  max-flow min-cut      the maximum flow equals the minimum cut capacity
  P                     solvable in polynomial time
  NP                    verifiable in polynomial time
  NP-complete           in NP and every NP problem reduces to it
  NP-hard               at least as hard as NP-complete; need not be in NP

The practical significance of NP-completeness is not that a problem is unsolvable but that
the right strategy changes. Once a problem is known NP-complete, searching for an exact
polynomial algorithm is almost certainly wasted effort, and attention should turn to
approximation algorithms with provable ratios, to heuristics with good empirical behaviour,
to exact algorithms exponential in a parameter that is small in practice, or to solvers —
SAT and integer programming solvers routinely dispatch instances with millions of variables
despite the worst case being intractable, because real instances have structure that worst-case
analysis ignores.

That gap between worst-case and typical-case difficulty deserves emphasis because it is
frequently misread. NP-completeness is a statement about the hardest instances of a problem,
and it says nothing about the instances that actually arise. Boolean satisfiability is the
canonical NP-complete problem and is also the engine behind practical hardware verification,
program analysis, and scheduling tools, because industrial instances are not adversarial. The
correct inference from NP-completeness is that no algorithm will be fast on everything, not
that no algorithm will be fast on your problem.
