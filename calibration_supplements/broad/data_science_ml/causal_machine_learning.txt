## Why Prediction Is Insufficient

Predictive models answer what will happen given what is observed; causal questions ask what would
happen if something were changed. These are different questions with different answers, and a
model that predicts excellently can be entirely misleading as a guide to action — which is the
central point and the one that most often needs establishing.

The canonical illustration is a model predicting hospital mortality that learns asthmatic
patients with pneumonia have lower mortality. The prediction is accurate: asthmatic patients were
admitted directly to intensive care because they were considered high risk, and the intensive
treatment produced better outcomes. Acting on the model — deprioritizing asthmatic patients
because they are predicted to do well — would remove the treatment producing the prediction. The
model correctly captured the association in the data and the association was produced by the
policy it would be used to change.

This is the general structure rather than an anomaly. Any model trained on observational data
learns associations shaped by the decisions that generated the data, and using the model to change
those decisions invalidates the associations it learned. Prediction is adequate when the goal is
forecasting under an unchanged policy and inadequate whenever the goal is choosing an action.

C. Have no effect

Reasoning: The association arises because dissatisfied customers contact support and dissatisfied
customers churn. Support contact is downstream of the cause rather than being the cause, and it
may be reducing churn relative to what would occur without it. This is the prediction-intervention
gap: the model captured a real association that does not support the intervention. Answer: B.

## Causal Graphs and Identification

A causal graph represents assumed causal relationships as directed edges, and its value is that
it makes the assumptions explicit and mechanically determines what can be estimated. The graph is
not derived from the data — several graphs are compatible with any dataset — so it encodes domain
knowledge, and stating it is what permits the analysis to be criticized.

Three structures determine how association flows and they must be distinguished because the
correct handling of each is different. A chain, where one variable causes a second which causes a
third, transmits association, and conditioning on the middle variable blocks it. A fork, where one
variable causes two others, transmits association between the two effects, and conditioning on
the common cause blocks it — this is confounding, and the common cause is the confounder. A
collider, where two variables both cause a third, transmits no association, and conditioning on
the common effect creates one.

The collider case is where intuition fails most severely. Adjusting for additional variables is
widely believed to be conservative, and it is not: conditioning on a common effect of the
treatment and the outcome induces a spurious association where none existed. Selection into the
sample is a form of conditioning, so a sample selected on a variable affected by both the treatment
and the outcome produces collider bias regardless of what is adjusted for in the analysis.

Positivity deserves emphasis because it is frequently violated silently. Estimating the effect of
a treatment requires that units with the relevant covariates could have received either treatment
level, and if some region of covariate space deterministically receives one treatment, no data
informs the counterfactual there. A model will nonetheless produce an estimate by extrapolating,
and the extrapolation is unconstrained by data. Checking the overlap of covariate distributions
between treatment groups is the diagnostic, and restricting the analysis to the region of common
support is the honest response.

## Estimation

Given identification — an argument that the effect is estimable from the available data under
stated assumptions — estimation is the statistical task of computing it. Machine learning
contributes here because the adjustment frequently requires modelling high-dimensional
relationships that parametric models handle poorly.

Naively plugging a machine learning model into a causal estimator produces bias, and the reason
is instructive. Machine learning methods are regularized to minimize prediction error, which
introduces bias to reduce variance, and that bias does not vanish at the rate required for valid
inference about a causal parameter. The resulting estimate has a bias term that dominates the
standard error, so confidence intervals have incorrect coverage.

Double machine learning addresses this with two devices. Orthogonalization constructs an
estimating equation insensitive to first-order errors in the nuisance models, so that errors in
estimating the treatment assignment or the outcome model do not propagate directly into the
causal estimate. Cross-fitting estimates the nuisance models on one part of the data and the
causal parameter on another, which removes the overfitting bias that arises when the same
observations inform both. Together they permit flexible machine learning for the nuisance
components while retaining valid inference.

Estimation approaches:

The doubly robust property is worth understanding because it explains why these estimators are
preferred. An estimator combining an outcome model and a propensity model is consistent if either
one is correctly specified, which is a genuinely useful guarantee — it does not require getting
both right, and it means two independent attempts at modelling provide protection that either
alone does not. The property does not survive both being wrong, and in high dimensions both are
approximations, which is why the combination with cross-fitting and orthogonalization matters
rather than the doubly robust property alone.

## Heterogeneous Effects and Policy Learning

An average treatment effect answers whether a treatment helps on average, and it frequently
conceals that it helps some units substantially and harms others. Estimating how the effect varies
with covariates — the conditional average treatment effect — is what permits targeting, and it is
where machine learning contributes most directly to causal inference.

The estimation problem is harder than prediction because the target is never observed. For a
predictive model, each observation has a label and the error is measurable. For an effect estimate,
each unit's individual treatment effect is the difference between two potential outcomes and only
one is observed, so there is no label to compare against and standard model selection does not
apply. This is why methods designed for the purpose exist rather than applying a regression to
the difference.

Causal forests adapt random forests to this task by splitting to maximize heterogeneity in the
treatment effect rather than in the outcome, and by using separate samples for splitting and
estimation so that the estimates within a leaf are honest. Meta-learners construct the effect
estimate from standard predictive models in various arrangements, and which arrangement performs
better depends on the structure — whether the baseline outcome or the effect is the more complex
function.

The four-quadrant framing deserves emphasis because it clarifies what targeting achieves. Under
any treatment, some units would have the desired outcome regardless, some would not regardless,
some are influenced positively, and some are influenced negatively. A predictive model identifies
who will have the outcome, which mixes the first group with the third. An effect model identifies
who is influenced, which is the only group where treatment changes anything. Targeting by
predicted outcome therefore spends the budget substantially on units whose behaviour is unchanged,
and the difference between predictive and causal targeting is frequently large enough to determine
whether a campaign is profitable.

Your highest-churn-probability customers divide into two groups that the model cannot

outcome. Others are churning for reasons a discount does not touch — they have moved, their needs
changed, a competitor offers something you do not — and treating them costs money and changes
nothing. The model ranks both identically because both have high churn probability.

The group you are missing entirely is customers with moderate churn probability who are highly
responsive to a discount. They rank low on your list and they are where the budget produces the
most retained revenue per pound.

There is also a group worth actively excluding. Some customers would have stayed and, on
receiving an unsolicited discount offer, are prompted to reconsider the relationship or to expect
discounts thereafter — the sleeping dogs. Targeting them is worse than doing nothing, and a
predictive model cannot identify them because their predicted churn is low and their effect is
negative.

You have a randomized holdout, which is what makes the fix straightforward. Use it to estimate
the conditional average treatment effect — how much the discount changes churn probability as a
function of customer covariates — with a causal forest or an X-learner. Then rank by estimated
effect rather than by predicted churn and target the top of that ranking within your budget.

Two practical points. Validate the uplift model with a Qini or uplift curve on held-out
randomized data rather than with a standard classification metric, since the target is
unobservable and ordinary metrics do not measure what you need. And run the new policy against the
old one as an experiment rather than switching outright — the uplift estimate is itself uncertain,
and the comparison is what establishes the gain.

Expect the targeted set to overlap with your current one by substantially less than intuition
suggests, frequently under half, which is the practical measure of how different the two questions
are.

## Sensitivity and Practice

Causal conclusions from observational data rest on untestable assumptions, and the honest
treatment is to state them and to quantify how much violation would overturn the conclusion rather
than to assert that they hold. Sensitivity analysis does this: it asks how strong an unmeasured
confounder would need to be, in its association with both treatment and outcome, to explain away
the estimated effect.

The E-value expresses this in an interpretable form — the minimum strength of association an
unmeasured confounder would need with both the exposure and the outcome to account for the
observed effect. An E-value of 1.3 means a modest unmeasured confounder suffices and the finding is
fragile; an E-value of 5 means an implausibly strong one would be required. This converts an
untestable assumption into a quantified statement about how much would have to be wrong.

The practical workflow that produces defensible causal analysis has a consistent shape. State the
causal question precisely, including the population, the treatment, the comparison, and the
outcome. Draw the assumed causal graph and state what is assumed. Determine identification from
the graph — which is where the analysis frequently ends, because the effect is not identified with
the available data and no estimation method rescues it. Then estimate, check the assumptions that
are checkable, and conduct sensitivity analysis on the ones that are not.

Triangulation deserves the closing emphasis because it is the strongest available response to the
untestability problem. Any single observational design rests on assumptions that cannot be
verified, so a single estimate however carefully produced carries that exposure. Several designs
with different and largely unrelated assumptions — an instrumental variable analysis, a
difference-in-differences, a regression discontinuity, and a natural experiment — that agree
provide evidence far stronger than the sum of their individual strengths, because the assumptions
would all have to fail in a coordinated way to produce agreement. Where designs disagree, that is
informative too, and it identifies which assumption is doing the work.
