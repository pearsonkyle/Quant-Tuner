## Linguistic Structure

Natural language processing benefits from knowing what makes language hard, and the
difficulties are structural rather than incidental. Ambiguity is pervasive at every level:
lexical, where a word has several senses; syntactic, where a sentence admits several parse
structures; semantic, where a parsed sentence admits several interpretations; and pragmatic,
where the intended meaning depends on context and speaker intent. Human listeners resolve these
so fluently that the ambiguity is invisible until a system fails on it.

The classical levels of linguistic analysis remain a useful organizing framework even though
modern systems do not process them in sequence. Phonology concerns sound. Morphology concerns
word structure — how a stem combines with affixes to convey tense, number, case, and
derivation. Syntax concerns how words combine into phrases and sentences. Semantics concerns
literal meaning. Pragmatics concerns meaning in context, including implicature, presupposition,
and speech acts. Discourse concerns structure above the sentence, including coreference and
rhetorical relations.

Morphological richness varies enormously between languages and it determines how much work
tokenization must do. English is morphologically sparse, so words are reasonable units.
Turkish and Finnish are agglutinative, forming words by stacking many morphemes, so a single
word may correspond to an English clause and word-level vocabulary is unbounded. Arabic and
Hebrew have non-concatenative morphology where the root consonants interleave with vowel
patterns. Chinese and Japanese have no whitespace between words at all. A method designed
around English word tokens transfers poorly to any of these, which is a large part of why
subword tokenization became standard.

Linguistic levels and phenomena:
  morphology           word-internal structure; inflection and derivation
  agglutinative        many morphemes per word; Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian
  fusional             morphemes merge; Latin, Russian
  isolating            few morphemes per word; Chinese
  syntax               phrase and sentence structure
  constituency         hierarchical phrase structure
  dependency           head-dependent relations between words; often more useful
                       computationally and more portable across languages
  attachment ambiguity  where a modifier attaches; the classic parsing difficulty
  semantics            literal compositional meaning
  polysemy             one word, related senses
  homonymy             one form, unrelated senses
  pragmatics           meaning in context; implicature, presupposition
  coreference          which mentions refer to the same entity
  discourse            structure above the sentence
  Zipf's law           word frequency is inversely proportional to rank; a few
                       words dominate and the tail is enormous, which is why rare
                       word handling matters so much

Zipf's law has direct engineering consequences. The most frequent handful of word types
accounts for a large fraction of tokens, while the majority of types occur once or twice in any
corpus. This means a vocabulary large enough to cover most types is enormous and mostly
unused, while a small vocabulary leaves a substantial fraction of tokens unrepresented. Subword
tokenization resolves this by decomposing rare words into frequent pieces, so the vocabulary
stays fixed while any string remains representable.

Q: A model trained on English word tokens performs poorly when applied to Turkish. The most
fundamental cause is:
A. Turkish has fewer speakers
B. Turkish is agglutinative, so word-level vocabulary is effectively unbounded and most words
   in test data are unseen
C. Turkish has different word order
D. The training corpus was too small
Reasoning: Agglutination forms words by stacking morphemes, producing an unbounded set of
possible word forms. Word-level vocabulary therefore covers a small fraction of test tokens
regardless of corpus size, and each unseen word is opaque even when its morphemes are familiar.
Subword tokenization addresses this directly. Answer: B.

## Preprocessing and Representation

Text preprocessing prepares raw text for modelling, and the appropriate steps depend entirely
on the task and the model. Lowercasing removes a distinction that matters for named entities and
does not for topic classification. Stopword removal discards frequent function words, which
helps sparse bag-of-words models and destroys the syntactic information a contextual model uses.
Stemming and lemmatization normalize inflected forms, which reduces sparsity and conflates
distinctions. Each is a lossy transformation justified only when the discarded information is
irrelevant to the task, and modern contextual models generally want the raw text.

Unicode handling is the preprocessing concern that causes the most subtle bugs. Text may
contain multiple byte representations of the same visual character, since accented characters
can be a single code point or a base plus a combining mark, so normalization to a canonical
form is required before any comparison. Invisible characters — zero-width joiners, directional
marks, non-breaking spaces — are present in real text and break naive tokenization and matching.
And confusable characters from different scripts that render identically are a security concern
in identifiers and domain names.

Text representation converts strings to vectors. Bag-of-words counts term occurrences and
discards order entirely, which is a severe loss and is adequate for topic-level tasks. TF-IDF
weights terms by frequency in the document against rarity across the corpus, which down-weights
terms appearing everywhere and is a strong baseline for retrieval. Static word embeddings assign
each word a dense vector learned from co-occurrence, capturing semantic similarity and assigning
one vector per word type regardless of context. Contextual embeddings produce a different vector
per occurrence, resolving polysemy.

Representation approaches:
  bag of words         counts; no order, sparse, high dimensional
  n-grams              limited local order; sparsity grows quickly with n
  TF-IDF               term frequency times inverse document frequency; a strong
                       and cheap retrieval baseline
  BM25                 a refined TF-IDF with length normalization and term
                       frequency saturation; the lexical retrieval standard
  word2vec / GloVe     static dense embeddings; one vector per type
  analogy structure    static embeddings encode some relational structure in
                       vector offsets, an effect that is real and weaker than
                       early presentations suggested
  contextual embeddings  a vector per occurrence; resolves polysemy
  sentence embeddings  a vector per sentence or document; enables semantic
                       similarity and clustering
  subword tokenization  BPE, WordPiece, Unigram; fixed vocabulary, any string
                       representable
  normalization        Unicode canonical form before any comparison
  the preprocessing rule  each step is lossy; justify it by the task rather than
                       applying a standard pipeline

The shift from static to contextual embeddings resolved a fundamental limitation worth stating.
A static embedding assigns one vector to "bank", which must simultaneously represent a financial
institution and a river edge, so it lands somewhere between and represents neither well. A
contextual model computes the representation from the surrounding text, so the two senses receive
different vectors. This is the single largest quality improvement in the field's history and it
is what made transfer learning from pretrained models effective.

## Classical Tasks and Sequence Labelling

Text classification assigns a label to a document and is the most common applied task —
sentiment, topic, intent, spam, routing. It is well served by a hierarchy of approaches whose
appropriate choice depends on data volume: a linear model on TF-IDF features is a strong
baseline requiring little data and no compute, a fine-tuned pretrained encoder is stronger and
requires labelled examples in the hundreds to thousands, and a prompted large model requires no
task-specific labels at all and is more expensive per inference.

Sequence labelling assigns a label to each token and covers named entity recognition,
part-of-speech tagging, and chunking. The output has structure that independent per-token
classification ignores, since labels are correlated — a token inside an entity is likely
adjacent to another token inside the same entity. Conditional random fields model those
dependencies explicitly and were the standard before neural sequence models, and a CRF layer on
top of a neural encoder remains a useful combination because it enforces valid label sequences
that the encoder alone may violate.

The tagging scheme matters for extraction tasks and is a frequent source of evaluation
confusion. The BIO scheme marks each token as beginning an entity, inside one, or outside any,
which permits adjacent entities of the same type to be distinguished. Variants add explicit end
and single-token markers. Evaluation must be at the entity level rather than the token level,
because a system that gets three of four tokens of an entity correct has not extracted the
entity — and token-level metrics report that as 75% correct, which substantially overstates the
useful performance.

Classical tasks:
  classification       document to label; the most common applied task
  named entity recognition  extract and type spans; people, organizations,
                       locations, dates, domain-specific types
  BIO tagging          beginning, inside, outside; distinguishes adjacent entities
  entity-level eval    partial matches are failures; token-level metrics overstate
  part of speech       word class per token
  dependency parsing   head-dependent structure; more portable across languages
                       than constituency
  coreference          which mentions corefer; hard, and required for anything
                       reading multi-sentence text carefully
  relation extraction  typed relations between entities
  information extraction  populating a structured schema from text; the practical
                       goal that the above tasks serve
  CRF                  models label dependencies; enforces valid sequences
  nested entities      spans within spans; standard BIO cannot represent them
  span-based models    predict spans directly; handle nesting and overlap

Nested entities are worth noting because standard tagging cannot express them and real text
contains them constantly. An organization name containing a location, a person's title
containing an organization, a date range containing dates — each is a span within a span, and a
scheme assigning one label per token cannot represent both. Span-based models that enumerate
candidate spans and classify them handle this directly, at greater computational cost, and are
the appropriate choice when the domain has nested structure.

## Retrieval and Semantic Search

Retrieval finds relevant documents for a query, and the field has two complementary families
with different failure modes. Lexical retrieval matches terms, is fast, requires no training,
handles rare terms and exact matches well, and fails on vocabulary mismatch — a query and a
relevant document expressing the same concept in different words. Dense retrieval embeds queries
and documents in a shared space and matches by vector similarity, which handles paraphrase and
synonymy and fails on rare terms and exact identifiers that the embedding does not represent
distinctly.

Hybrid retrieval combines both and reliably outperforms either, which is the practical
recommendation. The combination may be a weighted score fusion, a reciprocal rank fusion that
requires no score calibration, or a two-stage pipeline where one method retrieves candidates and
the other reranks. Reciprocal rank fusion is the pragmatic default because it needs no tuning and
is robust to the two systems having incomparable score scales.

Reranking with a cross-encoder is the highest-value addition to a retrieval pipeline. A
bi-encoder embeds query and document independently, which permits precomputation and fast
search and prevents any interaction between them. A cross-encoder processes the pair jointly,
which is far more accurate and requires scoring each candidate individually — so it is
infeasible for retrieval and ideal for reranking the top candidates that retrieval produced.

Retrieval components:
  BM25                 lexical; fast, no training, exact match, rare terms
  vocabulary mismatch  lexical retrieval's characteristic failure
  dense retrieval      embedding similarity; paraphrase and synonymy
  rare term failure    dense retrieval's characteristic failure; identifiers,
                       part numbers, names
  hybrid               combine both; reliably better than either
  reciprocal rank fusion  combine rankings without score calibration
  bi-encoder           independent encoding; precomputable, fast, less accurate
  cross-encoder        joint encoding; accurate, not precomputable, use for
                       reranking
  ANN index            approximate nearest neighbour; HNSW and IVF are the common
                       families
  chunking             documents split for embedding; chunk size and overlap
                       materially affect retrieval quality and are underexplored
                       in most implementations
  metadata filtering   restrict by date, source, permissions; frequently more
                       important than semantic ranking
  query expansion      add related terms or generate a hypothetical answer to
                       embed instead of the query
  RAG                  retrieve then generate; grounds generation in sources and
                       makes attribution possible

Chunking deserves more attention than it typically receives because it determines what can be
retrieved at all. A chunk too small lacks the context to be interpretable and to match a query
about the broader topic. A chunk too large dilutes the embedding across multiple topics so that
none matches strongly, and it wastes generation context. Splitting on semantic boundaries —
sections, paragraphs — rather than at fixed character counts preserves coherence, and including
a small overlap prevents a relevant passage from being split across two chunks such that neither
contains it fully. Adding document-level context to each chunk, such as the title and section
path, improves retrieval substantially for a small cost.

Four changes, roughly in order of impact per effort.

Add lexical retrieval alongside your dense retriever and fuse the rankings. Dense retrieval
systematically misses exact identifiers, product codes, names, and rare technical terms, because
the embedding does not distinguish them from similar-looking alternatives. BM25 handles those
precisely. Reciprocal rank fusion combines the two rankings without needing the scores to be
comparable, and hybrid retrieval typically adds ten to twenty points of recall over dense alone.

Add chunk overlap. With zero overlap, a passage spanning a chunk boundary is split so that
neither chunk contains the complete answer, and both embed poorly. An overlap of ten to twenty
percent of the chunk size costs storage and eliminates this failure.

Retrieve more candidates and rerank. Three is far too few to feed a generator, and the fix is
retrieving perhaps fifty and reranking with a cross-encoder to select the best five. The
cross-encoder scores the query and passage jointly rather than comparing precomputed vectors,
which is substantially more accurate, and scoring fifty candidates is entirely affordable.

Then add metadata filtering if your corpus has structure. Restricting by date, document type,
or access permission before ranking removes irrelevant candidates that would otherwise crowd out
correct ones, and it is frequently a larger win than any ranking improvement.

Two measurement points. Evaluate retrieval separately from generation, as you have started to
do — the two failure modes need different fixes and an end-to-end number cannot distinguish
them. And once retrieval recall is above about 85%, re-examine the generation: if answers are
still wrong with the correct passage present, the problem moves to prompt construction, context
ordering, or the model's tendency to answer from parametric knowledge rather than from the
provided context.

## Generation, Evaluation, and Practice

Text generation is now dominated by large autoregressive models, and the practical concerns
have shifted from architecture to control and evaluation. Decoding strategy shapes the output's
character: greedy decoding is deterministic and repetitive, beam search suits tasks with a single
correct answer such as translation and produces bland text for open-ended generation, and
sampling with temperature and nucleus truncation is standard for creative or conversational
output.

Evaluation of generated text is the field's weakest area. Overlap-based metrics compare against
reference outputs and correlate poorly with human judgment for anything but the most constrained
tasks, because many good outputs share few words with any single reference. Embedding-based
metrics compare semantic similarity and improve on this while still requiring references.
Model-based judges evaluate outputs against criteria and correlate better with human judgment,
at the cost of inheriting the judge's biases — including documented preferences for longer
outputs, for outputs from the same model family, and for the first option presented.

Hallucination — generating fluent, confident, incorrect content — is the failure mode that
most limits deployment. It arises because the training objective rewards plausible continuation
rather than truth, and the model has no mechanism distinguishing what it knows from what it is
constructing. The mitigations are grounding generation in retrieved sources with explicit
attribution, constraining output format so claims are checkable, calibrating and surfacing
uncertainty, and verifying claims against sources after generation.

Generation and evaluation:
  greedy               deterministic, repetitive
  beam search          single-correct-answer tasks; bland for open generation
  temperature          scales the logits; lower is sharper
  top-p / nucleus      truncates to the smallest set exceeding a probability mass
  repetition penalty   discourages loops; heavy-handed and can degrade fluency
  BLEU / ROUGE         n-gram overlap; poor correlation with quality for open
                       tasks
  BERTScore            embedding similarity; better, still reference-based
  LLM-as-judge         best correlation with human judgment; inherits biases
                       toward length, self-preference, and position
  human evaluation     the standard; expensive, slow, and needs careful protocol
  hallucination        fluent and wrong; the primary deployment limitation
  grounding            retrieve and attribute; makes claims checkable
  structured output    constrained decoding to a schema; makes validation possible
  calibration          the model's confidence should track its accuracy; it

Multilingual and cross-lingual work carries its own concerns that English-centric development
tends to miss. Resource availability varies by orders of magnitude across languages, so a method
validated on English may fail where training data is scarce. Tokenizers trained predominantly on
English fragment other scripts into many more tokens, which costs context, costs money per token,
and degrades quality — a fairness issue as much as a technical one. Evaluation datasets for most
languages are translations of English ones, which carry English discourse structure and cultural
assumptions and therefore overstate performance on genuinely native text.

The practical concerns in deployment are consistent across tasks. Latency and cost scale with
model size and with tokens processed, so the architectural question is usually how to do the
minimum work per request — caching, routing easy cases to smaller models, and constraining
output length. Privacy matters because text frequently contains personal information, which
means detection and redaction before storage or transmission to a third-party service. And
domain adaptation matters more than in most fields, because language varies enormously by domain
— clinical notes, legal contracts, and social media differ in vocabulary, syntax, and
conventions so completely that a model performing well on one may be near-useless on another
without adaptation.
