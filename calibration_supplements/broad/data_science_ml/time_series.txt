## Components, Stationarity, and Structure

A time series is a sequence of observations ordered in time, and the ordering is what makes
it a distinct subject. Observations are not independent — that is the entire point — so
every technique built on the independence assumption needs modification or replacement.

Decomposition separates a series into interpretable components. Trend is the long-run
direction. Seasonality is periodic variation at a fixed known frequency — day of week,
month of year, hour of day — and a series can carry several seasonal periods
simultaneously. Cyclical variation is longer-term oscillation without a fixed period, such
as business cycles, and is distinguished from seasonality precisely by that irregularity.
The remainder is what the components do not explain.

The additive versus multiplicative distinction matters practically. In an additive
decomposition the seasonal swing has constant absolute magnitude; in a multiplicative one it
scales with the level. Retail sales usually behave multiplicatively — a December uplift is
a percentage, not a fixed number of units — and applying an additive model produces
systematic errors that grow with the level. Taking logs converts a multiplicative structure
to additive, which is why log transforms are so common in this field.

Stationarity is the property most classical methods require. A series is weakly stationary
when its mean, variance, and autocovariance structure do not change over time. Most real
series are not: they trend, their variance grows with their level, their seasonal pattern
evolves. Differencing removes a trend, seasonal differencing removes a seasonal pattern,
and a log or Box-Cox transform stabilizes variance. The number of differences required is
the integration order, and over-differencing is a real error that introduces artificial
negative autocorrelation and inflates forecast variance.

Core concepts and diagnostics:
  autocorrelation (ACF)    correlation with lagged values; decays slowly for a
                           trending series, spikes at seasonal lags
  partial autocorr (PACF)  correlation at a lag after removing shorter lags;
                           identifies AR order
  white noise              no autocorrelation at any lag; what residuals should be
  random walk              each value is the last plus noise; ACF decays very
                           slowly, and the best forecast is the last observation
  ADF / KPSS tests         stationarity tests with opposite null hypotheses; run
                           both, since they disagree informatively
  differencing             y_t - y_{t-1};  removes a linear trend
  seasonal differencing    y_t - y_{t-m};  removes a seasonal pattern of period m
  Ljung-Box test           tests whether residual autocorrelation is jointly zero
  spurious regression      two independent trending series correlate strongly;
                           the classic trap that motivates stationarity

Spurious regression deserves emphasis because it produces confident nonsense. Two
independent random walks will typically show a high R-squared and a highly significant
coefficient when regressed on each other, because both are trending and the standard errors
computed under independence are badly wrong. This is why regressions on levels of
non-stationary series are untrustworthy and why the analysis is done on differences, or
within a cointegration framework when the series genuinely share a long-run relationship.

Q: A series' ACF decays very slowly and its PACF has a single large spike at lag 1. The
series most plausibly:
A. Is white noise
B. Requires differencing; it behaves like a random walk or strongly autoregressive process
C. Has strong seasonality at lag 1
D. Is over-differenced
Reasoning: Slow ACF decay indicates non-stationarity or a near-unit-root autoregressive
process; a single PACF spike at lag 1 with slow ACF decay is the signature of an AR(1) with
coefficient near one, which is effectively a random walk. Differencing once should produce a
stationary series with no remaining structure. Over-differencing shows the opposite pattern:
a large negative ACF at lag 1. Answer: B.

## Classical Forecasting Models

Exponential smoothing forecasts by weighting past observations with exponentially decaying
weights, so recent data matters more. Simple exponential smoothing handles a level with no
trend or seasonality, using one smoothing parameter. Holt's method adds a trend component
with a second parameter. Holt-Winters adds seasonality with a third, in additive or
multiplicative form. The family is organized systematically by the error, trend, and
seasonal specification, giving a set of models from which one can be selected by information
criterion.

Damped trend variants deserve specific mention because they are consistently better in
practice. An undamped linear trend extrapolates indefinitely, which produces absurd
long-horizon forecasts — a product growing at last quarter's rate for five years. Damping
the trend so it flattens toward the horizon is more realistic and empirically improves
accuracy across large forecasting competitions, and it should generally be the default.

ARIMA models the series as a combination of autoregressive terms (regression on its own
past values), differencing for integration, and moving average terms (regression on past
forecast errors). Seasonal ARIMA adds the same three components at the seasonal lag. Order
selection was traditionally done by inspecting ACF and PACF plots and remains a useful
diagnostic skill, though automated selection by information criterion is now standard and
performs comparably.

Classical model families:
  naive                 forecast equals the last value; the benchmark every model
                        must beat, and frequently does not for financial series
  seasonal naive        forecast equals the value from the same season last cycle;
                        a strong benchmark for seasonal data
  drift                 naive plus the average historical change
  simple exp smoothing  level only; one parameter
  Holt                  level and trend
  Holt-Winters          level, trend, seasonality; additive or multiplicative
  damped trend          flattens the extrapolated trend; usually better
  ARIMA(p,d,q)          autoregressive, integrated, moving average
  SARIMA                plus seasonal terms at lag m
  ARIMAX / dynamic reg  ARIMA errors around a regression on external predictors
  TBATS                 multiple and non-integer seasonal periods
  state space / structural  components as latent states; handles missing data and
                        irregular sampling naturally

The benchmark discipline is the most important practice in the field and the most
frequently skipped. Any forecasting model must be compared against naive, seasonal naive,
and a simple exponential smoothing baseline on the same evaluation protocol. Complex models
routinely fail to beat seasonal naive on real data, and a project that reports only its own
model's error has not established that the model is worth deploying. For many financial
series the naive forecast is genuinely difficult to beat, which is informative about the
series rather than about the modeller.

## Evaluation

Evaluation of forecasts requires a protocol respecting time. Random cross-validation
splits are invalid because they train on the future to predict the past, and any temporal
autocorrelation converts that leakage into an inflated score. The correct approach is
rolling-origin evaluation: train on data up to a point, forecast the following horizon,
score it, advance the origin, and repeat. This produces many forecasts at each horizon and
reflects how the model will actually be used.

The forecast horizon must match the decision. A model evaluated on one-step-ahead accuracy
tells you nothing about its twelve-step performance, and the two can rank models
differently — a model capturing short-run momentum wins at one step and loses at twelve
where the level matters more. Accuracy should be reported per horizon, not averaged into a
single number that obscures where the model is useful.

Metric choice interacts with the data's properties in ways that cause real mistakes.
Percentage errors are undefined at zero and explode near it, which makes them unusable for
intermittent demand. They are also asymmetric, penalizing over-forecasts more than
under-forecasts, which biases model selection toward under-forecasting. Scale-dependent
metrics cannot be aggregated across series with different magnitudes.

Forecast evaluation:
  rolling origin         train, forecast, advance; the only valid protocol
  per-horizon reporting  accuracy at h=1 says nothing about h=12
  MAE                    scale-dependent, robust, optimal for the median
  RMSE                   scale-dependent, penalizes large errors, optimal for the
                         mean
  MAPE                   scale-free, undefined at zero, asymmetric; avoid despite
                         its popularity
  sMAPE                  symmetric variant; still unstable near zero
  MASE                   error scaled by the naive method's in-sample error;
                         scale-free, defined at zero, interpretable — below 1 beats
                         naive. The best general default.
  pinball loss           for quantile forecasts; the proper scoring rule
  CRPS                   for full predictive distributions
  coverage               fraction of actuals within the prediction interval;
                         should match the nominal level

The choice between MAE and RMSE is not stylistic: they are minimized by different
predictions. MAE is minimized by the conditional median and RMSE by the conditional mean, so
for a skewed distribution they call for different forecasts. If the business cost of error
is roughly linear, optimize MAE; if large errors are disproportionately costly, optimize
RMSE. Reporting one and optimizing the other produces a model that is systematically
mistuned for its own evaluation.

Prediction intervals matter more than point forecasts for most decisions and are the part
most often wrong. Intervals from classical models assume the model is correctly specified
and the parameters known, so they systematically understate uncertainty — empirical coverage
of nominal 95 percent intervals is frequently near 80. Intervals should be validated against
actual coverage on held-out data, and if they are too narrow, widened empirically or
replaced with conformal methods that provide distribution-free coverage guarantees.

## Machine Learning Approaches

Machine learning methods for time series reframe forecasting as supervised learning by
constructing features from the history. Lagged values, rolling statistics over various
windows, calendar features, and external regressors become the feature matrix, and any
standard regressor can be applied. Gradient-boosted trees on well-constructed features are
a very strong baseline and win a large share of forecasting competitions, particularly for
many related series where a single global model can be trained across all of them.

Feature construction for this framing has one dominant rule: every feature must be computable
at forecast time using only information available then. A rolling mean must be lagged so it
does not include the target period. A feature derived from a variable that is itself
forecast must account for that variable's forecast error. Violating this produces
spectacular backtest results and production failure, and it is the single most common error
in machine learning applied to forecasting.

The global versus local model distinction is where machine learning offers a genuine
advantage over classical methods. Classical models are fit per series, so a series with
short history cannot be modelled well. A global model trained across thousands of related
series learns shared patterns and transfers them, which is why global models dominate in
retail and demand forecasting where there are many series each with limited history. Series
identity enters as a categorical feature or an embedding.

Approaches and where each fits:
  gradient boosting     on lag and calendar features; the strongest general
                        baseline, especially for many related series
  linear with features  fast, interpretable, surprisingly competitive
  RNN / LSTM            sequential; largely superseded
  temporal convolution  dilated causal convolutions; parallel training, long
                        receptive field
  transformer variants  attention over the sequence; strong at scale, and often
                        beaten by simpler methods on modest data
  N-BEATS / N-HiTS      deep architectures with basis expansion; competitive and
                        interpretable in components
  DeepAR                probabilistic global model; outputs distributions
  foundation models     pretrained across many series, zero-shot forecasting;
                        promising and not yet dominant
  hybrid                classical model for structure, ML for the residual

The consistent empirical finding across competitions deserves stating plainly: simple
methods, properly evaluated, are hard to beat, and combining forecasts beats individual
models reliably. A simple average of several reasonable models almost always outperforms
the best individual one selected on validation data, because model selection overfits the
validation set while averaging reduces variance. The winning entries in large forecasting
competitions have repeatedly been ensembles of relatively simple methods rather than single
sophisticated ones.

## Hierarchical, Probabilistic, and Anomaly Detection

Hierarchical forecasting arises when series aggregate — products into categories into
regions into totals — and forecasts must be coherent, meaning the parts sum to the whole.
Forecasting each level independently produces incoherent results, since the sum of
independently forecast components will not equal the independently forecast total.

Three reconciliation strategies exist and the third dominates. Bottom-up forecasts the
lowest level and aggregates, which preserves detail and is noisy where individual series are
sparse. Top-down forecasts the total and disaggregates by historical proportions, which is
stable and loses series-specific information. Optimal reconciliation forecasts every level
independently and then projects the whole set onto the coherent subspace, using the
forecast error covariance to weight the adjustment — which uses information from all levels
and empirically beats both simpler approaches.

Probabilistic forecasting outputs a distribution rather than a point, and it is what most
decisions actually require. An inventory decision needs a high quantile of demand, not the
mean, because the costs of stockout and overstock are asymmetric. A capacity decision needs
the tail. Quantile regression targets specific quantiles directly using pinball loss;
distributional models output parameters of an assumed family; ensemble and simulation
approaches generate sample paths, which is the most flexible representation since any
functional of the distribution can be computed from samples. Conformal prediction provides
finite-sample coverage guarantees under exchangeability with adaptations for the temporal
setting.

Anomaly detection and change points:
  point anomaly        a single unusual observation; a spike or a sensor error
  contextual anomaly   normal in isolation, unusual in context — a value normal
                       for July occurring in January
  collective anomaly   a subsequence unusual as a group though each point is
                       ordinary
  level shift          a permanent change in the mean
  variance change      a change in volatility, often preceding a level change
  forecast residual    the general-purpose method: model the series, flag large
                       standardized residuals
  seasonal-hybrid ESD  decompose, then test residuals for outliers
  CUSUM                cumulative sum of deviations; detects small persistent
                       shifts efficiently
  Bayesian changepoint  posterior over change locations; handles multiple changes
  matrix profile       finds motifs and discords by subsequence similarity

The practical difficulty in anomaly detection is not the algorithm but the definition. A
spike may be an error to be removed, a genuine event to be explained, or the very thing the
system exists to detect, and no algorithm distinguishes these. The consequential decision is
what happens after a detection — page a human, suppress the value, retrain, or record it —
and designing that response first usually clarifies what should be detected. A detector
tuned without reference to the response either produces alerts nobody acts on, which
trains people to ignore it, or misses the cases that mattered.

Concept drift is the time series analogue of model staleness and requires monitoring rather
than a one-time fix. The relationship between features and target changes — consumer
behaviour shifts, a competitor enters, a pandemic occurs — and a model trained on the old
relationship degrades. Detecting it requires tracking forecast error over time and comparing
against its historical distribution, since a sustained rise in error is the signal. The
response is usually periodic retraining on a rolling window, with the window length itself a
tradeoff: short windows adapt quickly and use less data, long windows are more stable and
slower to adapt. Where the change is abrupt and identifiable, an explicit intervention
variable or a regime-switching model is preferable to hoping the retraining absorbs it.
