## Ranking Objectives and Loss Functions

Ranking differs from classification and regression because the quantity being optimized is an
ordering rather than a value. A model that predicts every relevance score slightly wrong but in the
right order is perfect for ranking; a model with lower squared error and a scrambled order is
useless. The loss functions of the field are attempts to express that distinction, and they divide
into three families.

Pointwise approaches treat each item independently, predicting its relevance as a regression or
classification problem and sorting by the prediction. They are simple, they use standard machinery,
and they optimize a proxy — accurate scores — rather than the objective. In practice they work
better than the theory suggests, particularly when the score has a meaningful calibrated
interpretation that downstream systems need.

Pairwise approaches learn from comparisons: given two items, which should rank higher. This matches
the objective more closely and matches the available data, since behaviour data naturally produces
preferences rather than absolute grades. Listwise approaches consider the whole ranked list and
optimize a metric-aligned objective directly, which is the closest match to what is wanted and
requires smoothing, since the metrics of interest are step functions of the ordering and have zero
gradient almost everywhere.

  the data match       behaviour naturally produces preferences

  the gradient problem  ranking metrics are step functions with zero gradient
  smoothed surrogates  differentiable approximations of the metric
  position weighting   errors near the top matter more; the loss should reflect

  the practical finding  the family matters less than the features and the
                       training data quality

Q: A ranking model achieves lower squared error on relevance labels than the previous model but
performs worse on ranking metrics. This is possible because:
A. The evaluation is broken
B. Squared error rewards accurate absolute scores while ranking metrics depend only on ordering;
   a model can reduce average error while making more inversions among the top results
C. The labels are wrong
D. The model is overfitting
Reasoning: The two objectives are genuinely different. Reducing error on many mid-relevance items
can outweigh degradation on the few top items, which is where ranking metrics place nearly all their
weight. Optimizing the metric you evaluate is the resolution. Answer: B.

## Features and Signals

Ranking features fall into categories that behave differently and should be thought about
separately. Query features describe the request: its length, its detected intent, its language, its
rarity. Document features describe the item independent of the query: its quality, its popularity,
its age, its length. Query-document features describe the match between them: lexical overlap
scores, embedding similarity, field-specific matches. And context features describe the situation:
device, location, time, session history.

The query-document features carry most of the relevance signal and the document features carry most
of the quality signal, and separating these is useful because they fail differently. A model relying
heavily on popularity produces a ranking that is safe and stale, reinforcing whatever is already
popular; a model relying only on match produces relevant but low-quality results. The balance
between them is a policy decision that should be made explicitly rather than emerging from whatever
the training data happened to favour.

Feature freshness and availability at serving time are practical constraints that shape which
features are usable. A feature computed from a nightly batch is a day stale, which is fatal for
anything time-sensitive. A feature requiring a service call adds latency to every request and a
dependency that can fail. Training with features that are unavailable or differently computed at
serving time is the classic training-serving skew, and it produces models that evaluate well offline
and underperform in production for reasons that are hard to find without explicitly comparing
feature distributions across the two paths.

  context features     device, location, time, session

## Embedding Retrieval and Two-Tower Models

Dense retrieval encodes queries and documents into a shared vector space and retrieves by nearest
neighbour. The architecture that makes this practical at scale is the two-tower model: separate
encoders for query and document, with no interaction between them until a final similarity
computation. This separation is what allows document embeddings to be computed offline and indexed,
so serving requires only encoding the query and searching the index.

The cost of that separation is expressiveness. Because the towers never see each other's input, the
model cannot represent interactions between specific query terms and specific document passages,
which a cross-encoder scoring the pair jointly can. Cross-encoders are substantially more accurate
and cannot be precomputed, so the standard architecture uses the two-tower model for retrieval and a
cross-encoder for reranking the small candidate set.

Training a two-tower model requires negatives, and the choice of negatives dominates the result.
In-batch negatives are free and easy and are mostly trivially irrelevant, which teaches the model
only to distinguish obviously different documents. Hard negatives — documents that are similar but
not relevant, typically mined from a first-pass retriever — are what teach the fine distinctions that
matter, and models trained without them retrieve plausible-looking but wrong results. The
counterpart risk is false negatives: mined hard negatives may actually be relevant but unlabelled,
and training against them actively harms the model.

Embedding retrieval:
  two-tower model      separate encoders, late interaction
  the enabling property  document embeddings precomputed and indexed
  the expressiveness cost  no term-level interaction between query and document
  cross-encoder        joint scoring; accurate and not precomputable
  the standard cascade  two-tower retrieve, cross-encoder rerank
  distillation         a cross-encoder teaching a two-tower model; recovers part
                       of the gap
  in-batch negatives   free, mostly trivial
  hard negatives       similar but not relevant; where the learning happens
  mining               a first-pass retriever supplying candidates
  false negatives      mined negatives that are actually relevant; actively
                       harmful
  the mitigation       score-based filtering or a threshold on the mining
                       retriever
  index refresh        embeddings must be recomputed when the model changes; a

  the operational implication  model iteration speed is bounded by reindex time,

                       architecture

## Behaviour Data and Bias Correction

Explicit relevance judgements are expensive and limited in volume; behavioural data is abundant and
biased. Clicks, dwell time, purchases, and subsequent queries all carry relevance signal and all
carry systematic distortions that must be corrected or the model learns the distortions rather than
relevance.

Position bias is the largest. Items shown higher receive more clicks regardless of relevance, so a
model trained on raw clicks learns to reproduce the current ranking. Since the current ranking came
from the previous model, the system converges on its own history and stops improving. Correcting it
requires either an estimate of the examination probability by position — used to weight each
observation inversely — or randomization that breaks the correlation between position and relevance
in a fraction of traffic.

Other biases compound this. Presentation bias means items with images or particular formats attract
attention independently of relevance. Selection bias means only items the current system retrieved
were ever seen, so nothing is learned about items it never showed. Trust bias means users click
top results partly because they trust the ranking. And the feedback loop closes on all of them: today's
model determines what data is collected, which trains tomorrow's model. Deliberate exploration —
occasionally showing items the model ranks lower — is the only reliable way to break the loop, and
it costs immediate quality in exchange for the ability to improve.

## Evaluation and Serving

Offline evaluation on a judgement set iterates quickly and correlates imperfectly with online
outcomes. Counterfactual evaluation using logged data with propensity weights estimates what a new
ranker would have achieved on past traffic, which is stronger than a judgement set and depends on
the logged policy having had support for the actions the new policy would take. Online evaluation is
authoritative, and interleaving — mixing two rankings within one result list and attributing clicks —
is far more sensitive than a conventional split test for ranking comparisons specifically.

Serving architecture is the multi-stage cascade, and each stage's job is to reduce the candidate set
cheaply enough that the next stage can afford to be more expensive. Retrieval produces thousands
from millions; a lightweight ranker reduces to hundreds; a heavy reranker orders the top tens; and
final policies apply diversity, deduplication, and business rules. The latency budget is divided
among these, and the design question at each stage is how much recall is lost for how much cost
saved.

Recall at the first stage bounds everything downstream, and it is the metric most often unmeasured.
A reranker cannot recover a relevant document the retriever never returned, so an investment in
reranking quality is wasted if the retrieval stage is the binding constraint. Measuring stage-wise
recall against a superset produced by an expensive method is the diagnostic that tells you where to
spend effort, and it frequently redirects work from the sophisticated end of the pipeline to the
simple end.
