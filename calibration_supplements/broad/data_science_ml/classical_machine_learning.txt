## Learning Theory and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff

Supervised learning assumes examples are drawn independently from a fixed but unknown
distribution, and the goal is to find a function minimizing expected loss on new draws
from that distribution. Everything difficult about the field follows from the fact that
you can only measure loss on the sample you have, while you care about the population
you do not.

The bias-variance decomposition makes the tension precise. Expected squared error at a
point decomposes into squared bias — how far the average prediction over training sets
sits from the truth — plus variance — how much the prediction moves across training sets
— plus irreducible noise. A model that is too simple has high bias and cannot represent
the truth however much data it sees. A model that is too flexible has high variance and
fits the particular noise in its training set. The classical picture is a U-shaped test
error as complexity increases, with an optimum in between.

That classical picture is incomplete, and the modern qualification matters. Very
overparameterized models — deep networks, wide random forests, high-dimensional
interpolators — exhibit double descent: test error rises to a peak near the
interpolation threshold where the model has just enough capacity to fit the training data
exactly, then falls again as capacity increases further. The explanation involves
implicit regularization: among the many parameter settings that fit the training data,
gradient descent finds ones with small norm, and those generalize. This does not repeal
the bias-variance tradeoff so much as show that "complexity" measured by parameter count
is the wrong axis.

No free lunch theorems state that averaged over all possible problems, every learning
algorithm performs identically. The theorem is often quoted as though it counselled
agnosticism; its real content is the opposite. It says that all performance comes from
assumptions matching the structure of the actual problem, so the practical question is
never which algorithm is best but which algorithm's inductive bias matches your data.
Convolution assumes locality and translation invariance. Trees assume axis-aligned
piecewise-constant structure. Linear models assume additivity. Kernel methods assume
smoothness in a chosen metric. Matching the bias to the domain is the work.

Core learning concepts:
  empirical risk       average loss on the training sample
  generalization gap   difference between empirical and expected risk
  overfitting          low training loss, high test loss; variance-dominated
  underfitting         high loss on both; bias-dominated
  capacity             VC dimension, Rademacher complexity; formal measures of
                       how much a model class can fit
  regularization       any modification reducing variance at some bias cost
  inductive bias       the assumptions that make generalization possible at all
  curse of dimension   volume grows exponentially, so data becomes sparse and
                       distance-based methods degrade

The curse of dimensionality has a specific and often-missed consequence for
distance-based methods: as dimension grows, the ratio between the nearest and farthest
neighbour distances approaches one, so "nearest neighbour" ceases to be meaningfully
different from "any neighbour". This is why k-nearest-neighbours degrades sharply in
high dimensions, why distance-based clustering needs dimensionality reduction first, and
why feature selection is not merely a convenience but often a precondition for these
methods working at all.

Q: A model achieves 99% training accuracy and 71% test accuracy. The most appropriate
first intervention is:
A. Increase model capacity
B. Reduce capacity or add regularization, and get more data if possible
C. Train for more epochs
D. Change the evaluation metric
Reasoning: The 28-point gap is the signature of variance-dominated error — the model is
fitting the training set's noise. Increasing capacity or training longer worsens it. The
remedies that reduce variance are regularization, simpler models, more data, and
ensembling. Answer: B.

## Linear Models

Linear regression fits a hyperplane by least squares, and its enduring value is that it
is fast, exactly solvable, and interpretable: each coefficient is the expected change in
the target per unit change in that predictor, holding the others fixed. That "holding
the others fixed" clause is what makes coefficients hard to interpret when predictors are
correlated, since the data may contain no examples where one varies and the others do
not.

Logistic regression models the log-odds of a binary outcome as a linear function,
producing calibrated probabilities rather than raw scores. It is fit by maximum
likelihood, has a convex objective so there are no local optima, and its coefficients
exponentiate to odds ratios. It remains the default in medicine, credit scoring, and any
domain where the model must be explained and defended, and it is a strong baseline that
gradient-boosted trees often beat by only a few points on tabular data.

Regularized variants trade bias for variance. Ridge adds an L2 penalty, shrinking
coefficients smoothly and handling correlated predictors gracefully by splitting the
effect among them. Lasso adds an L1 penalty, driving coefficients exactly to zero and so
performing selection, but among correlated predictors it arbitrarily keeps one, making
the selected set unstable across resamples. Elastic net combines both and recovers
grouped selection. All require standardized features, since the penalty is scale
dependent, and none produce valid conventional p-values — post-selection inference is
genuinely hard and the naive standard errors from a lasso-selected model are
meaningless.

Linear model reference:
  OLS solution           beta = (X'X)^{-1} X'y;  compute via QR, never by inverting
  ridge                  beta = (X'X + lambda I)^{-1} X'y;  always invertible
  lasso                  no closed form; coordinate descent or LARS
  logistic link          log(p/(1-p)) = X beta;  p = 1/(1 + exp(-X beta))
  odds ratio             exp(beta);  multiplicative change in odds per unit
  multinomial            softmax over K classes; one coefficient vector per class
  Poisson regression     log link for counts; check for overdispersion
  regularization path    fit across lambda values; choose by cross-validation
  standardization        required for penalized models, harmless for plain OLS

Generalized linear models unify these by pairing an exponential-family distribution with
a link function. The choice of family should follow from the data-generating process:
binomial for binary outcomes, Poisson or negative binomial for counts, gamma for positive
continuous quantities with variance growing with the mean, Tweedie for the
zero-inflated positive continuous data common in insurance. Using ordinary least squares
on a count or a proportion is a frequent shortcut that produces impossible predictions
(negative counts, probabilities above one) and heteroskedastic residuals that invalidate
the standard errors.

## Trees and Ensembles

A decision tree recursively partitions the feature space by axis-aligned splits, choosing
at each node the split that most reduces impurity — Gini or entropy for classification,
variance for regression. The result is interpretable, handles mixed data types natively,
requires no scaling, captures interactions automatically, and is invariant to monotone
transformations of the features. It is also unstable: a small change in the data can
produce an entirely different tree, because an early split changes everything below it.

That instability is precisely what makes trees ideal ensemble members. Bagging trains
many trees on bootstrap resamples and averages them, and the variance of an average of B
correlated predictors falls with B but is floored by the correlation between them.
Random forests reduce that correlation further by restricting each split to a random
subset of features, which is why they outperform plain bagging. The out-of-bag samples —
those excluded from each bootstrap — provide a free validation estimate without a
separate holdout.

Boosting takes the opposite approach: fit models sequentially, each correcting the
errors of the ensemble so far. Gradient boosting fits each new tree to the negative
gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions, which for squared error is
just the residual and for other losses generalizes cleanly. Because each tree is fit to
what remains, boosting reduces bias where bagging reduces variance, and boosted ensembles
of shallow trees typically outperform random forests on tabular data — at the cost of
sensitivity to hyperparameters and a real ability to overfit that random forests largely
lack.

Ensemble methods compared:
  bagging            parallel, independent, reduces variance; hard to overfit
  random forest      bagging plus feature subsampling; strong default, few knobs
  extra trees        random split thresholds too; faster, more variance reduction
  gradient boosting  sequential, reduces bias; best tabular accuracy, needs tuning
  XGBoost            regularized objective, second-order optimization, sparsity-aware
  LightGBM           histogram binning, leaf-wise growth; fastest on large data
  CatBoost           ordered target statistics for categoricals; best default
                     handling of high-cardinality categorical features
  stacking           train a meta-model on base model predictions; use out-of-fold
                     predictions or the meta-model learns from leakage

The key boosting hyperparameters interact in a predictable way. Learning rate and number
of trees trade off directly: halving the rate roughly doubles the trees needed, and lower
rates with more trees generalize slightly better at proportionally more compute. Tree
depth controls interaction order — depth 3 captures three-way interactions — and shallow
trees with many rounds usually beat deep trees with few. Subsampling rows and columns per
tree adds randomness that reduces variance. Early stopping on a validation set is the
single most valuable practice, because it removes the tree count from the search entirely.

Feature importance from tree ensembles is widely used and widely misinterpreted. The
default impurity-based importance is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous
features, which have more possible split points and therefore more chances to appear
useful even when uninformative. Permutation importance — measuring the performance drop
when a feature is randomly shuffled — is unbiased in that respect but attributes shared
credit arbitrarily among correlated features and can be misleading when permutation
creates impossible feature combinations. SHAP values provide a theoretically grounded
attribution with consistency guarantees and are the current best practice, at
substantially higher computational cost.

## Kernels, Distance Methods, and Clustering

Support vector machines find the hyperplane maximizing the margin between classes, which
is a convex optimization with a unique solution. The decision boundary depends only on
the support vectors — the points at or inside the margin — so the model is sparse in the
training data. Soft margins introduce slack variables allowing violations, with a
regularization parameter C controlling the tradeoff between margin width and violations.

The kernel trick is the idea with the widest reach. Because the SVM's optimization
depends on the data only through inner products, replacing the inner product with a
kernel function implicitly maps into a higher-dimensional space without ever computing
the coordinates there. The radial basis function kernel corresponds to an
infinite-dimensional space, so an RBF SVM can represent arbitrarily complex boundaries
while remaining a convex problem. The same trick applies to PCA, ridge regression, and
Gaussian processes, all of which have kernelized forms.

The limitation is scale. Kernel methods require the N-by-N kernel matrix, so memory grows
quadratically and training typically between quadratic and cubic in the number of
samples. Beyond a few tens of thousands of points this becomes prohibitive, which is why
kernel methods lost ground to trees and neural networks as datasets grew, and why
approximations like random Fourier features and the Nystrom method exist.

Clustering finds structure without labels, and the algorithms differ in what shape of
cluster they can find. K-means minimizes within-cluster variance and therefore finds
spherical clusters of similar size; it is fast, requires k in advance, and is sensitive
to initialization (k-means++ addresses this) and to feature scaling. Hierarchical
clustering builds a dendrogram permitting any number of clusters after the fact, at
quadratic or worse cost. DBSCAN groups by density, finding arbitrary shapes, identifying
outliers as noise, and requiring no k — but needing a density scale that may not be
uniform across the data. Gaussian mixture models fit soft, elliptical clusters by
expectation-maximization and give probabilistic assignments.

Clustering guidance:
  k-means             fast, spherical, needs k, scale-sensitive; use k-means++
  hierarchical        dendrogram, any linkage; O(n^2) memory, good for small n
  DBSCAN              density-based, arbitrary shapes, finds outliers, no k needed
  HDBSCAN             hierarchical DBSCAN; handles varying density, fewer knobs
  GMM                 soft assignment, elliptical, model-based; use BIC to choose k
  spectral            uses graph Laplacian eigenvectors; finds non-convex clusters
  choosing k          elbow on inertia (crude), silhouette score, gap statistic,
                      BIC for model-based; or a downstream task metric
  validation          without labels there is no ground truth; stability across
                      resamples is the most honest internal criterion

The uncomfortable truth about clustering is that it always returns clusters, whether or
not the data has any. Running k-means with k = 5 on uniform random noise produces five
tidy-looking clusters with a plausible silhouette score. Before interpreting clusters as
real structure, it is worth checking whether the clustering tendency exceeds what random
data of the same shape would produce (the Hopkins statistic, or a gap statistic against
a null reference), and whether the same clusters appear under resampling.

## Dimensionality Reduction and Imbalanced Data

Principal component analysis projects onto the directions of maximum variance, which are
the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix or equivalently the right singular vectors of
the centred data. It is linear, deterministic, and invertible up to the discarded
components, and the eigenvalues give the variance explained. Two cautions dominate
practice: PCA is not scale-invariant, so unstandardized features let whichever variable
has the largest units dominate the components; and variance is not relevance, so the
components that explain the most variance may not be the ones that predict the target —
supervised alternatives like partial least squares exist for that reason.

Nonlinear methods preserve local structure at the cost of interpretability and global
fidelity. t-SNE and UMAP produce visually compelling two-dimensional embeddings and are
heavily over-interpreted. In both, cluster sizes are meaningless, distances between
clusters are largely meaningless, and the apparent structure depends strongly on
hyperparameters — perplexity for t-SNE, n_neighbors and min_dist for UMAP. They are
excellent for visual exploration and for generating hypotheses, and they are not
appropriate as a preprocessing step feeding a downstream model, nor as evidence that
clusters are separable.

Class imbalance is a framing problem more often than an algorithmic one. With 1%
positives, accuracy is useless, and the first response should be to choose an appropriate
metric — average precision, or expected cost under the real asymmetry between false
positives and false negatives — rather than to alter the data. Resampling techniques
distort the predicted probabilities, which matters whenever the score is used for ranking
or expected-value decisions rather than a hard classification.

Handling imbalance:
  do nothing first     train on the natural distribution, optimize log loss, and
                       choose the threshold afterward from the cost structure.
                       This is correct more often than the literature suggests.
  class weights        reweight the loss; equivalent to resampling in expectation
                       without duplicating data
  random undersampling discards majority data; fast, loses information
  random oversampling  duplicates minority data; risks overfitting those points
  SMOTE                synthesizes minority points by interpolation; can create
                       implausible examples in high dimensions or across a
                       nonlinear class boundary
  threshold tuning     the highest-value intervention; the default 0.5 is almost
                       never the right operating point
  calibration          Platt scaling or isotonic regression after resampling, to
                       restore probability meaning

Interpretability requirements should shape model choice from the start rather than being
retrofitted. Intrinsically interpretable models — linear models with few terms, shallow
decision trees, generalized additive models, rule lists — expose their reasoning directly
and are the right choice when a decision must be explained to a regulator or contested by
the person it affects. Post-hoc explanation methods applied to black-box models — LIME,
SHAP, partial dependence plots, counterfactuals — approximate the model's behaviour and
can be misleading: LIME's local surrogates are unstable across runs, partial dependence
plots assume feature independence and produce nonsense when features are correlated, and
SHAP values answer a specific attribution question that may not be the one being asked.
An accurate model with a plausible-sounding wrong explanation is worse than a slightly
less accurate model that is genuinely transparent.
