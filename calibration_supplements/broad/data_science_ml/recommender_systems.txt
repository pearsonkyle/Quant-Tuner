## Problem Framing

A recommender system predicts what a user will engage with from a large catalogue, and the
framing decision that governs everything downstream is what "engage" means. Predicting explicit
ratings, predicting a click, predicting a purchase, predicting long-term satisfaction, and
maximizing catalogue diversity are different objectives that produce different systems, and the
gap between the optimizable proxy and the actual goal is the field's central difficulty.

The data is almost always implicit rather than explicit. Explicit ratings are sparse, biased
toward the extremes since people rate what they loved or hated, and available for a tiny
fraction of interactions. Implicit signals — views, clicks, dwell time, purchases, skips — are
abundant and ambiguous, because the absence of an interaction may mean dislike or may mean the
item was never seen. That asymmetry is fundamental: implicit feedback gives positive signals
with confidence and negative signals only by inference.

Sparsity and scale set the engineering constraints. A catalogue of millions of items and a
user base of millions produces an interaction matrix that is typically over 99.9 percent empty,
so any method requiring the dense matrix is infeasible and the algorithms must exploit
sparsity. Serving requires selecting from millions of candidates within tens of milliseconds,
which is why production systems are staged rather than scoring everything.

Framing considerations:
  explicit feedback     ratings; sparse, biased toward extremes, low coverage
  implicit feedback     clicks, views, purchases; abundant and ambiguous
  missing not at random  unobserved does not mean disliked; it usually means
                        unseen, and the exposure was determined by the previous
                        model
  position bias         items shown higher are clicked more regardless of quality
  popularity bias       popular items accumulate more interactions, which makes
                        them more recommended, which makes them more popular
  cold start            new users and new items have no interaction history
  the long tail         most items have few interactions; most value may be there
  objective mismatch    clicks are measurable and are a proxy for satisfaction;
                        optimizing the proxy diverges from the goal
  short vs long term    engagement now versus retention over months
  scale                 millions of items; candidate generation must precede
                        ranking

Position bias deserves early emphasis because it contaminates every metric computed from
logged data. Items presented at the top of a list receive far more clicks than identical items
presented lower, so click-through rate confounds relevance with position. Training on that data
without correction teaches the model to reproduce the previous model's ordering rather than to
predict relevance. The corrections are inverse propensity weighting using an estimated
examination probability, randomized exploration that produces unbiased data at some cost, and
models that estimate position and relevance jointly.

Q: A recommender is trained on click logs and its offline metrics improve substantially, but
online engagement is flat. The most likely explanation is:
A. The model is undertrained
B. The offline data reflects the previous system's exposure and position bias, so the model
   learned to reproduce it rather than to improve relevance
C. The catalogue changed
D. The metric is computed incorrectly
Reasoning: Logged interactions occur only for items the previous system showed, in the
positions it showed them. A model trained to predict those clicks is rewarded for agreeing with
the previous system, and offline metrics computed on the same logs reward the same agreement.
Improvement on that measure need not correspond to improvement in relevance. Answer: B.

## Collaborative Filtering and Matrix Factorization

Collaborative filtering recommends based on interaction patterns without using item content,
on the principle that users who agreed in the past will agree in the future. User-based
filtering finds similar users and recommends what they liked; item-based filtering finds items
similar in their interaction patterns and recommends items similar to what a user liked.
Item-based is usually preferred in production because item-item similarities are more stable
than user-user ones and can be precomputed, since users' preferences change faster than items'
audiences do.

Matrix factorization learns low-dimensional latent representations of users and items whose
inner product approximates the interaction. The interpretation is that the latent dimensions
capture unnamed factors — genre, tone, complexity, price sensitivity — discovered from the data
rather than specified. It handles sparsity far better than neighbourhood methods because it
generalizes across the observed entries rather than requiring direct overlap.

Training on implicit feedback requires treating the missing entries carefully, because
treating them as zeros trains the model to predict that everything unobserved is disliked, and
ignoring them entirely gives a degenerate solution predicting high scores everywhere. Weighted
approaches treat all missing entries as weak negatives with low confidence and observed entries
as positives with confidence increasing in the interaction strength. Ranking approaches
sidestep the question by optimizing the relative order of an observed item against a sampled
unobserved one, which matches the ranking objective directly.

Collaborative methods:
  user-based CF        find similar users; unstable as preferences shift
  item-based CF        find similar items; stable, precomputable, the practical
                       neighbourhood choice
  similarity measures  cosine, Pearson, Jaccard; cosine on centred data is the
                       usual default
  matrix factorization  latent factors for users and items; the inner product
                       predicts the interaction
  ALS                  alternating least squares; convex in each factor
                       separately, parallelizes well
  SGD                  stochastic optimization; flexible objectives
  implicit ALS         confidence-weighted; treats unobserved as weak negatives
  BPR                  Bayesian personalized ranking; optimizes the order of an
                       observed item over a sampled unobserved one
  biases               user and item bias terms capture systematic generosity and
                       popularity; they account for a large share of the
                       predictable variance and cost almost nothing
  regularization       essential; the factorization overfits badly without it
  factor count         typically 32-256; more captures finer structure and
                       overfits sooner

The bias terms deserve mention because they are easy to omit and account for a surprising
fraction of predictive power. Some users rate everything highly and others harshly; some items
are broadly liked and others broadly disliked. Modelling these additively before the
interaction term means the latent factors capture genuine preference structure rather than
spending capacity on systematic offsets, and a model with biases and few factors frequently
beats one with many factors and none.

## Content, Hybrid, and Deep Approaches

Content-based filtering represents items by their attributes — text, category, tags, images,
audio features — and recommends items similar to what a user engaged with. Its advantage is
that a new item can be recommended immediately from its attributes, solving item cold start
entirely. Its limitation is that it recommends more of the same, since similarity in attribute
space rarely surfaces anything unexpected, and it cannot capture quality or appeal that the
attributes do not encode.

Hybrid systems combine approaches, and essentially every production system is one. The
combination may be a weighted blend of scores, a switch selecting a method by situation, a
cascade where one method filters and another ranks, or a unified model taking both interaction
and content features as input. The last is now the common architecture, since a model that
consumes user history, item content, and context in one function subsumes the others.

Deep learning contributes several capabilities that earlier methods lacked. Embeddings learned
end-to-end capture item and user representations from raw features including text and images.
Sequential models capture the order and recency of interactions, which matters because
preference is not a static vector — what someone wants next depends on what they just did.
Attention lets the model weight past interactions by relevance to the current context rather
than treating history as an unordered set.

Approaches beyond collaborative filtering:
  content-based        item attributes; solves item cold start, limited discovery
  demographic          user attributes; weak, and raises fairness concerns
  knowledge-based      explicit constraints and requirements; suits infrequent
                       high-consideration purchases
  hybrid               weighted, switching, cascade, or unified; universal in
                       production
  two-tower            separate user and item encoders producing embeddings scored
                       by dot product; enables approximate nearest neighbour
                       retrieval at scale
  wide and deep        memorization of feature crosses alongside generalization
  sequential models    order and recency; RNN, then self-attention
  session-based        no persistent user identity; the only signal is the current
                       session
  graph neural nets    propagate over the user-item interaction graph; captures
                       higher-order connectivity
  multi-task           predict several objectives jointly; shares representation
                       and lets the objectives be weighted explicitly
  side information     content, context, time, device; helps most where
                       interaction data is sparsest

The two-tower architecture deserves specific attention because it is what makes deep
retrieval feasible at scale. Encoding users and items separately into a shared embedding space,
with the score being a dot product, means item embeddings can be precomputed and indexed, and
retrieval becomes an approximate nearest neighbour search over millions of vectors in
milliseconds. A model that jointly encodes the user-item pair is more expressive and requires
scoring every candidate, which is impossible at retrieval scale — so the architecture choice is
dictated by the serving constraint rather than by modelling preference.

## Candidate Generation, Ranking, and Serving

Production recommenders are staged because scoring millions of items with an expensive model
per request is impossible. Candidate generation retrieves a few hundred to a few thousand
plausible items cheaply from the full catalogue. Ranking scores those candidates with a much
more expensive model. Re-ranking adjusts the final ordering for diversity, freshness, business
rules, and constraints that are awkward to express in a pointwise score.

The stages have genuinely different objectives, which is frequently misunderstood. Candidate
generation optimizes recall — it must not miss anything good, and precision matters little
since ranking will sort it out. Ranking optimizes precision at the top of the list, since only
the first few items are seen. Using the same model or the same metric for both leads to a
retrieval stage tuned for the wrong thing.

Multiple candidate sources are standard, each contributing a slice: a collaborative source for
personalized items, a content source for similar items, a trending source for freshness, a
sequential source for session continuity, and an exploration source for items the system knows
little about. Blending several sources gives coverage that a single retrieval method cannot,
and it makes the system robust to any one source degrading.

Serving architecture:
  candidate generation  cheap retrieval; optimize recall
  multiple sources      collaborative, content, trending, sequential, exploration
  ANN index             approximate nearest neighbour over item embeddings
  ranking               expensive model on hundreds of candidates; optimize
                        precision at the top
  feature availability  ranking features must be computable within the latency
                        budget; precompute what can be precomputed
  re-ranking            diversity, freshness, business rules, constraints
  diversity             maximal marginal relevance or determinantal point
                        processes; a list of ten near-identical items is worse
                        than a varied one even if each scores higher
  freshness             new items need exposure to accumulate signal
  business rules        promotions, contractual obligations, editorial
                        constraints; better applied explicitly here than smuggled
                        into the model
  latency budget        typically tens of milliseconds end to end
  caching               precomputed recommendations for the common case, with
                        real-time adjustment for session context
  fallback              a non-personalized ranking when the model or features are
                        unavailable

Diversity deserves explicit treatment because a purely score-maximizing list is usually worse
than a slightly lower-scoring diverse one. If the top ten items by predicted relevance are ten
near-identical items, the list satisfies a narrow interpretation of the user's preference and
fails if that interpretation was wrong, which the system cannot know. A diverse list hedges
across interpretations and covers more of the user's possible intents, which is why maximal
marginal relevance — selecting each item to balance its score against its similarity to already
selected items — reliably improves online metrics despite lowering the sum of predicted scores.

[assistant] The offline metric is measured on logged impressions from the old system, which
means it rewards agreeing with the old system rather than being better than it. That gap is the
most common failure in this field and it has a specific diagnosis path.

Your evaluation data contains only items the previous model chose to show, in the positions it
showed them, with no propensity correction. A new model that ranks those same items in a more
similar order to the observed clicks scores higher on NDCG without necessarily being better,
because the clicks themselves were shaped by position. Meanwhile the new model may be
retrieving and promoting different items entirely in production, where the offline data says
nothing about them.

Run one diagnostic before anything else: compare the distribution of what the two models
actually recommend. Specifically, the popularity distribution of recommended items, the number
of distinct items appearing across all users, and the intra-list similarity. Two patterns
account for most cases like this. If the new model concentrates more heavily on popular items,
it will score well offline — popular items dominate the logs — and reduce online engagement
because users see less that is personalized to them. If intra-list similarity rose, the lists
became less diverse, which lowers click-through even when each item scores higher.

Three fixes, in order of how much circularity each one removes.

Add propensity correction to the offline evaluation. Weight each logged interaction by the
inverse of the probability that item was shown at that position, so the metric estimates
performance under uniform exposure rather than under the old policy's exposure. This alone
usually collapses spurious offline gains.

Reserve a small fraction of traffic for randomized exploration. A few percent of impressions
with randomized ordering produces unbiased data, which gives you an offline evaluation set that
actually predicts online performance. The cost is small and it is the only way to break the
circularity permanently.

Treat offline metrics as a filter rather than a decision. Their job is to reject clearly worse
candidates cheaply; the decision belongs to an online test. A model that improves offline is
worth testing, and only the test settles it.

## Evaluation, Feedback Loops, and Ethics

Offline evaluation splits logged data and measures ranking quality, and its central weakness
is that it measures agreement with historical exposure rather than performance under the new
policy. Metrics divide into rating prediction accuracy, which is largely obsolete since the
task is ranking rather than rating, and ranking metrics measuring the quality of an ordered
list. Beyond accuracy, coverage measures what fraction of the catalogue is ever recommended,
and diversity, novelty, and serendipity measure properties that accuracy metrics actively
penalize.

Online evaluation through controlled experiments is the ground truth, with the standard
cautions of experimentation applying with particular force. Novelty effects are strong in
recommendation, since a changed interface or a changed selection draws attention initially.
Network and marketplace interference is common, since recommending one item to more users takes
exposure from others. And short-term engagement metrics diverge from long-term satisfaction
more sharply here than in most domains, which is why holdback groups maintained for months are
standard practice at the larger platforms.

Metrics and evaluation:
  precision@k          fraction of the top k that are relevant
  recall@k             fraction of relevant items in the top k
  MAP                  mean average precision across users
  NDCG                 discounted cumulative gain; weights position, the standard
                       ranking metric
  MRR                  mean reciprocal rank of the first relevant item
  hit rate             whether any relevant item appears in the top k
  coverage             fraction of the catalogue ever recommended
  novelty              inverse popularity of recommendations
  serendipity          relevant and unexpected; hard to measure, valuable
  intra-list diversity  dissimilarity within a recommendation list
  temporal split       train on past, evaluate on future; random splits leak
  counterfactual eval  inverse propensity or doubly robust estimators
  online A/B           the ground truth; watch for novelty and interference
  long-term holdback   a group excluded from a change for months, to measure
                       retention effects that short experiments cannot

The feedback loop is the structural problem that distinguishes recommendation from other
prediction tasks. The system's recommendations determine what users see, which determines what
they interact with, which becomes the training data for the next model. A model that
under-recommends a category will see fewer interactions with it, will conclude it is less
relevant, and will recommend it less — a self-reinforcing narrowing that no amount of model
improvement corrects, because the model is learning correctly from data the model itself
generated. Breaking it requires deliberate exploration, which costs measurable short-term
engagement for a benefit that is counterfactual and therefore hard to defend.

Filter bubbles and their consequences follow from the same mechanism at the level of the
individual user. A system optimizing engagement narrows toward what reliably engages, which for
many users means progressively more of the same, and in content domains it can mean
progressively more extreme material because extremity engages. Whether observed narrowing is
caused by the algorithm or by user choice is empirically contested and the mechanism by which an
engagement-optimizing system would produce it is not in doubt. The mitigations are diversity
objectives with explicit weight, exposure to varied content as a constraint rather than as an
outcome, and multi-objective optimization that includes something other than engagement — each
of which costs the metric the system is measured on, which is why they require organizational
commitment rather than technical work alone.

Fairness in recommendation has two sides that can conflict. User-side fairness asks whether
different groups receive comparable recommendation quality, and disparities arise naturally
because groups with less interaction data get worse personalization. Provider-side fairness
asks whether items and creators receive comparable exposure, and popularity bias systematically
disadvantages the long tail regardless of quality. Improving one can worsen the other, and both
trade against accuracy, so the resolution is a stated policy about what the system should
achieve rather than a technique — which is the same conclusion the field reaches on nearly every
question where the optimizable proxy diverges from the intended goal.
