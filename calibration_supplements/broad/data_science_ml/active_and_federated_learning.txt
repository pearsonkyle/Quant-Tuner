## Active Learning and the Economics of Labels

Supervised learning consumes labels, and labels are the expensive input in most applied machine
learning. Active learning attacks this directly by choosing which examples to label rather than
labelling at random, on the premise that examples differ enormously in how much they teach. The
premise is correct — a model that already classifies an example confidently and correctly learns
nothing from its label — and the practical gains are real when the setup is right.

Uncertainty sampling is the standard strategy and the simplest: label the examples the current model
is least confident about. Confidence can be measured by the top probability, by the margin between
the top two classes, or by entropy over the distribution, and the three differ in which examples they
prioritize when there are many classes. Its weakness is that it selects for ambiguity, and ambiguity
includes inherently unlabelable examples — blurred images, contradictory text, mislabelled data —
which a naive uncertainty sampler will happily consume the entire labelling budget on.

Diversity-based strategies correct this by selecting a batch that covers the input distribution
rather than clustering in one difficult region. In practice the strategies are combined, since batch
labelling is how annotation actually works and a batch of a hundred near-identical uncertain
examples wastes ninety-nine of them. The combined criterion — informative and mutually diverse — is
what production active learning systems implement.

Active learning:
  the premise          examples differ in how much they teach
  the economics        labels are the expensive input
  uncertainty sampling  label what the model is least sure about
  least confidence     the top probability is low
  margin sampling      the top two classes are close
  entropy              the whole distribution is flat; differs from margin when
                       there are many classes
  the ambiguity trap   unlabelable examples are maximally uncertain
  diversity sampling   cover the input distribution
  batch selection      the realistic setting; naive uncertainty produces
                       redundant batches
  the combined criterion  informative and mutually diverse
  query by committee   disagreement among an ensemble

Q: An active learning loop using pure uncertainty sampling plateaus after several rounds while
consuming its labelling budget. The most likely cause is:
A. The model has learned everything available
B. The strategy is selecting inherently ambiguous or corrupted examples, which are maximally
   uncertain and carry little learnable signal, and each batch is also internally redundant
C. Too many labels were collected
D. The learning rate is wrong
Reasoning: Uncertainty is high both for informative boundary cases and for noise. Without a
diversity term and some filtering of unlabelable data, the budget flows to the latter. Inspecting
the selected examples directly is the diagnostic. Answer: B.

## Weak, Semi-Supervised, and Self-Supervised Approaches

When labels are scarce the alternative to labelling more is extracting signal from unlabelled data
or from imprecise sources. Weak supervision generates noisy labels programmatically — from heuristic
rules, existing knowledge bases, regular expressions, or other models — and then models the noise to
combine them. The combination step matters: labelling functions have different accuracies and
correlate with each other, and a generative model over their outputs produces better probabilistic
labels than majority voting.

Semi-supervised learning uses unlabelled data alongside a small labelled set, and the methods that
work rest on a small number of assumptions. The smoothness assumption says nearby points share
labels; the cluster assumption says the decision boundary lies in low-density regions; the manifold
assumption says data lies on a lower-dimensional structure. Consistency regularization enforces
these by requiring the model to give the same prediction for augmented versions of an unlabelled
example, which is the mechanism behind most current methods.

Pseudo-labelling trains on the model's own confident predictions and is effective and dangerous in
equal measure. It amplifies whatever the model already believes, so an early systematic error
becomes entrenched, and the confidence threshold plus a mechanism to prevent class collapse are what
separate the working versions from the failing ones. Self-supervised pretraining sidesteps the issue
entirely by constructing a task from the data's own structure, which is how the strongest general
representations are now learned.

Data-efficient approaches:
  weak supervision     noisy programmatic labels from rules and existing sources
  labelling functions  imprecise, differently accurate, and correlated
  the combination      a generative model over their outputs beats majority vote
  semi-supervised      unlabelled data alongside a small labelled set
  smoothness assumption  nearby points share labels
  cluster assumption   the boundary lies in low-density regions
  manifold assumption  data occupies a lower-dimensional structure
  consistency regularization  the same prediction under augmentation
  the augmentation requirement  augmentations must preserve the label; the
                       method fails silently when they do not
  pseudo-labelling     training on confident self-predictions
  confirmation bias    early errors entrenched by self-training
  confidence threshold  the primary control
  class collapse       all pseudo-labels going to one class; needs an explicit
                       balancing term
  self-supervised pretraining  a task constructed from the data's structure
  transfer             a pretrained representation plus a small labelled set is
                       usually the strongest baseline and should be tried before
                       anything more elaborate

## Federated Learning

Federated learning trains a shared model across many devices or institutions without centralizing
the data. Each participant computes updates on its local data and sends only those updates to a
server that aggregates them into a new global model. The motivation is usually regulatory or
contractual — health records, financial data, and personal device data that cannot be pooled — and
sometimes bandwidth, since model updates are smaller than raw data.

The algorithmic core is straightforward and its difficulties are all systems difficulties. The
server distributes the current model; a subset of clients trains locally for several passes; the
server averages the resulting updates weighted by data volume. Running multiple local passes before
communicating is what makes the scheme practical, since communication is the bottleneck, and it is
also what creates the central problem: local models drift apart when their data distributions
differ, and averaging drifted models degrades the result.

Non-identical data across clients is the norm rather than an edge case. One hospital's patient
population differs from another's; one user's typing differs from another's. Under strong
heterogeneity, naive averaging converges slowly or to a poor solution, and the mitigations —
proximal terms limiting local drift, control variates correcting it, server-side momentum, or
personalizing a shared representation with local heads — are the substance of the federated
learning literature.

Federated learning:
  the motivation       data that cannot be centralized
  the loop             distribute, train locally, aggregate
  weighted averaging   by local data volume
  local passes         more passes means less communication and more drift
  communication cost   the bottleneck; updates compressed, quantized, or sparsified
  client heterogeneity  non-identical distributions; the normal case
  the drift problem    local models diverging, averaging degrading
  proximal terms       penalize distance from the global model
  control variates     correct the drift direction explicitly
  personalization      a shared representation with local heads
  systems heterogeneity  devices differ in speed, memory, and availability
  stragglers           slow clients; usually handled by deadline-based partial
                       participation
  participation bias   available devices are not a random sample of users, which
                       biases the model in ways that are hard to detect
  cross-device versus cross-silo  millions of unreliable devices versus a few
                       reliable institutions; different algorithms suit each
  evaluation           a global test set may not exist; per-client metrics and
                       their distribution are what matter, and a good average
                       hiding poor performance for a minority of clients is the
                       standard failure

## Privacy Mechanisms

Federated learning keeps raw data local and does not by itself provide privacy, because model
updates leak information about the data that produced them. Gradient inversion attacks reconstruct
training examples from updates with surprising fidelity under some conditions, and membership
inference determines whether a particular record was in the training set. Treating decentralization
as equivalent to privacy is a category error that appears frequently.

Differential privacy provides a formal guarantee: the output distribution changes only slightly
whether or not any single record is present, with the bound quantified by a privacy parameter. The
mechanism is calibrated noise addition, and in machine learning it is applied by clipping per-example
gradient contributions to a bounded norm and adding noise proportional to that bound. The guarantee
is meaningful and the cost is real — utility falls as the privacy parameter tightens, and the
tradeoff is more severe for smaller datasets and for underrepresented subgroups.

Secure aggregation addresses a different threat: it lets a server compute the sum of client updates
without seeing any individual one, using cryptographic protocols. Combined with differential
privacy, this permits the noise to be added once to the aggregate rather than by each client
separately, which improves the utility for a given guarantee substantially. The two mechanisms
protect against different adversaries and are complementary rather than alternatives.

Privacy mechanisms:
  decentralization is not privacy  updates leak information about the data
  gradient inversion   reconstructing examples from updates
  membership inference  determining whether a record was in training
  differential privacy  a formal bound on the influence of any single record
  the privacy parameter  smaller means stronger and costlier
  gradient clipping    bounding each example's contribution
  noise calibration    proportional to the clipping bound
  the utility cost     real, and worse for small data and rare subgroups
  the equity concern   privacy noise disproportionately harms underrepresented
                       groups, which is an accuracy-fairness interaction that is
                       easy to overlook
  secure aggregation   the server sees only the sum
  the combination      aggregate-level noise beats per-client noise for the same
                       guarantee
  the threat models    inversion by the server, inference by the model's users,
                       and collusion; state which one you are defending against
  accounting           privacy loss accumulates across training rounds and must
                       be tracked
  the honest position  an unquantified claim of privacy is not a guarantee, and
                       the value of the formal framework is that it forces the
                       claim to be stated in a way that can be checked

## Continual Learning and Practice

Continual learning trains on a stream of tasks or distributions without retaining all past data, and
its defining problem is catastrophic forgetting: training on new data degrades performance on old
tasks, sometimes severely and quickly. The cause is that gradient descent has no reason to preserve
parameters that matter for data it no longer sees.

The mitigations fall into three families. Replay stores a small sample of past data and mixes it
into current training, which is the most effective approach and the one that conflicts with the
privacy motivations that often prompt continual learning in the first place. Regularization
penalizes changes to parameters identified as important for past tasks, estimated from curvature or
from gradient statistics. Architectural approaches allocate separate capacity per task, which avoids
forgetting entirely and grows without bound.

In practice the deployed alternative to continual learning is periodic retraining from an
accumulating dataset, and it should be the default unless something prevents it. Retraining is
simpler, more predictable, easier to evaluate, and avoids the entire forgetting problem. Continual
learning earns its complexity where data cannot be retained, where the model must adapt faster than
a retraining cycle allows, or where per-user adaptation happens on a device.

  the stream setting   tasks or distributions arriving over time
  catastrophic forgetting  new training degrading old performance
  the cause            no gradient signal preserves unseen tasks
  replay               a stored sample of past data mixed in; most effective
  the tension          replay conflicts with the privacy motivation
  generative replay    synthesizing past data instead of storing it
  regularization       penalize changes to parameters important for past tasks
  importance estimation  from curvature or accumulated gradients
  architectural isolation  separate capacity per task; no forgetting, unbounded
                       growth
  task boundaries      known or unknown; the unknown case is much harder
  the stability-plasticity tradeoff  resisting change preserves old and prevents

  when continual is warranted  data cannot be retained, adaptation must be fast,
