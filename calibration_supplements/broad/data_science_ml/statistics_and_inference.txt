## Probability Foundations

A probability space is a triple (Omega, F, P): a sample space of outcomes, a
sigma-algebra of measurable events, and a measure assigning each event a number in
[0, 1] with P(Omega) = 1 and countable additivity over disjoint events. Most applied
work never touches the measure-theoretic scaffolding directly, but it is what makes
conditional expectation well defined for continuous variables, and it is why a
"probability zero" event is not the same as an impossible one — a continuous random
variable takes any particular value with probability zero, yet it takes some value
every time you sample it.

A random variable is a measurable function from the sample space to the reals. Its
distribution is fully described by the cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X <=
x), which is right-continuous and non-decreasing with limits 0 and 1 at minus and
plus infinity. Discrete variables have a probability mass function; continuous ones
have a density f(x) = dF/dx. The density is not a probability — it can exceed 1 — and
only its integral over an interval is one. Mixed distributions, common in practice
(a spend variable that is exactly zero for non-purchasers and continuous above), have
neither a pure pmf nor a pure density, and modelling them as if they did is a
frequent source of bad likelihoods.

Expectation is the Lebesgue integral E[X] = integral x dF(x), linear in its argument
regardless of dependence: E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y] always, even for correlated X
and Y. Variance is not linear; Var(aX + b) = a^2 Var(X), and Var(X + Y) = Var(X) +
Var(Y) + 2Cov(X, Y), with the covariance term vanishing only under uncorrelatedness.
Independence implies zero correlation, but the converse fails: if X is standard
normal and Y = X^2, then Cov(X, Y) = E[X^3] = 0 while Y is a deterministic function
of X. Correlation measures linear association only, and forgetting that is how
analysts declare "no relationship" in the presence of a perfect parabola.

The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem are the two pillars that make
statistics work. The weak LLN says the sample mean converges in probability to the
population mean; the strong LLN upgrades this to almost-sure convergence. The CLT
says that for iid variables with finite variance sigma^2, sqrt(n)(Xbar - mu)
converges in distribution to Normal(0, sigma^2), regardless of the shape of the
underlying distribution. The practical consequences are enormous: it is why standard
errors shrink as 1/sqrt(n), why doubling precision costs four times the data, and why
normal-theory confidence intervals work for means of decidedly non-normal data at
moderate sample sizes. It is also routinely over-applied — the CLT says nothing
useful about the sample mean of a Cauchy distribution (infinite variance, the sample
mean has the same distribution as a single observation no matter how much data you
collect), and its convergence is slow in the tails, which is exactly where risk
calculations live.

Conditional probability is defined as P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B) for P(B) > 0, and
Bayes' theorem simply rearranges it: P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B). The theorem is
arithmetically trivial and conceptually the most misused result in the field, because
P(A | B) and P(B | A) feel interchangeable and are not. The probability that a test
is positive given disease is a property of the test; the probability of disease given
a positive test depends on how common the disease is. For a test with 99%
sensitivity and 99% specificity applied to a disease with 0.1% prevalence, a positive
result carries only about a 9% chance of disease, because the false positives drawn
from the enormous healthy population swamp the true positives from the tiny sick one.

Key discrete distributions and what generates them:
  Bernoulli(p)          single binary trial; mean p, variance p(1-p)
  Binomial(n, p)        count of successes in n independent trials; mean np,
                        variance np(1-p)
  Poisson(lambda)       count of events in fixed time at constant rate; mean =
                        variance = lambda (equidispersion; real count data is
                        usually overdispersed, motivating negative binomial)
  Geometric(p)          trials until first success; memoryless
  NegBinomial(r, p)     trials until r-th success; Poisson-Gamma mixture, the
                        standard overdispersed count model
  Multinomial(n, p_vec) categorical counts across k cells

Key continuous distributions:
  Uniform(a, b)         maximum entropy on a bounded interval
  Normal(mu, sigma^2)   maximum entropy for fixed mean and variance; CLT limit
  Exponential(lambda)   waiting time at constant hazard; memoryless
  Gamma(k, theta)       sum of k exponentials; conjugate prior for Poisson rate
  Beta(a, b)            distribution on [0,1]; conjugate prior for a proportion
  ChiSquare(k)          sum of k squared standard normals; sampling distribution
                        of variance
  Student-t(nu)         normal with estimated variance; heavier tails, converging
                        to normal as nu grows
  Cauchy                t with nu = 1; undefined mean, infinite variance

Q: X and Y are random variables with Var(X) = 4, Var(Y) = 9, and Corr(X, Y) = 0.5.
What is Var(X - Y)?
A. 13   B. 7   C. 19   D. 5
Reasoning: Cov(X, Y) = Corr * sd(X) * sd(Y) = 0.5 * 2 * 3 = 3. Var(X - Y) = Var(X) +
Var(Y) - 2Cov(X, Y) = 4 + 9 - 6 = 7. The sign flip on the covariance term is what
separates this from Var(X + Y) = 19. Answer: B.

Q: A screening test has 95% sensitivity and 90% specificity. Disease prevalence is
2%. A patient tests positive. The probability they have the disease is closest to:
A. 95%   B. 62%   C. 16%   D. 2%
Reasoning: Out of 10,000 people, 200 have the disease and 9,800 do not. True
positives: 0.95 * 200 = 190. False positives: 0.10 * 9,800 = 980. Positive predictive
value = 190 / (190 + 980) = 190 / 1170 = 16.2%. The specificity looks high but is
applied to a base 49 times larger, so false positives dominate. Answer: C.

## Estimation

An estimator is a function of the data used to infer a population parameter. Its
quality is judged on bias (does its expectation equal the truth?), variance (how much
does it move across samples?), and consistency (does it converge to the truth as n
grows?). Mean squared error decomposes exactly as MSE = bias^2 + variance, and this
decomposition is the single most useful idea transferred from statistics into machine
learning, where it becomes the bias-variance tradeoff. An unbiased estimator is not
automatically preferable: the James-Stein estimator is biased and dominates the
unbiased sample mean in three or more dimensions, and ridge regression deliberately
accepts bias to buy a larger reduction in variance.

Maximum likelihood estimation picks the parameter value maximizing the probability of
the observed data. Under regularity conditions the MLE is consistent, asymptotically
normal, and asymptotically efficient — it attains the Cramer-Rao lower bound, so no
unbiased estimator has smaller asymptotic variance. Its asymptotic covariance is the
inverse Fisher information, which is why the inverse Hessian of the negative
log-likelihood at the optimum gives standard errors. The regularity conditions matter:
MLE misbehaves when the parameter is on a boundary (a variance component estimated at
exactly zero), when the support depends on the parameter (the MLE for the upper limit
of a uniform is the sample maximum, which is biased and not asymptotically normal),
and when the number of parameters grows with n.

The method of moments equates sample moments to population moments and solves. It is
usually less efficient than MLE but often has a closed form where MLE requires
numerical optimization, and it provides good starting values. Generalized method of
moments extends this to more moment conditions than parameters, weighting them
optimally, and is the workhorse of econometrics where a full likelihood is often
unavailable or untrustworthy.

A confidence interval is a procedure, not a statement about a particular interval. A
95% CI means that the procedure, applied repeatedly to fresh samples, produces
intervals containing the true parameter 95% of the time. Once computed, a specific
interval either contains the parameter or does not; there is no probability left. The
Bayesian credible interval is the object people usually intend — given the data and a
prior, there is a 95% posterior probability the parameter lies inside. The two often
coincide numerically with flat priors and large samples, which lets the distinction be
ignored right up until it matters.

Standard error formulas worth having memorized:
  mean, known sigma            SE = sigma / sqrt(n)
  mean, estimated sigma        SE = s / sqrt(n), use t with n-1 df
  proportion                   SE = sqrt(p(1-p)/n)
  difference of means          SE = sqrt(s1^2/n1 + s2^2/n2)  (Welch, unequal var)
  difference of proportions    SE = sqrt(p1(1-p1)/n1 + p2(1-p2)/n2)
  regression slope             SE = s_resid / (s_x * sqrt(n-1))
  correlation (Fisher z)       z = atanh(r), SE = 1/sqrt(n-3)

The bootstrap replaces analytic standard errors with resampling. Draw B samples of
size n with replacement from the observed data, recompute the statistic on each, and
use the spread of the resulting distribution as the sampling distribution. It works
for statistics with no tractable analytic error — medians, trimmed means, ratios,
the difference between two AUCs — and requires only that the statistic be a smooth
functional of the empirical distribution. It fails at the boundary: bootstrapping the
sample maximum does not work, because resampling can never produce a value above the
observed maximum. For dependent data the naive bootstrap destroys the dependence
structure and understates uncertainty; the block bootstrap resamples contiguous
blocks to preserve it.

plausible range on "19% better" runs roughly from 4% to 34%. Second, this test assumes
these were the only comparison you made and the sample size was fixed in advance. If
you have been peeking at the results daily, or this is one of several metrics you
checked, the nominal p-value understates your false-positive rate and you need a
sequential or multiplicity correction.

## Hypothesis Testing

A hypothesis test fixes a null hypothesis, computes a statistic whose distribution
under that null is known, and reports how extreme the observed value is. The p-value
is the probability, under the null, of a result at least as extreme as the one
observed. It is not the probability the null is true, not the probability the result
was due to chance, and not a measure of effect size. A p-value of 0.001 from a huge
sample can accompany a completely trivial effect, and a p-value of 0.2 from a small
one can hide a large effect the study lacked power to detect.

The two error types trade off. A Type I error rejects a true null (rate alpha, set by
convention at 0.05); a Type II error fails to reject a false null (rate beta). Power
is 1 - beta, the probability of detecting an effect that is really there. Power
increases with sample size, effect size, and alpha, and decreases with variance. The
standard rule of thumb for a two-sample comparison of means is that detecting an
effect of d standard deviations at 80% power and alpha 0.05 requires roughly 16/d^2
observations per group — so d = 0.5 needs about 64 per group, d = 0.2 needs about
400. Running an underpowered study is worse than running none, because it not only
usually misses the effect but, on the occasions it does reach significance,
necessarily overstates the magnitude (the winner's curse, or Type M error).

Common tests and when each applies:
  one-sample t          mean vs a fixed value, roughly normal or large n
  two-sample t (Welch)  two independent means, unequal variances (the default;
                        Student's pooled version needs equal variance and buys
                        very little)
  paired t              same units measured twice; tests mean difference, far more
                        powerful than the unpaired version when pairing is real
  Mann-Whitney U        two independent samples, ordinal or non-normal; tests
                        stochastic dominance, not means
  Wilcoxon signed-rank  paired nonparametric analogue
  chi-square            association in a contingency table, expected counts >= 5
  Fisher exact          same, valid for small counts
  ANOVA / F-test        3+ group means; significance says only "not all equal"
  Kruskal-Wallis        nonparametric ANOVA analogue
  Kolmogorov-Smirnov    whole-distribution comparison

Multiple comparisons destroy naive error control. Running 20 independent tests at
alpha = 0.05 gives roughly a 64% chance of at least one false positive (1 - 0.95^20).
Bonferroni divides alpha by the number of tests: simple, valid under any dependence,
and very conservative. Holm-Bonferroni is uniformly more powerful and equally valid.
Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate — the expected proportion of
rejections that are false — rather than the family-wise error rate, and is the right
choice for screening contexts like genomics or feature selection where you expect
many true effects and can tolerate some false ones.

Q: A study with 80% power at alpha = 0.05 fails to reject the null. The correct
interpretation is:
A. The null hypothesis is true
B. There is a 20% chance the null is true
C. The data are consistent with the null, but effects smaller than the study was
   powered to detect remain plausible
D. The study should be repeated until significance is reached
Reasoning: Failing to reject is not evidence of absence; it means the data did not
provide enough evidence against the null at the chosen threshold. The 80% power was
computed for a specific alternative effect size, so smaller effects were always
likely to be missed. Option D describes p-hacking, which invalidates error control.
The honest report pairs the non-significant test with a confidence interval showing
which effect sizes remain compatible with the data. Answer: C.

Q: You run 40 independent hypothesis tests at alpha = 0.05 and obtain 4 significant
results. Under Benjamini-Hochberg at FDR = 0.05, roughly how many of your rejections
would you expect to be false, if all 4 survive the procedure?
A. 4   B. 2   C. 0.2   D. 0
Reasoning: FDR control at 0.05 bounds the expected proportion of false discoveries
among rejections at 5%. With 4 rejections, the expected number of false ones is at
most 0.05 * 4 = 0.2. Note this is an expectation over repeated experiments, not a
guarantee about this particular set. Answer: C.

## Regression

Ordinary least squares fits y = X beta + epsilon by minimizing the sum of squared
residuals, giving the closed form beta_hat = (X'X)^{-1} X'y. It is the best linear
unbiased estimator under the Gauss-Markov conditions: linearity in parameters, random
sampling, no perfect collinearity, zero conditional mean of the errors, and
homoskedasticity. Normality of errors is not required for unbiasedness or for the
Gauss-Markov result — it is required only for exact small-sample t and F
distributions, and the CLT covers that asymptotically.

Each condition fails in a characteristic way. Heteroskedasticity leaves the
coefficients unbiased but breaks the standard errors, fixed by using
heteroskedasticity-consistent (White / HC3) errors, which cost nothing and should
arguably be the default. Correlated errors — time series, repeated measures, clustered
sampling — likewise leave coefficients unbiased but badly understate standard errors;
cluster-robust errors or an explicit mixed model are the remedies, and ignoring
clustering is probably the most common inference error in applied work. Endogeneity,
where a regressor correlates with the error because of omitted variables, simultaneity,
or measurement error, biases the coefficients themselves and no standard-error fix
touches it; instrumental variables, fixed effects, or a design change are required.

Multicollinearity inflates standard errors without biasing coefficients. The variance
inflation factor VIF_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2), where R_j^2 comes from regressing predictor j
on all the others, quantifies it; values above 5 or 10 are conventional warning
thresholds. The important nuance is that multicollinearity is not a problem for
prediction at all — the fitted values are fine — it only makes individual coefficients
unstable and hard to interpret. If the goal is forecasting, high VIF can be ignored;
if the goal is attributing effect to a specific variable, it is fatal.

Diagnostics and what they reveal:
  residuals vs fitted        nonlinearity (curvature), heteroskedasticity (funnel)
  Q-Q plot of residuals      tail behaviour, outliers
  scale-location             heteroskedasticity more clearly than resid-vs-fitted
  Cook's distance            influential points; > 4/n warrants a look
  leverage (hat values)      unusual predictor combinations; > 2p/n is high
  Durbin-Watson              first-order autocorrelation; ~2 is clean
  VIF                        collinearity
  partial residual plots     the functional form for one predictor, others held

Generalized linear models extend OLS by pairing a distribution from the exponential
family with a link function connecting the linear predictor to the mean. Logistic
regression uses a binomial family with a logit link, so coefficients are log odds
ratios: exponentiating gives the multiplicative change in odds per unit predictor.
Poisson regression uses a log link, so coefficients are log rate ratios; because real
count data is almost always overdispersed, the Poisson standard errors are typically
too small and quasi-Poisson or negative binomial is the safer default. GLMs are fit by
iteratively reweighted least squares maximizing the likelihood, and their residuals
(deviance, Pearson) need care — the raw response residuals are not interpretable the
way OLS residuals are.

Regularization adds a penalty to the loss. Ridge (L2) shrinks coefficients smoothly
toward zero, never reaching it, and handles collinearity gracefully by splitting the
effect among correlated predictors. Lasso (L1) drives coefficients exactly to zero,
performing selection, but among a group of correlated predictors it arbitrarily keeps
one and discards the rest, making the selected set unstable across resamples. Elastic
net mixes both and recovers grouped selection. All three require standardized
predictors, because the penalty is scale-dependent, and none of them produce valid
conventional standard errors — post-selection inference is a genuinely hard problem
and the naive p-values from a lasso-selected model are meaningless.

[assistant] Two different things, and it is worth keeping them apart.

The coefficient 0.83 is on the log-odds scale. Exponentiating gives an odds ratio of
2.29, with a 95% CI of [1.58, 3.33]. Plain language: treated users have roughly 2.3
times the odds of converting compared to untreated, and the data are consistent with
anything from about 1.6x to 3.3x. Note that odds ratios are not risk ratios — if the
baseline conversion rate is low, say 3%, an odds ratio of 2.3 corresponds to a risk
ratio near 2.2 and they are close; but at a 40% baseline the same odds ratio is only
about a 1.5x risk ratio. Always translate to absolute percentage points before
anyone makes a business decision on it.

The AUC of 0.71 is about ranking, not effect size. It says that given one converter
and one non-converter at random, the model scores the converter higher 71% of the
time. 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect. These two numbers are independent in a
useful sense: a single strong predictor can produce a large, highly significant odds
ratio while the model as a whole still ranks individuals only moderately well,
because conversion depends on much more than this one variable. A significant
treatment effect and a mediocre AUC together are exactly what you would expect from a
real but partial driver of behaviour.

## Bayesian Inference

Bayesian inference treats parameters as random variables with distributions
expressing uncertainty. Bayes' theorem updates a prior into a posterior:
posterior proportional to likelihood times prior. Everything follows from that single
mechanic. Where frequentist inference asks "how would this procedure behave over
repeated samples?", Bayesian inference asks "given the data I actually have, what
should I believe?" — which is the question most practitioners think they are asking
anyway.

Conjugate priors make the posterior analytically tractable by staying in the same
family as the prior. Beta-Binomial is the canonical example: with a Beta(a, b) prior
on a probability and s successes in n trials, the posterior is Beta(a + s, b + n - s).
The prior parameters read as pseudo-counts, which makes prior specification concrete —
Beta(1, 1) is uniform and worth one observation of each outcome, Beta(30, 70) encodes
a belief centred at 0.3 with the strength of 100 prior observations. Other conjugate
pairs: Gamma-Poisson for rates, Normal-Normal for a mean with known variance,
Normal-Inverse-Gamma for a mean and variance jointly, Dirichlet-Multinomial for
category probabilities.

For non-conjugate models the posterior has no closed form and is approximated.
Markov chain Monte Carlo constructs a chain whose stationary distribution is the
posterior; Metropolis-Hastings and Gibbs sampling are the classical algorithms, and
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn Sampler (Stan, PyMC, NumPyro) is the modern
default because it exploits gradients to move efficiently through high-dimensional
correlated posteriors. Convergence must be checked, not assumed: R-hat should be below
1.01, effective sample size should be in the hundreds at minimum, trace plots should
look like white noise rather than wandering, and divergent transitions in HMC signal
geometry the sampler cannot navigate — usually a funnel that calls for a
non-centred reparameterization. Variational inference replaces sampling with
optimization over a simpler family, trading exactness for speed; it typically
underestimates posterior variance because the reverse-KL objective is mode-seeking.

Hierarchical models are where the Bayesian machinery earns its keep. When you have
many related groups — stores, users, experiments, sensors — a hierarchical model
estimates each group's parameter while shrinking it toward the population mean, with
the amount of shrinkage determined by the data rather than by fiat. Groups with
little data shrink heavily toward the global estimate; groups with abundant data
barely move. This partial pooling sits between the two bad extremes of complete
pooling (one estimate for everyone, ignoring real heterogeneity) and no pooling
(separate estimates, wildly noisy for small groups) and it is the principled answer
to the ubiquitous problem of ranking entities with unequal sample sizes.

Model comparison in the Bayesian world uses different tools. The Bayes factor is the
ratio of marginal likelihoods and directly compares evidence for two models, but it is
notoriously sensitive to prior specification even where the posterior is not.
Information criteria approximate out-of-sample predictive accuracy: WAIC and PSIS-LOO
are the modern choices, both estimating leave-one-out predictive density with a
complexity penalty, and PSIS-LOO helpfully reports Pareto k diagnostics flagging
observations that dominate the estimate. Posterior predictive checks — simulating
datasets from the fitted model and comparing them to the real one on chosen summary
statistics — are the most practically informative check of all, because they reveal
what the model cannot reproduce.

Q: A Beta(2, 8) prior is updated with 15 successes in 30 trials. The posterior mean
is closest to:
A. 0.50   B. 0.425   C. 0.20   D. 0.475
Reasoning: The posterior is Beta(2 + 15, 8 + 15) = Beta(17, 23). The mean of a
Beta(a, b) is a/(a+b) = 17/40 = 0.425. It sits between the prior mean of 0.2 and the
data's 0.5, pulled toward the data because 30 observations outweigh the prior's 10
pseudo-counts. Answer: B.

Q: An HMC run reports R-hat = 1.34 and 180 divergent transitions. The correct next
step is:
A. Report the posterior means, since the chains did run
B. Increase the number of samples until R-hat falls
C. Diagnose the model geometry — likely reparameterize — before trusting anything
D. Switch to variational inference for speed
Reasoning: R-hat of 1.34 means the chains have not mixed and are exploring different
regions; the posterior summaries are meaningless. Divergences indicate the sampler is
failing in a region of high curvature, classically a hierarchical funnel, and simply
drawing more samples does not fix biased exploration. The standard remedy is a
non-centred parameterization, tighter priors, or both. Answer: C.

## Experimental Design and Causal Inference

Randomization is what licenses causal claims. By assigning treatment independently of
everything else, it makes the treated and control groups exchangeable in expectation
on every covariate — observed and unobserved alike — so the difference in outcomes
estimates the average treatment effect without a model of the confounding. No
observational adjustment achieves this, because adjustment can only handle the
confounders you measured and thought of.

The potential outcomes framework makes this precise. Each unit has outcomes Y(1) and
Y(0) under treatment and control; the individual effect is their difference, and the
fundamental problem of causal inference is that only one is ever observed. Randomized
assignment makes the observed group means unbiased for E[Y(1)] and E[Y(0)], so their
difference estimates the average treatment effect. The SUTVA assumption is doing
quiet work here: no interference between units, and only one version of the
treatment. Interference breaks A/B tests in marketplaces and social networks, where
treating one user changes the experience of untreated ones — a supply-side test that
gives some sellers better placement necessarily takes impressions from the control
sellers, so the naive comparison overstates the effect. Cluster randomization or
switchback designs are the usual mitigations.

Practical experiment design decisions:
  unit of randomization   user > session > request; randomize at the level where
                          interference stops and where the metric is measured
  sample size             fix in advance from a power calculation on the minimum
                          detectable effect that would change a decision
  stratification          block on strong pre-treatment predictors to cut variance
  CUPED                   regress out a pre-period covariate; typically 30-50%
                          variance reduction on engagement metrics for free
  guardrail metrics       monitor for harm even when not the primary hypothesis
  sequential testing      always-valid p-values or alpha spending if you will peek
  SRM check               sample ratio mismatch flags assignment bugs before any
                          effect is interpreted

Sample ratio mismatch deserves particular emphasis because it is the highest-yield
diagnostic in industrial experimentation. If a 50/50 split delivers 49.2% / 50.8% on
two million users, a chi-square test rejects overwhelmingly, and that means the
randomization or the logging is broken — bot filtering that correlates with treatment,
a redirect that drops slow clients, a bug where errors default to control. Any effect
estimate from an experiment with SRM is untrustworthy no matter how significant,
because the groups are no longer comparable.

When randomization is impossible, quasi-experimental designs exploit structure.
Difference-in-differences compares the change over time in a treated group to the
change in an untreated one, differencing out both time-invariant group differences
and common time shocks; it stands or falls on the parallel trends assumption, which
should be supported by pre-period data. Regression discontinuity exploits a threshold
rule — a scholarship awarded above a test-score cutoff — and compares units just above
and just below, giving a locally randomized comparison with strong internal validity
but limited external validity. Instrumental variables use a source of variation that
affects treatment but influences the outcome only through it; the exclusion
restriction is untestable and usually the weak point. Synthetic control constructs a
weighted combination of untreated units matching the treated unit's pre-period
trajectory, and is the standard tool for a single treated unit like a state or market.

Confounding, colliders, and mediators all bias estimates, but only confounders are
fixed by adjustment. A confounder causes both treatment and outcome and must be
controlled. A collider is caused by both, and controlling for it induces spurious
association between them — this is why conditioning on hospitalization creates
correlations between unrelated diseases (Berkson's paradox), and why "controlling for
everything available" is not a conservative strategy but an active source of bias. A
mediator lies on the causal path; controlling for it removes the very effect you
wanted to measure. Directed acyclic graphs turn these distinctions into a mechanical
procedure: draw the assumed causal structure, and the back-door criterion tells you
exactly which set of variables to adjust for.

[user] Our A/B test shows a 3% lift in revenue per user but the control group has
51.4% of the traffic out of 1.8M users. Should I ship it?
[assistant] tool_call stats.srm_check({"observed": [925200, 874800], "expected_ratio":
[0.5, 0.5]})
[tool] {"chi_square": 1428.0, "p_value": 4.2e-313, "observed_split": [0.514, 0.486],
"verdict": "sample ratio mismatch"}
[assistant] No — do not ship, and do not interpret the 3% lift at all yet.

You have a sample ratio mismatch. A 51.4/48.6 split on 1.8M users under an intended
50/50 assignment has a p-value on the order of 1e-313. That is not sampling noise; a
50/50 randomizer on this sample size should stay within roughly plus or minus 0.15
percentage points. Something is systematically excluding users from one arm.

The practical implication is that your two groups are no longer exchangeable, so the
3% revenue difference could be entirely composition rather than treatment. The usual
culprits are worth checking in this order: bot or fraud filtering applied
post-assignment and correlated with the treatment, client-side assignment where the
treatment variant loads more slowly and drops users before the exposure event fires,
redirect-based implementations that lose a slice of traffic, and logging joins that
silently drop rows.

Fix the assignment mechanism, verify a clean split on an A/A test, then rerun. If
there is schedule pressure, the one salvageable analysis is to check whether the SRM
is confined to an identifiable segment and whether the effect holds within strata
that show a clean split — but treat that as a hypothesis to re-test, not a result.

## Resampling and Model Validation

Cross-validation estimates out-of-sample performance by partitioning the data,
training on all but one fold and evaluating on the held-out fold, then rotating.
K-fold with k = 5 or 10 is standard; leave-one-out is nearly unbiased but has high
variance and is expensive, and for most models the k = 10 compromise dominates. The
estimate is of the performance of the *procedure*, not of any particular fitted
model, and the folds' scores are not independent, so their standard deviation
understates true uncertainty.

The dominant failure mode in applied cross-validation is leakage: any information
flowing from the validation fold into training. Standardizing features on the full
dataset before splitting leaks the validation set's mean and variance. Imputing
missing values globally leaks. Selecting features by their correlation with the target
on all the data, then cross-validating only the model fit, produces spectacularly
optimistic estimates — this is the classic error that yields 95% accuracy on pure
noise. The rule is that every data-dependent step, including preprocessing and
feature selection, must sit inside the cross-validation loop, which is precisely what
scikit-learn pipelines exist to enforce.

Structured data needs structured splitting. Time series require forward-chaining
validation: train on the past, test on the future, never the reverse, because random
folds let the model see the future and any temporal autocorrelation converts to
inflated scores. Grouped data — multiple rows per patient, per user, per device —
require group-aware splits keeping all of an entity's rows on one side, or the model
memorizes the entity rather than the signal. Rare-outcome data benefits from
stratified folds preserving class balance, without which small folds can end up with
zero positives and undefined metrics.

Choosing a metric matters more than choosing a model in imbalanced settings. With 1%
positives, a model predicting "negative" always achieves 99% accuracy and is useless.
Precision-recall curves and average precision are far more informative than ROC-AUC
when the positive class is rare, because ROC's false-positive-rate axis is normalized
by a huge negative population and stays flattering. For probabilistic forecasts, use
a proper scoring rule — log loss or Brier score — since these are minimized only by
the true probabilities, and follow up with a calibration plot, since a model can rank
perfectly (AUC 1.0) while its probabilities are systematically wrong by a factor of
three.

Metric selection quick reference:
  balanced classification, ranking matters      ROC-AUC
  rare positives, ranking matters               PR-AUC / average precision
  probabilities used downstream                 log loss + calibration curve
  fixed decision threshold, costs asymmetric    expected cost, or F-beta
  regression, outliers meaningful               RMSE
  regression, outliers are noise                MAE, or Huber
  regression, relative error matters            MAPE (breaks near zero), or
                                                RMSLE
  forecasting vs a naive baseline               MASE

Q: A model is trained with feature selection performed on the full dataset before a
10-fold cross-validation of the model fit alone. The resulting CV accuracy is:
A. An unbiased estimate of test performance
B. Optimistically biased, potentially severely
C. Pessimistically biased
D. Unbiased if the number of features is small
Reasoning: The selection step saw every fold's labels, so each validation fold
influenced which features exist. The subsequent CV therefore evaluates on data already
used, and the bias can be extreme — the canonical demonstration selects features from
pure noise and obtains high apparent accuracy. Only nesting the selection inside each
training fold gives an honest estimate. Answer: B.

Q: For a fraud model where positives are 0.2% of transactions, the least informative
headline metric is:
A. Precision at the operating threshold   B. Recall at the operating threshold
C. Overall accuracy   D. Average precision
Reasoning: Predicting "not fraud" for everything yields 99.8% accuracy while catching
zero fraud, so accuracy carries almost no information at this base rate. The other
three all speak directly to performance on the rare class. Answer: C.
