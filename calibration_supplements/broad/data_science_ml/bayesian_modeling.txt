## The Bayesian Framework

Bayesian modeling treats unknown quantities as random variables with probability
distributions expressing uncertainty, and updates those distributions as data arrives. The
mechanic is Bayes' theorem: the posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior.
Everything else in the subject is the consequence of that single relation applied carefully.

The framework's practical advantage is that it answers the question people actually ask. A
frequentist confidence interval is a statement about a procedure's long-run behaviour — that
intervals constructed this way contain the true value 95% of the time — and it is not a
statement about the particular interval in hand. A Bayesian credible interval states that given
the data and the prior, there is a 95% probability the parameter lies in the interval. The
second is what practitioners almost always intend, and the routine misinterpretation of the
first as the second is evidence for how natural the Bayesian reading is.

The framework also handles several situations that are awkward otherwise. Uncertainty
propagates automatically through any derived quantity, since the posterior over parameters
induces a posterior over any function of them. Prior information from previous studies or from
domain knowledge enters explicitly rather than informally. Small samples are handled without
asymptotic approximations. And nuisance parameters are eliminated by integration rather than by
plug-in estimates that understate uncertainty.

Framework concepts:
  prior                 belief before the data; a modelling choice requiring
                        justification
  likelihood            the probability of the data given the parameters; the same
                        object frequentist inference uses
  posterior             belief after the data; proportional to likelihood times
                        prior
  marginal likelihood   the normalizing constant; the probability of the data
                        under the model, and the basis of Bayes factors
  credible interval     a probability statement about the parameter given the data
  confidence interval   a statement about the procedure's long-run coverage
  posterior predictive  the distribution of new data, integrating over parameter
                        uncertainty; what forecasting should use
  marginalization       integrate out nuisance parameters rather than plugging in
                        estimates
  exchangeability       the assumption justifying a shared prior across units;
                        weaker than independence and the actual basis of
                        hierarchical modelling
  updating              yesterday's posterior is today's prior; sequential
                        analysis is natural rather than a special case

The posterior predictive distribution deserves emphasis because it is where the framework's
advantage is most concrete. A prediction from a fitted model that plugs in point estimates
understates uncertainty, because it ignores that the parameters themselves are uncertain. The
posterior predictive integrates over the parameter posterior, so the resulting intervals include
both the inherent variability of new observations and the uncertainty about the parameters. For
small samples the difference is substantial, and it is exactly the difference between intervals
that achieve their nominal coverage and intervals that are systematically too narrow.

Q: A model is fit and a 95% credible interval for a parameter is [2.1, 4.8]. The correct
interpretation is:
A. Repeated sampling would produce intervals containing the true value 95% of the time
B. Given the data and the prior, there is a 95% probability the parameter lies between 2.1 and
   4.8
C. 95% of observations fall in this range
D. The parameter is definitely in this range
Reasoning: A credible interval is a direct probability statement about the parameter
conditional on the observed data and the assumed prior. Option A describes a confidence
interval's frequentist coverage property. Option C describes a prediction interval, which
concerns observations rather than parameters. Answer: B.

## Priors

The prior is the framework's most discussed feature and its most misunderstood. It is a
modelling choice requiring justification in the same way a likelihood does, and the objection
that it introduces subjectivity applies with equal force to the choice of likelihood, of
variables, of transformations, and of which analyses to report — all of which are made in every
analysis regardless of framework and most of which are less visible.

Priors range from strongly informative, encoding substantial knowledge, to weakly informative,
constraining the parameter to a plausible range without asserting much within it, to flat, which
is frequently a poor choice despite its appearance of neutrality. A flat prior on an unbounded
parameter is improper — it does not integrate to a finite value — which may still yield a proper
posterior and may not, and a flat prior on one parameterization is informative on another, so
its neutrality is illusory.

Weakly informative priors are the practical default and are worth stating as such. A prior that
rules out physically impossible or implausible values — a negative variance, a treatment effect
of a thousand standard deviations, a probability outside the unit interval — costs nothing when
the data is informative and regularizes usefully when it is not. It also improves computation
substantially, since a sampler exploring a region the prior excludes is wasting effort in a
region the analyst would reject anyway.

Prior specification:
  informative           encodes real knowledge; requires and repays justification
  weakly informative    constrains to plausible ranges; the practical default
  flat / uniform        appears neutral, is not; parameterization-dependent and
                        often improper
  improper prior        does not integrate to one; may still give a proper
                        posterior, and must be checked
  Jeffreys prior        invariant under reparameterization; the standard
                        objective choice for a single parameter
  reference priors      constructed to be minimally informative in a defined
                        sense
  conjugate prior       the posterior stays in the same family; analytically
                        convenient and rarely the right belief
  regularizing prior    equivalent to a penalty; ridge regression is a Gaussian
                        prior and lasso is a Laplace prior on the coefficients
  prior predictive check  simulate data from the prior and inspect it; the most
                        useful tool for judging whether a prior is reasonable
  sensitivity analysis  refit under alternative priors and report how much the
                        conclusion moves

The prior predictive check is the technique that makes prior specification tractable. Rather
than reasoning directly about whether a prior on a coefficient is reasonable, simulate parameter
values from the prior, generate datasets from the model, and look at them. If the simulated data
includes people two hundred metres tall, treatment effects larger than any ever observed, or
probabilities concentrated at zero and one, the prior is unreasonable regardless of how neutral
it appeared. This converts an abstract judgment into a concrete one.

The connection between priors and regularization is worth making explicit because it dissolves
an apparent disagreement. Ridge regression's L2 penalty is exactly the maximum a posteriori
estimate under a Gaussian prior on the coefficients, and the lasso's L1 penalty corresponds to a
Laplace prior. Practitioners who use regularization while objecting to priors are using priors
and not calling them that, and the Bayesian version has the advantage of producing a full
posterior rather than a point estimate, so the uncertainty is available.

## Computation

The posterior is analytically tractable only in special cases, so computation dominates
practice. Conjugate models — beta-binomial, gamma-Poisson, normal-normal — give closed forms and
are valuable for intuition and for components of larger models, and the requirement that the
prior be conjugate is a mathematical convenience rather than a statement about belief.

Markov chain Monte Carlo constructs a chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior,
then samples from it. Metropolis-Hastings proposes a move and accepts it with a probability that
enforces detailed balance with respect to the target, which requires the target only up to a
normalizing constant — and that is precisely why the method works, since the marginal likelihood
is the intractable part. Gibbs sampling updates each parameter from its full conditional, which
is efficient when those conditionals are tractable and mixes poorly when parameters are strongly
correlated.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo uses gradient information to propose distant moves with high acceptance,
which makes it dramatically more efficient than random-walk methods in high dimensions. The
No-U-Turn Sampler removes the need to tune the trajectory length, and together they are what made
Bayesian inference practical for models with hundreds or thousands of parameters. The requirement
is that the posterior be differentiable, which excludes discrete parameters — handled by
marginalizing them out where possible.

Computation and diagnostics:
  conjugate             closed form; useful for intuition and as components
  grid approximation    evaluate on a grid; only for one or two parameters
  Metropolis-Hastings   propose and accept; needs the target only up to a constant
  Gibbs sampling        update from full conditionals; poor mixing under
                        correlation
  Hamiltonian MC        gradient-informed proposals; efficient in high dimension
  NUTS                  removes trajectory tuning; the modern default
  variational inference  optimize an approximating family; fast, and systematically
                        underestimates posterior variance because the reverse-KL
                        objective is mode-seeking
  Laplace approximation  a Gaussian at the posterior mode; cheap and adequate when
                        the posterior is near-Gaussian
  R-hat                 compares within- and between-chain variance; should be
                        below 1.01
  effective sample size  accounts for autocorrelation; hundreds at minimum, and
                        report it for the quantities you care about rather than
                        only for the parameters
  trace plots           should look like white noise; wandering indicates poor
                        mixing
  divergent transitions  HMC failing in a high-curvature region; a warning that
                        the sampler is not exploring correctly and the results
                        cannot be trusted
  non-centred parameterization  the standard fix for divergences in hierarchical
                        models

Divergent transitions deserve specific emphasis because they are the diagnostic most likely to
be ignored and most likely to indicate real bias. They occur when the sampler's numerical
integration fails in a region of high curvature, typically the narrow neck of a funnel-shaped
posterior that arises in hierarchical models when a group-level variance approaches zero. The
sampler cannot explore that region, so the posterior is systematically misrepresented — and the
chains may otherwise look healthy, with acceptable R-hat and reasonable trace plots. The remedy
is reparameterization, and treating divergences as a tuning nuisance to be suppressed by
increasing the target acceptance rate frequently hides the problem rather than fixing it.

## Hierarchical Models

Hierarchical modelling is where the framework's practical advantage is largest, and the problem
it solves is ubiquitous: estimating a parameter for each of many related groups, some of which
have abundant data and some almost none. The two obvious approaches both fail. Complete pooling
estimates one parameter for all groups and ignores real heterogeneity. No pooling estimates each
group independently and produces wildly noisy estimates for small groups.

Partial pooling estimates each group's parameter while shrinking it toward the population mean,
with the degree of shrinkage determined by the data rather than chosen. A group with abundant
data barely moves; a group with little data shrinks substantially toward the population. This is
the correct answer rather than a compromise between two wrong ones, and it follows directly from
modelling the group parameters as drawn from a common distribution whose parameters are also
estimated.

The consequences are visible in any ranking problem. Ranking hospitals by mortality rate,
schools by test scores, players by performance, or products by rating produces top and bottom
positions dominated by small samples under no pooling, because extreme observed values arise
most easily where the sample is smallest. Partial pooling corrects this automatically, and the
resulting rankings are both more accurate and more stable.

Hierarchical modelling:
  complete pooling      one parameter for all; ignores heterogeneity
  no pooling            independent per group; noisy for small groups
  partial pooling       shrinkage toward the population, data-determined
  hyperparameters       the population distribution's parameters, also estimated
  shrinkage factor      depends on the group's sample size and on the ratio of
                        within- to between-group variance
  borrowing strength    small groups gain precision from the population
  varying intercepts    group-specific baselines
  varying slopes        group-specific effects; requires modelling the correlation
                        between intercepts and slopes
  centred parameterization  natural, and samples poorly when the group-level
                        variance is small
  non-centred           reparameterize so the group effects are standardized
                        deviations; fixes the funnel geometry
  multilevel regression  the general framework; the same model appears as mixed
                        effects in the frequentist literature
  post-stratification   combine multilevel estimates with population weights; the
                        standard method for small-area estimation from
                        non-representative samples

[assistant] The 0% and 100% rates are almost certainly not real, and they are an artifact of
estimating each page independently. A page with 30 visits and zero conversions is entirely
consistent with a true rate of 3%, and a page with 30 visits and 30 conversions is consistent
with a true rate of 90%. Independent estimation reports the observed proportion as if it were
the parameter.

Use a hierarchical beta-binomial model. Each page has its own conversion rate, and those rates
are drawn from a common beta distribution whose parameters are estimated from all 400 pages
jointly. The result is that each page's estimate is a compromise between its own data and the
population, weighted by how much information its own data carries.

The shrinkage is automatic and appropriate. Your 30-visit pages will shrink roughly 82% toward
the population mean, so a page with zero conversions out of 30 will be estimated near the
population rate rather than at zero. Your 50,000-visit pages barely move at all, shrinking about
0.3%, because their own data is overwhelming. You do not choose these weights; they follow from
the estimated between-page variance relative to the within-page sampling variance.

The ranking consequence is the one that matters most for your stated goal. Ranking by raw
observed rate systematically selects pages with the smallest samples, because extreme values
arise most easily where n is small — so your top-ranked pages are mostly a list of pages you know
little about. Ranking by posterior mean corrects this, and ranking by the posterior probability
of exceeding a threshold is better still if the decision is which pages to keep.

Two extensions worth considering. If you have page attributes — template, traffic source,
product category — add them as predictors, which lets pages borrow strength from similar pages
rather than from the global mean and improves the small-sample estimates further. And report
the full posterior interval rather than the point estimate for each page, because a page
estimated at 4% with an interval from 1% to 12% supports a different decision than one estimated
at 4% with an interval from 3.8% to 4.2%.

## Checking, Comparison, and Decisions

Model checking asks whether the model can reproduce the data's relevant features, and posterior
predictive checking is the primary tool. Simulate replicated datasets from the fitted model,
compute a summary statistic on each and on the observed data, and compare. If the observed value
falls in the tail of the replicated distribution, the model fails to reproduce that feature —
which is informative regardless of whether any parameter appears significant.

The choice of summary statistic determines what is checked, and it should reflect what the
model is for. A model reproducing the mean and failing on the variance, on the number of zeros,
on the extremes, or on the autocorrelation may be adequate or inadequate depending on the use.
Checking several statistics addressing different aspects, including ones the model was not fit to
match, is what makes the check informative.

Model comparison in the Bayesian framework has several tools with different properties. Bayes
factors compare marginal likelihoods and are theoretically clean and notoriously sensitive to
prior specification, including to the priors on parameters that both models share — which means
a Bayes factor can be dominated by a prior choice that the posterior is insensitive to.
Information criteria estimate out-of-sample predictive accuracy, with leave-one-out
cross-validation approximated by importance sampling being the modern standard, and it reports
diagnostics indicating when the approximation is unreliable.

Checking, comparison, and decisions:
  posterior predictive check  simulate and compare; the primary diagnostic
  choice of statistic   check what the model is for, including features it was not
                        fit to match
  graphical checks      overlay replicated and observed distributions; more
                        informative than a single p-value
  Bayes factor          marginal likelihood ratio; prior-sensitive, including to
                        shared priors
  WAIC and PSIS-LOO     estimate out-of-sample predictive density; report the
                        diagnostics indicating unreliability
  model averaging       weight predictions by model plausibility rather than
                        selecting one
  stacking              optimize predictive weights directly; usually better than
                        weights derived from information criteria
  decision theory       choose the action minimizing expected loss under the
                        posterior; the natural completion of the framework
  loss function         encodes the asymmetry of consequences; a symmetric loss is
                        an assumption rather than a default
  value of information  the expected improvement in decision quality from more
                        data; determines whether to collect it
  reporting             the posterior distribution, not only a point and an
                        interval; and the prior, so the analysis is reproducible

Decision theory is the framework's natural completion and is underused. Having a posterior
distribution over an unknown quantity and a loss function describing the consequences of each
action makes the optimal action a computation: choose the action minimizing expected loss under
the posterior. This handles asymmetric consequences naturally — where over-estimating costs more
than under-estimating, the optimal point estimate is not the posterior mean — and it makes
explicit that the summary statistic to report depends on what will be done with it. The posterior
mean minimizes squared error loss, the median minimizes absolute error, and a quantile minimizes
the asymmetric loss appropriate to an inventory decision, so reporting the mean by default embeds
an unstated assumption about consequences.

The practical workflow that has emerged in the field is worth stating as a sequence. Specify the
model and the priors; check the priors by simulating from them; fit to simulated data with known
parameters and confirm the parameters are recovered, which validates the implementation; fit to
the real data; check the computational diagnostics before looking at any result; perform
posterior predictive checks; compare against alternative models; and conduct sensitivity analysis
on the choices that were least well justified. Skipping the simulation-based validation is the
most commonly omitted step and the one most likely to leave an implementation error undetected,
because a model with a coding error frequently produces plausible-looking results on real data
where the truth is unknown.
