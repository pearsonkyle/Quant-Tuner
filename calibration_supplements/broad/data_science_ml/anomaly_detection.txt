## Problem Framing

Anomaly detection identifies observations that differ from expected behaviour, and the framing
problem is that "anomalous" is not a property of an observation but a relationship between an
observation and a definition of normal. The same value may be routine in one context and alarming
in another, so the first task is establishing what normal means and over what population.

The available supervision determines the approach and is the most consequential distinction.
Supervised detection has labelled examples of both classes and is simply an imbalanced
classification problem, with all the standard techniques applying. Semi-supervised detection has
labelled normal data and no labelled anomalies, so a model of normality is built and departures
are flagged — which is the common situation, since normal behaviour is abundant and anomalies are
rare and heterogeneous. Unsupervised detection has no labels and assumes anomalies are rare and
different, identifying them by their statistical isolation.

The critical asymmetry favouring semi-supervised and unsupervised methods is that anomalies are
heterogeneous while normal behaviour is not. A supervised model learns the anomalies it was shown
and generalizes poorly to novel failure modes, which is precisely the case that matters most — a
new attack, an unprecedented equipment failure, a fraud technique not previously seen. Modelling
normality and flagging departures catches novel anomalies by construction.

Framing:
  anomaly              relative to a definition of normal, not intrinsic
  point anomaly        a single observation unusual in isolation
  contextual anomaly   normal in general and unusual in its context; a temperature
                       normal for July occurring in January
  collective anomaly   a subsequence unusual as a group while each point is
                       ordinary
  supervised           labelled both classes; imbalanced classification
  semi-supervised      labelled normal only; model normality, flag departures
  unsupervised         no labels; assume anomalies are rare and different
  novelty detection    identify data unlike the training distribution
  outlier detection    identify unusual points within a contaminated dataset
  the heterogeneity asymmetry  normal is coherent, anomalies are diverse; this
                       favours modelling normality
  novel failure modes  the case that matters most and that supervised methods
                       generalize to worst
  what happens next    the detection's purpose; determines the acceptable false
                       positive rate and therefore the threshold

Q: A supervised fraud model trained on historical labelled fraud performs well in backtesting and
misses a new fraud technique entirely. This is:
A. A model quality problem to be fixed by more training
B. Expected, since a supervised model learns the anomaly types it was shown and novel types are
   outside its training distribution
C. A data leakage problem
D. A threshold problem
Reasoning: Supervised learning generalizes within the distribution it was trained on, and a novel
technique is by definition outside it. Detecting novel anomalies requires modelling normality and
flagging departures rather than modelling known anomalies. Production fraud systems combine both:
supervised models for known patterns and unsupervised detection for novelty. Answer: B.

## Statistical and Distance-Based Methods

The simplest statistical approaches assume a distribution and flag observations in its tails.
The three-sigma rule flags points more than three standard deviations from the mean, which is
straightforward and rests on a normality assumption that real data frequently violates, and which
is additionally corrupted by the anomalies themselves — the mean and standard deviation are
computed from data containing the outliers, so extreme values inflate the standard deviation and
mask themselves.

Robust statistics address the masking problem by using estimators insensitive to outliers. The
median and the median absolute deviation replace the mean and standard deviation, and because the
median is unaffected by extreme values regardless of their magnitude, a few outliers do not
distort the reference. This is a small change that substantially improves reliability and is
underused relative to how simple it is.

Distance-based methods define anomalies by isolation in the feature space, with the distance to
the k-th nearest neighbour being the natural score. Their assumption is that normal points lie in
dense regions and anomalies do not, and their limitation is that a global distance threshold
performs badly when the data has regions of differing density — a point that is anomalous within a
dense cluster may be closer to its neighbours than a normal point in a sparse region.

Local outlier factor addresses this by comparing each point's local density to that of its
neighbours rather than to a global standard. A point in a sparse region with neighbours in
similarly sparse regions is normal; a point whose neighbours are much denser than its own
neighbourhood is anomalous. This local normalization is what makes the method work on data with
varying density, and it is the standard reference for distance-based detection.

Statistical and distance methods:
  three sigma          simple; assumes normality and is corrupted by the outliers
                       it seeks
  masking              outliers inflate the variance estimate and hide themselves
  robust estimators    median and median absolute deviation; unaffected by extremes
  modified z-score     using robust estimators; a drop-in improvement
  interquartile range  the box plot rule; distribution-free
  Grubbs and Dixon     formal tests for a single outlier; assume normality
  Mahalanobis distance  accounts for covariance; the correct multivariate distance
                       when the data is roughly elliptical
  k-nearest neighbour  distance to the kth neighbour as the score; assumes uniform
                       density
  local outlier factor  compares local density to neighbours'; handles varying
                       density
  DBSCAN               density clustering; points not assigned to a cluster are
                       noise, which is anomaly detection as a side effect
  the curse of dimensionality  distances concentrate in high dimension so all
                       points become equidistant; distance-based methods degrade
                       and require dimensionality reduction first

The curse of dimensionality deserves specific emphasis for this application because it undermines
the premise. As dimension grows, the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbour distances
approaches one, so every point is approximately equidistant from every other and the notion of
being distant loses discriminating power. Distance-based anomaly detection therefore degrades
badly above a few dozen dimensions, and the remedies are feature selection, dimensionality
reduction, or methods that do not rely on distance — which is part of why isolation-based and
reconstruction-based approaches became prominent for high-dimensional data.

## Model-Based Methods

Isolation forest takes an approach that inverts the usual reasoning. Rather than profiling normal
points and measuring departure, it isolates points by random partitioning, and the observation is
that anomalies are isolated in fewer splits because they are few and different. The score is the
average path length to isolation across many random trees. It has linear time complexity, needs no
distance computation, handles high dimensions well, and requires no assumption about the
distribution's shape — which together make it the strongest general-purpose default.

Reconstruction-based methods learn to compress and reconstruct normal data, and use the
reconstruction error as the anomaly score. An autoencoder trained on normal data learns a
representation capturing its structure, so normal inputs reconstruct accurately and anomalous ones
do not. Principal component analysis provides the linear version, where the reconstruction error
from the leading components serves the same purpose.

One-class support vector machines learn a boundary enclosing the normal data in a feature space,
flagging points outside it. They are principled and require kernel and parameter selection that is
difficult without labelled anomalies to validate against, which is the recurring difficulty in
this field — the methods have hyperparameters and the data to tune them is exactly what is
missing.

Model-based methods:
  isolation forest     random partitioning; anomalies isolate in fewer splits;
                       linear time, high-dimensional, assumption-free
  extended isolation forest  non-axis-parallel splits; removes an artifact of the
                       original
  autoencoder          reconstruct normal data; error is the score
  variational autoencoder  a probabilistic version; gives a likelihood
  PCA reconstruction   the linear case; cheap and effective when the structure is
                       linear
  one-class SVM        a boundary enclosing normal data; sensitive to parameters
  Gaussian mixture     model the density; low-likelihood points are anomalous
  kernel density       non-parametric density; bandwidth selection is the
                       difficulty
  normalizing flows    exact likelihood under a learned transformation; strong and
                       computationally heavy
  deep methods caution  frequently outperformed by isolation forest on tabular
                       data, and the published comparisons are heavily affected by
                       tuning on the test set given the absence of validation
                       labels
  ensembles            combine several detectors; robust to any one method's
                       assumption failing, and the standard practical choice

The caution about deep methods deserves stating because the literature is unusually unreliable
here. Evaluating an unsupervised anomaly detector requires labels, and the labels used are the
test labels, so hyperparameter selection frequently involves the test set — which inflates results
in a way that is difficult to detect from the paper. Independent comparisons repeatedly find that
isolation forest and simple statistical baselines match or exceed elaborate deep methods on
tabular data, and the practical recommendation is to establish those baselines first and require a
substantial margin before adopting something more complex.

## Time Series Anomalies

Time series anomaly detection differs because the temporal structure is itself informative. A
value is not anomalous in isolation but relative to what the series' recent history and seasonal
pattern predict, so the natural approach is to forecast and flag large residuals.

That framing handles several anomaly types with one mechanism. A point anomaly produces a large
one-step residual. A level shift produces a sustained sequence of same-signed residuals. A change
in variance produces residuals whose magnitude changes. And a change in the seasonal pattern
produces residuals correlated with the seasonal phase. Examining the residual structure rather
than only its magnitude therefore identifies the anomaly type as well as its presence.

The decomposition approach separates trend, seasonality, and remainder, and applies outlier
detection to the remainder. Using robust estimation in the decomposition matters, because a
non-robust decomposition absorbs the anomalies into the trend or seasonal components and they
disappear from the residual — the anomaly hides itself in the model fitted to find it.

Change point detection is the related task of identifying when the generating process changed
rather than which individual points are unusual. Cumulative sum methods accumulate deviations and
signal when the accumulation exceeds a threshold, which detects small persistent shifts far more
efficiently than examining points individually. Bayesian change point methods give a posterior
over change locations and handle multiple changes naturally.

Time series methods:
  forecast residuals   the general-purpose approach; large residuals are anomalies
  residual structure   the pattern identifies the anomaly type, not only its
                       presence
  robust decomposition  seasonal-trend decomposition with robust fitting; a
                       non-robust fit absorbs the anomalies
  seasonal hybrid ESD  decompose then apply an outlier test; a common production
                       choice
  control charts       Shewhart for large shifts, CUSUM and EWMA for small
                       persistent ones
  CUSUM                accumulates deviation; detects small shifts efficiently
  change point         when the process changed rather than which points are odd
  matrix profile       subsequence similarity; finds discords and motifs without
                       specifying what to look for
  multivariate         correlated series; a single series within normal range while
                       the correlation structure breaks is a common and important
                       case
  prediction interval  the threshold from the forecast's uncertainty rather than
                       from a fixed value; adapts to periods of higher variance
  seasonality          multiple periods are common; hourly, daily, weekly, and
                       annual can all be present simultaneously
  the retraining question  a model retrained on data containing the anomaly learns
                       to expect it

The multivariate case deserves emphasis because it catches failures that univariate monitoring
cannot. In a system with correlated measurements, a fault frequently manifests as a break in the
correlation rather than as any single series leaving its normal range — a pump's power draw and
flow rate normally move together, and a blockage raises pressure while reducing flow with both
remaining within their individual limits. Monitoring the residual from a model predicting each
series from the others detects this, and monitoring each series independently does not.

Model the seasonality. Static thresholds on metrics with daily and weekly cycles fire every
morning when traffic rises and every Monday when the week starts, and those are the normal
pattern rather than anomalies. Replace fixed thresholds with a baseline predicted from the same
time of day and day of week, and flag departures from that. This typically removes half to three
quarters of the volume immediately because most of it is periodic.

Group correlated alerts. One underlying incident produces alerts on every metric it touches —
latency, error rate, queue depth, CPU, and downstream dependencies all fire together, and the team
receives twenty alerts describing one problem. Grouping by time and by service topology into a
single incident is what converts a wall of alerts into a comprehensible signal.

Add severity tiers tied to what should happen. An alert that should wake someone, an alert that
should be looked at during the working day, and an alert that should be recorded and reviewed
weekly are three different things and currently all page. Deciding the response for each alert
type before it fires is what makes the tier meaningful.

Then tie the threshold to the action rather than to the statistics. The right false positive rate
depends on the cost of investigating versus the cost of missing, and those differ by metric. A
detector for data loss should be sensitive and a detector for a latency blip should not.

One measurement to add: track the action rate per alert type, not just overall. That identifies
which specific rules are producing the noise, and in most systems a small number of rules account
for the large majority of the volume. Fixing or deleting those is a short piece of work with a
large effect.

## Evaluation, Deployment, and Applications

Evaluating anomaly detection is difficult for a structural reason: labels are scarce because
anomalies are rare, and the labels that exist are biased because they were found by whatever
method was previously in use. A detector evaluated against labels produced by the incumbent system
is rewarded for agreeing with it rather than for being better.

The metrics require care because of the extreme imbalance. Accuracy is meaningless when anomalies
are one in ten thousand. Precision and recall at the operating threshold are informative and
depend on the threshold, so the full precision-recall curve and the average precision summarize
better. Precision at a fixed alert budget — how many of the top hundred flagged are genuine — is
frequently the most operationally meaningful measure, because the alert budget is what the team
can actually investigate.

Deployment concerns dominate whether a detector delivers value. The threshold determines the
alert volume, and it should be set from the investigation capacity and the cost of a miss rather
than from a statistical criterion. Alert fatigue is the failure mode that renders any detector
useless, and it results from volume rather than from any individual alert being wrong. And drift
means normal behaviour changes, so a static model degrades and a continuously retrained one
gradually learns to accept the anomalies.

Evaluation and deployment:
  label scarcity       few labelled anomalies; and those that exist are biased by
                       how they were found
  incumbent bias       evaluating against the previous system's findings rewards
                       agreement rather than improvement
  accuracy is useless  at extreme imbalance
  precision and recall  at the operating threshold; report both
  average precision    summarizes the curve
  precision at budget  how many of the top k are genuine; the operationally
                       meaningful measure
  threshold from capacity  set by what can be investigated and the cost of a miss
  alert fatigue        the dominant failure mode; caused by volume
  grouping             one incident should produce one alert
  explanation          which feature or which series drove the score; without it,
                       an alert is not actionable
  feedback loop        record investigation outcomes; the only route to labels
  drift                normal changes; static models degrade, continuously
                       retrained ones learn to accept anomalies
  retraining exclusion  exclude confirmed anomalies from the training window
  human in the loop    the detector proposes and a person disposes; the realistic
                       architecture for most applications

The explanation requirement deserves the closing emphasis because it determines whether a
detection is actionable. An alert stating that an observation is anomalous, with a score, gives an
investigator nothing to start from — they must reconstruct why from scratch, which for a
high-dimensional system is the bulk of the work. An alert identifying which features contributed
most to the score, what their values were, and what was expected converts the investigation from
open-ended to specific. For isolation forest this is available from which features were used in
the short isolating paths; for reconstruction methods it is the per-feature reconstruction error;
for forecast-based methods it is the residual per series. Providing it costs little and is the
difference between a detector that is used and one that is switched off.
