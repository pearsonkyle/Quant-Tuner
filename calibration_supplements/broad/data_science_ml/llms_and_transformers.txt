## Attention

Scaled dot-product attention is the operation the entire architecture is built from.
Given queries Q, keys K, and values V, it computes softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k))V. Each
query is compared against every key by dot product, those similarities are normalized
into weights summing to one, and the output is the weighted average of the values. The
mechanism is content-based retrieval: the query asks what it is looking for, the keys
advertise what each position offers, and the values carry what is actually retrieved.

The sqrt(d_k) scaling is not cosmetic. If the components of Q and K are roughly
independent with unit variance, their dot product over d_k dimensions has variance
d_k, so the logits grow with dimension. Large logits push softmax into a saturated
regime where it approaches a one-hot distribution and its gradient nearly vanishes,
which stalls training. Dividing by sqrt(d_k) restores unit variance and keeps the
softmax in its responsive range. This is a good example of a detail that looks like
tuning and is actually load-bearing.

Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel on projected
subspaces, then concatenates and projects the result. The motivation is that a single
softmax-weighted average can only attend to one thing at a time in a meaningful sense —
averaging over several distinct positions blends them — whereas separate heads can
simultaneously track syntactic dependency, coreference, and positional adjacency.
Empirically, heads specialize in interpretable ways, and many can be pruned after
training with little loss, suggesting substantial redundancy.

Causal masking is what makes a decoder autoregressive. Setting the attention logits for
future positions to negative infinity before the softmax ensures each position attends
only to itself and earlier positions, so the model can be trained on all positions in
parallel while remaining valid for left-to-right generation. Without the mask the model
trivially cheats by reading the token it is asked to predict, and the symptom is a
training loss that collapses toward zero while generation produces nonsense — one of
the more memorable bugs to encounter.

Attention variants and what they trade:
  multi-head (MHA)      separate K, V per head; maximum expressiveness, largest
                        KV cache
  multi-query (MQA)     one K, V shared across all heads; cache shrinks by the head
                        count, some quality loss
  grouped-query (GQA)   K, V shared within groups; the standard compromise, used by
                        most current large models
  sliding window        attend only to the last W positions; linear in sequence
                        length, loses exact long-range access
  flash attention       exact, IO-aware tiling that never materializes the n-by-n
                        matrix; a memory and speed win with identical math
  linear attention      kernel approximation removing the softmax; O(n) but
                        consistently weaker in practice

The quadratic cost is the architecture's defining constraint. Attention is O(n^2 d) in
sequence length for both compute and, naively, memory. Flash attention removes the
memory term by tiling and recomputation, keeping the exact result, which is why it is a
pure win and universally adopted. The compute term remains, which is why context length
extension is expensive and why sparse and windowed patterns keep being proposed. At
inference the picture changes: with a KV cache, each new token attends over the whole
cache, so per-token cost is linear in context length and the cache size becomes the
dominant memory consumer for long contexts.

Q: In scaled dot-product attention, removing the 1/sqrt(d_k) factor most directly
causes:
A. Incorrect masking
B. Softmax saturation and vanishing gradients at large d_k
C. Quadratic memory growth
D. Loss of permutation invariance
Reasoning: Dot products over d_k dimensions have variance proportional to d_k, so
without scaling the logits grow with model width, pushing softmax toward a one-hot
output where its gradient is nearly zero. The other options are unaffected by the
scaling factor. Answer: B.

## Positional Information

Attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the input positions and the outputs
shuffle identically. Without positional information a transformer cannot distinguish
"the dog bit the man" from "the man bit the dog", so position must be injected
explicitly. How it is injected turns out to matter enormously for how well a model
extrapolates beyond its training context.

Sinusoidal encodings, from the original paper, add fixed sine and cosine functions of
varying frequency to the token embeddings. They require no parameters and in principle
allow extrapolation, since the functions are defined at any position, but in practice
models trained with them degrade quickly beyond their training length. Learned absolute
embeddings assign a trainable vector per position, which works well within the trained
range and cannot extrapolate at all, since positions beyond the table have no
embedding.

Rotary position embeddings are now the dominant choice. Rather than adding a positional
vector, RoPE rotates the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to their
position, applying a rotation in each two-dimensional subspace at a frequency that
varies across dimensions. Because attention depends on the dot product of a query and a
key, and the relative rotation between them depends only on their positional
difference, RoPE encodes relative position while being applied absolutely. That
property is what makes it both efficient and well-behaved.

Context extension methods build on RoPE's structure. Position interpolation scales
positions down so that a longer sequence maps into the trained angular range, trading
resolution for reach, and requires brief fine-tuning to recover quality. NTK-aware
scaling adjusts the frequency base rather than the positions uniformly, preserving
high-frequency detail for nearby tokens while extending the low-frequency components
that carry long-range information. YaRN combines interpolation with an attention
temperature adjustment. These methods routinely extend a model trained at 4k tokens to
32k or beyond with modest fine-tuning, which is why context windows grew so quickly
across the field.

Positional method comparison:
  sinusoidal absolute   parameter-free, poor extrapolation in practice
  learned absolute      strong in-range, zero extrapolation
  relative bias (T5)    learned bias per relative distance bucket; extrapolates
                        moderately
  ALiBi                 linear distance penalty added to attention logits; strong
                        extrapolation, implicit recency bias
  RoPE                  rotation encoding relative position; the modern default
  RoPE + interpolation  context extension with brief fine-tuning
  NoPE                  no explicit encoding; causal masking alone conveys some
                        positional information in decoder-only models

## Tokenization

Tokenization converts text into the integer sequence the model consumes, and it is the
most consequential preprocessing decision in the stack. Character-level tokenization
gives a tiny vocabulary and very long sequences, wasting the quadratic attention budget
on trivial structure. Word-level gives short sequences and an unbounded vocabulary that
cannot handle unseen words. Subword tokenization splits the difference: common words
become single tokens, rare words decompose into meaningful pieces, and the vocabulary
stays fixed while any string remains representable.

Byte-pair encoding builds the vocabulary greedily. Start from individual bytes, count
adjacent pairs across a corpus, merge the most frequent pair into a new token, and
repeat until the target vocabulary size is reached. The learned merge list is then
applied deterministically at inference. WordPiece, used by BERT, selects merges by
likelihood improvement rather than raw frequency. Unigram, used by SentencePiece,
starts from a large candidate vocabulary and prunes, which permits probabilistic
segmentation and subword regularization during training.

Byte-level BPE, used by the GPT family and most current models, operates on UTF-8 bytes
rather than Unicode characters. This guarantees that any input is representable with no
unknown token, at the cost of multi-byte characters consuming multiple tokens — which
is why non-Latin scripts and emoji have systematically worse token efficiency and
therefore cost more per unit of text and consume context faster. A page of English might
be 250 tokens and the same content in a script requiring three bytes per character
might be 700.

Tokenization consequences worth knowing:
  character-level tasks   models struggle to count letters or reverse strings
                          because characters are not individually visible inside a
                          multi-character token
  arithmetic              number tokenization determines digit alignment; splitting
                          numbers into consistent digit groups measurably improves
                          arithmetic
  code                    indentation and common keywords benefit from dedicated
                          tokens; tokenizers trained without code handle it poorly
  trailing whitespace     a trailing space changes which token follows and can
                          substantially degrade completion quality
  vocabulary size         larger vocabulary means shorter sequences and a larger
                          embedding and output layer; 32k-256k is the current range
  special tokens          control markers must be parsed as single ids, never as
                          literal text; mismatches here silently corrupt training
                          and evaluation

The special-token pitfall deserves emphasis because it is subtle and consequential. A
chat-formatted corpus contains control markers delimiting turns. If the training or
evaluation path tokenizes them as ordinary text rather than as the reserved single ids,
the model sees a completely different sequence than intended — several tokens where one
belongs — and the resulting distribution shift is invisible in any surface inspection of
the text. This is the same class of error as evaluating perplexity on a chat corpus with
a tool that lacks special-token parsing: the comparison between two models on the same
file remains valid, but the absolute numbers describe a distribution the model was never
trained on.

Q: A model consistently fails to count the letters in a word. The most fundamental
cause is:
A. Insufficient training data
B. Words are single tokens, so individual characters are not separately represented
C. The attention window is too small
D. The vocabulary is too large
Reasoning: Subword tokenization maps a common word to one token id, and the model
receives an embedding for that id rather than a sequence of characters. Character
identity is only recoverable to the extent it was learned indirectly. This is an
architectural consequence of tokenization, not a data or capacity limitation.
Answer: B.

## Pretraining and Scaling

Causal language modelling — predicting the next token given all previous ones — is the
dominant pretraining objective, and its power comes from being simultaneously
self-supervised (any text is training data), dense (every position provides a
gradient), and general (accurate next-token prediction requires syntax, semantics,
world knowledge, and reasoning). Masked language modelling, predicting held-out tokens
from bidirectional context, produces better representations for classification but does
not support generation naturally, which is why the decoder-only causal architecture won
for general-purpose models.

Scaling laws describe how loss falls with model size, data, and compute, and they are
strikingly regular: loss follows a power law in each factor over many orders of
magnitude. The practical question is how to split a fixed compute budget between
parameters and tokens. The Chinchilla analysis concluded that earlier large models were
substantially undertrained, and that compute-optimal training scales parameters and
tokens roughly in proportion — approximately 20 tokens per parameter. That finding
redirected the field from ever-larger models toward better-trained ones.

Compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal, and the distinction drives current practice.
Chinchilla scaling minimizes training loss for a given training budget, but a model that
will serve billions of inference requests should be smaller and trained far longer than
compute-optimal, because inference cost scales with parameters while training cost is
paid once. This is why recent small models are trained on token counts an order of
magnitude beyond the Chinchilla ratio and outperform much larger models from earlier
generations.

Scaling relationships:
  loss vs parameters    L ∝ N^-a, roughly a ≈ 0.07 in the observed range
  loss vs data          L ∝ D^-b, similar exponent
  compute              C ≈ 6ND FLOPs for a dense transformer (forward + backward)
  Chinchilla optimal    D ≈ 20N tokens for a fixed training budget
  inference-optimal     far more tokens per parameter; overtrain deliberately
  emergent behaviour    some capabilities appear abruptly with scale on discrete
                        metrics; often smooth under continuous metrics, so the
                        "emergence" is partly a measurement artifact
  data quality          deduplication, filtering, and curation shift the curve
                        itself, not just the position along it

Data quality is the least glamorous and most decisive factor. Deduplication matters
enormously: repeated documents cause memorization and waste budget, and near-duplicate
detection across a web-scale corpus is a substantial engineering problem in itself.
Filtering for quality — by classifier, by heuristic, by source — reliably improves
downstream capability at fixed compute. Contamination, where evaluation data leaks into
training, inflates benchmark scores without improving capability and is pervasive
enough that any headline benchmark number from an undisclosed corpus deserves
skepticism.

Mixture-of-experts architectures decouple parameter count from per-token compute. Each
feedforward block is replaced by many expert networks with a router selecting a small
number per token, so a model with a trillion total parameters might activate only tens
of billions per token. This gives the capacity benefits of scale at a fraction of the
inference FLOPs, at the cost of high memory requirements (all experts must be resident),
routing instability during training requiring auxiliary load-balancing losses, and more
complex distributed serving.

## Fine-tuning and Alignment

A pretrained base model predicts likely continuations and does not follow instructions,
because nothing in its objective rewarded doing so. Post-training turns it into an
assistant. Supervised fine-tuning trains on curated instruction-response pairs, which
teaches format, style, and task-following. This alone produces a usable assistant and is
by far the largest single quality jump in the pipeline.

Preference optimization goes further by learning from comparisons rather than
demonstrations, which is important because it is far easier for annotators to say which
of two responses is better than to write an ideal one. Reinforcement learning from human
feedback trains a reward model on preference pairs and then optimizes the policy against
it with PPO, including a KL penalty against the reference model to prevent the policy
drifting into degenerate text that games the reward. Direct preference optimization
derives a closed-form objective that optimizes the same preference likelihood without
training a separate reward model or running RL, which is dramatically simpler and has
become the common default.

Reward hacking is the characteristic failure. The reward model is a proxy for human
preference, and optimizing hard against any proxy eventually exploits its errors. The
observable symptoms are familiar: responses grow longer because length correlates with
preference in the training data, hedging and caveats proliferate, and the model becomes
sycophantic because agreement is rated more favourably. The KL penalty limits drift, and
careful reward model construction helps, but the underlying tension is structural and is
why alignment is an ongoing engineering effort rather than a solved step.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods:
  full fine-tuning    updates every parameter; best quality, requires optimizer
                      state for the whole model (~12-16 bytes per parameter)
  LoRA                trains low-rank update matrices alongside frozen weights;
                      typically 0.1-1% of parameters, mergeable at inference
  QLoRA               LoRA on a 4-bit quantized base model; fits large models on
                      a single consumer GPU
  adapters            small trained modules inserted between layers; adds
                      inference latency unless merged
  prefix / prompt     trains virtual tokens prepended to the input; very few
                      parameters, weaker than LoRA on hard tasks
  choosing rank       r = 8-16 for style and format adaptation; 64+ when teaching
                      genuinely new capability

LoRA's effectiveness rests on the empirical observation that fine-tuning updates have
low intrinsic rank — adaptation moves the weights within a low-dimensional subspace, so
representing the update as a product of two thin matrices loses little. The practical
consequences are large: a single base model can serve many tasks by swapping small
adapters, training memory drops by an order of magnitude because optimizer state is
needed only for the adapter, and the adapter can be merged into the base weights after
training so inference cost is unchanged.

Full fine-tuning needs about 318 GB — 60 GB for bf16 weights plus roughly 240 GB of
AdamW optimizer state (fp32 master weights plus two moment buffers, about 12-16 bytes
per parameter) plus activations. That is four A100s minimum even with aggressive
sharding, and it is not close.

Plain LoRA at 78.4 GB technically fits in 80 GB but leaves no headroom. You would hit
fragmentation OOMs at unpredictable points, and you could not raise the batch size or
sequence length. Not worth the fragility.

QLoRA lands at about 35 GB by keeping the base weights in 4-bit NF4 and training only
the adapter. That leaves you room to raise batch size or context length, and for a
style-adaptation task the quantized base costs very little quality — you are not
teaching new knowledge, you are teaching format and tone.

Concrete settings for documentation style: rank 16 is sufficient and rank 32 is a safe
upper bound; target all linear projections rather than just the attention ones, which
consistently outperforms the original attention-only recommendation; alpha equal to the
rank; learning rate around 1e-4 to 2e-4, an order of magnitude higher than full
fine-tuning tolerates. Use gradient checkpointing and paged optimizers.

The thing most likely to determine your outcome is not the method but the data. A few
hundred to a few thousand high-quality examples in exactly the target style will beat
tens of thousands of mixed-quality ones. Hold out a set from the start and read the
generations yourself — for style adaptation, loss is a poor proxy for whether it worked.

## Inference and Evaluation

Generation is autoregressive: the model produces a distribution over the vocabulary,
one token is selected, appended, and the process repeats. This makes generation
sequential and therefore latency-bound in a way training is not. The KV cache stores
past keys and values so each step attends over history without recomputation, reducing
per-token cost from quadratic to linear. The cache grows with context and with batch
size and frequently exceeds the model weights in memory for long contexts, which is
what motivated grouped-query attention and what PagedAttention addresses by managing the
cache in non-contiguous blocks to eliminate fragmentation and enable prefix sharing.

Decoding strategy shapes output character. Greedy decoding takes the argmax and is
deterministic but repetitive and prone to loops. Beam search maintains several
hypotheses and is appropriate for tasks with a single correct answer such as
translation, while producing bland text for open-ended generation. Temperature scales
the logits before softmax, with lower values sharpening the distribution. Top-k
restricts sampling to the k most likely tokens; top-p (nucleus) restricts to the
smallest set whose cumulative probability exceeds p, adapting the candidate count to
the model's confidence. Min-p sets a threshold relative to the top token's probability
and behaves well across a wide temperature range.

Serving throughput comes from batching, and the arithmetic explains why. Decoding at
batch size one is memory-bandwidth-bound: every weight is read to produce one token, so
the arithmetic intensity is terrible. Batching amortizes those reads across many
sequences, so throughput rises nearly linearly with batch size until the workload
becomes compute-bound. Continuous batching improves on static batching by admitting new
requests as others finish rather than waiting for the whole batch, which raises
utilization substantially under realistic arrival patterns. Speculative decoding attacks
latency instead: a small draft model proposes several tokens and the large model
verifies them in one parallel forward pass, accepting the longest correct prefix, which
yields real speedups with provably identical output distribution.

Inference optimization levers:
  KV cache            essential; linear per-token cost instead of quadratic
  GQA / MQA           shrinks the cache by the group factor
  quantization        weight-only int8/int4 nearly proportionally reduces
                      bandwidth-bound decode latency
  continuous batching raises throughput under mixed request lengths
  paged KV cache      eliminates fragmentation, enables prefix sharing
  prefix caching      reuse the KV for a shared system prompt across requests
  speculative decode  draft-and-verify; identical outputs, lower latency
  multi-token predict a bundled draft head predicting several future tokens
  chunked prefill     interleaves long prompt processing with decode so one long
                      request does not stall others

Evaluation is the weakest link in the whole enterprise. Perplexity measures next-token
prediction on held-out text and correlates with capability across large differences
while being nearly useless for fine distinctions or for comparing models with different
tokenizers — perplexity is per token, and different tokenizers mean different tokens.
Multiple-choice benchmarks are cheap and reproducible and are heavily contaminated,
scoring capabilities that may not transfer to generation. Human preference rankings
capture what users actually want and are expensive, slow, and biased toward length and
confident presentation. LLM-as-judge is the practical compromise and inherits the
judge's biases, including a well-documented preference for its own outputs and for the
first option presented.

For quantization specifically, the right evaluation is comparison against the
full-precision reference rather than an absolute benchmark score. KL divergence between
the quantized and reference output distributions over a representative corpus is the
most sensitive available signal, since it measures the whole distribution rather than
the argmax and detects degradation long before accuracy metrics move. Perplexity delta
is a reasonable cheap proxy. Task-level evaluation on the actual deployment
distribution is the ground truth, and the recurring finding is that static metrics and
agentic performance can diverge sharply at low bit widths — a model whose perplexity
barely moved may have lost the ability to produce well-formed tool calls, because that
capability depends on the tail of the distribution rather than on average next-token
accuracy.
