## Image Formation and Representation

An image is a projection of a three-dimensional scene onto a two-dimensional sensor, and
the information lost in that projection is what makes vision hard. Depth is not directly
recoverable from a single view, scale and distance are confounded, and the appearance of a
surface depends on illumination, viewing angle, and material properties as much as on the
object's identity. Every vision system is in some sense recovering what the projection
discarded.

The pinhole camera model relates world coordinates to image coordinates through a
perspective projection, parameterized by intrinsics — focal length, principal point, and skew
— and extrinsics giving the camera's pose in the world. Real lenses deviate from the pinhole
model through radial and tangential distortion, which is corrected by calibration: imaging a
known pattern from many angles and solving for the parameters that best explain the observed
positions. Uncalibrated geometry is why measurements from ordinary photographs are unreliable
and why any metric vision task begins with calibration.

Digital images quantize both space and intensity. Spatial sampling introduces aliasing when
the scene contains detail finer than the pixel spacing, which appears as moiré patterns on
regular structures and as jagged edges elsewhere; the remedy is optical low-pass filtering
before sampling, which is why many sensors include an anti-aliasing filter and why removing
it improves sharpness at the cost of moiré. Intensity quantization to eight bits is
adequate for display and inadequate for processing, since operations that stretch contrast
expose the quantization as banding — which is why raw capture and higher bit depths matter
for any pipeline that adjusts exposure.

Image formation concepts:
  pinhole model         perspective projection; parallel lines meet at vanishing
                        points
  intrinsics            focal length, principal point, skew; internal to the camera
  extrinsics            rotation and translation; the camera's pose
  radial distortion     barrel or pincushion; corrected by calibration
  colour spaces         RGB for capture and display, HSV for intuitive
                        manipulation, Lab for perceptual uniformity, YCbCr for
                        compression since chroma can be subsampled
  gamma                 display encoding is nonlinear; linearize before any
                        arithmetic on light
  bit depth             8-bit for display, 12-16 for capture; processing in higher
                        depth avoids banding
  aliasing              from sampling below Nyquist; requires optical prefiltering
  demosaicing           reconstructing colour from a Bayer pattern; a source of
                        artifacts at edges
  dynamic range         the sensor's ratio of saturation to noise floor; the
                        limiting factor in high-contrast scenes

The gamma issue deserves the same emphasis it receives in graphics, because it silently
corrupts image processing. Standard image files are gamma-encoded, so pixel values are not
proportional to light. Averaging, blurring, resizing, and blending performed directly on
encoded values produce results that are systematically too dark, and the effect is most
visible when downsampling — a checkerboard of black and white pixels averaged in encoded
space gives a mid-grey that is far darker than the correct result. Converting to linear
before arithmetic and back afterward fixes it, and libraries that do this by default are the
exception rather than the rule.

Q: Downsampling an image by averaging pixel values directly in an 8-bit sRGB file produces a
result that is too dark. The cause is:
A. Insufficient bit depth
B. sRGB values are gamma-encoded and not proportional to light, so averaging them does not
   average the light
C. The wrong interpolation kernel
D. Chroma subsampling
Reasoning: sRGB applies a roughly 2.2 power curve, so a value of 0.5 corresponds to about 22
percent of the light of a value of 1.0. Averaging encoded values therefore weights bright
regions too little. Linearizing, averaging, and re-encoding gives the correct result.
Answer: B.

## Classical Processing and Features

Filtering is the foundation of classical image processing and remains embedded inside modern
architectures. Convolution with a kernel computes a weighted sum over a neighbourhood, and
the kernel determines the operation: a Gaussian smooths, a Laplacian detects intensity
changes in all directions, and directional derivative kernels detect edges of a particular
orientation. Separability matters for cost — a two-dimensional Gaussian can be applied as two
one-dimensional passes, reducing the work from the square of the kernel size to twice its
size.

Nonlinear filters solve problems linear ones cannot. A median filter removes impulse noise
while preserving edges, because the median of a neighbourhood containing a few outliers is
unaffected by them while a mean is dragged toward them. Bilateral filtering smooths while
preserving edges by weighting neighbours by both spatial and intensity distance, so pixels
across an edge contribute little. Morphological operations on binary images — erosion,
dilation, and their compositions opening and closing — clean up segmentation results by
removing small artifacts and filling small gaps.

Feature detection finds points that can be reliably located across images, and the criterion
is distinctiveness in all directions. A flat region has no localizable position; an edge is
localizable perpendicular to itself and not along it; a corner is localizable in both
directions, which is why corner detectors are the basis of matching. Descriptors then
characterize the local appearance around each point in a way that is invariant to the
transformations expected — rotation, scale, illumination — so that the same physical point
produces a similar descriptor in a different image.

Classical operations:
  Gaussian blur        smoothing; separable; the standard scale-space operator
  Sobel / Scharr       gradient; edge detection with orientation
  Laplacian            second derivative; zero crossings at edges
  Canny                gradient, non-maximum suppression, hysteresis thresholding;
                       still the standard edge detector
  median               removes impulse noise, preserves edges
  bilateral            edge-preserving smoothing
  morphology           erosion, dilation, opening, closing on binary masks
  histogram equalization  contrast enhancement; CLAHE does it locally to avoid
                       amplifying noise in flat regions
  Harris corner        distinctive in both directions
  SIFT / ORB           scale- and rotation-invariant descriptors; ORB is free of
                       patent concerns and much faster
  RANSAC               robust model fitting from data with outliers; essential for
                       geometry estimation from noisy correspondences
  homography           projective transform between planes; image stitching and
                       perspective correction

RANSAC deserves emphasis because it is what makes geometry from real correspondences
possible. Feature matching produces many correct correspondences and a substantial fraction of
wrong ones, and a least-squares fit to that mixture is dominated by the outliers. RANSAC
repeatedly samples the minimum number of correspondences needed to fit the model, counts how
many others agree, and keeps the hypothesis with the most support. The number of iterations
needed grows with the outlier fraction and with the minimal sample size, which is why models
requiring fewer points are strongly preferred in this setting.

## Convolutional Networks

Convolutional networks impose two priors that match natural images: locality, since nearby
pixels are related, and translation equivariance, since a pattern's meaning does not depend on
where it appears. Weight sharing across positions implements both, reducing parameters
enormously relative to a fully connected layer and making learned features
position-independent.

The receptive field — the input region influencing one output unit — grows with depth and is
a real design constraint. Stacking 3x3 convolutions grows it by two per layer, so
capturing context spanning a large fraction of the image requires either many layers,
downsampling to increase the effective field relative to the image, or dilated convolutions
that skip positions and grow the field exponentially without additional parameters.
Architectures that fail on large objects frequently have receptive fields smaller than the
objects.

Residual connections were the change that made very deep networks trainable. Adding the
input of a block to its output gives gradients an identity path to flow along, so the
effective depth for optimization purposes is much less than the nominal depth, and networks of
hundreds of layers train stably where plain stacks of twenty degrade. The reframing worth
carrying is that a residual block learns a correction to the identity rather than a full
transformation, which is an easier optimization problem when the desired mapping is close to
identity.

Convolutional architecture elements:
  convolution           local weighted sum with shared weights
  stride                downsampling by skipping positions
  padding               preserves spatial size; 'same' padding is the usual default
  pooling               downsampling by max or average; increasingly replaced by
                        strided convolution
  1x1 convolution       channel mixing and dimensionality change with no spatial
                        extent; the workhorse of efficient architectures
  depthwise separable   spatial convolution per channel followed by 1x1 mixing;
                        roughly a tenfold reduction in computation
  dilated / atrous      skips positions; exponentially growing receptive field
  residual connection   identity path; enables very deep training
  batch normalization   stabilizes training; couples examples within a batch
  global average pool   replaces flattening plus dense layers; far fewer parameters
                        and translation invariant
  receptive field       must exceed the scale of the structures to be recognized

Data augmentation matters more for vision than for most domains because the invariances are
known and enumerable. Random crops teach translation invariance, horizontal flips teach
mirror invariance where appropriate, colour jitter teaches illumination robustness, and
rotation teaches orientation invariance where the domain permits. The caution is that the
augmentation must reflect an invariance the deployed system actually faces: vertical flips
are appropriate for satellite imagery and inappropriate for photographs of scenes with a
gravitational up, and applying them wastes capacity teaching an invariance that will never be
needed while potentially confusing classes distinguished by orientation.

## Detection, Segmentation, and Beyond Classification

Classification assigns a label to an entire image and is the simplest task. Detection
additionally localizes objects with bounding boxes and must handle a variable number of
outputs, which is the structural difficulty. Two-stage detectors first propose candidate
regions and then classify and refine them, giving high accuracy at higher latency. One-stage
detectors predict boxes and classes directly over a dense grid of positions, which is faster
and historically less accurate, with the gap having largely closed. Transformer-based
detectors treat detection as set prediction with learned queries, removing the hand-designed
anchor boxes and non-maximum suppression entirely.

Segmentation assigns a label per pixel. Semantic segmentation labels each pixel with a class
without distinguishing instances; instance segmentation separates individual objects;
panoptic segmentation combines both, labelling every pixel with a class and, for countable
classes, an instance. Encoder-decoder architectures with skip connections dominate, because
the encoder's downsampling provides context while the skip connections restore the spatial
detail that downsampling destroyed — which is exactly why the U-Net shape recurs across
segmentation, depth estimation, and image-to-image translation.

The evaluation metrics have specific properties worth understanding. Intersection over union
measures overlap between predicted and ground truth regions and is the standard for both
detection and segmentation. Mean average precision integrates precision over recall at a
range of IoU thresholds, which makes it sensitive to localization quality as well as
classification. The threshold choice matters: results reported at IoU 0.5 tolerate loose
boxes, and averaging over thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 rewards precise localization
substantially more.

Task and evaluation vocabulary:
  classification        one label per image
  detection             boxes plus classes; variable output count
  semantic segmentation per-pixel class, instances merged
  instance segmentation per-pixel class with instances separated
  panoptic              both, covering every pixel
  keypoint / pose       structured point prediction
  depth estimation      per-pixel distance; monocular depth is scale-ambiguous
  IoU                   intersection over union; the standard overlap measure
  mAP                   mean average precision; averaged over classes and IoU
                        thresholds
  non-maximum suppression  removes duplicate detections; a source of failure when
                        objects genuinely overlap
  anchor boxes          predefined shapes; require tuning to the dataset's object
                        aspect ratios
  class imbalance       most positions contain background; focal loss and hard
                        example mining address it

## Video, 3D, and Transformers

Video adds a temporal dimension and the associated redundancy and opportunity. Consecutive
frames are highly correlated, so processing every frame independently wastes computation, and
the motion between them carries information a single frame cannot. Optical flow estimates
per-pixel motion between frames and is the classical representation of that information.
Modern approaches use 3D convolutions over space and time, two-stream architectures
processing appearance and motion separately, or temporal attention.

Three-dimensional vision recovers geometry from images. Stereo uses two calibrated cameras
and computes disparity — the horizontal shift of corresponding points — which is inversely
proportional to depth, so precision degrades quadratically with distance and the usable range
is set by the baseline separation. Structure from motion recovers both camera poses and scene
structure from an unordered image collection. Simultaneous localization and mapping does the
same incrementally in real time, which is what robots and augmented reality devices require.
Neural radiance fields and Gaussian splatting represent a scene as a continuous or
point-based function optimized to reproduce the observed images, achieving photorealistic
novel view synthesis from a modest number of photographs.

Vision transformers apply the transformer architecture to images by splitting them into
patches, embedding each, and treating the sequence with self-attention. They discard the
locality and translation-equivariance priors that convolutions impose, which means they need
substantially more data to reach the same accuracy — the priors were doing real work — and
they exceed convolutional networks when that data is available. Hybrid architectures with
convolutional stems or hierarchical windowed attention recover much of the efficiency while
keeping the flexibility.

Video, 3D, and modern architectures:
  optical flow          per-pixel motion; classical and learned methods
  3D convolution        space-time filtering; expensive
  two-stream            separate appearance and motion pathways
  temporal attention    long-range dependencies across frames
  stereo disparity      depth from two views; precision falls as distance squared
  structure from motion poses and geometry from an image collection
  SLAM                  incremental, real-time mapping and localization
  NeRF / splatting      learned scene representation; photorealistic novel views
  vision transformer    patches plus self-attention; data-hungry, highly flexible
  hierarchical / windowed  restores locality and efficiency to attention
  self-supervised       contrastive or masked-patch pretraining; removes the label
                        bottleneck that dominated the field for a decade
  vision-language       joint image-text embedding; enables open-vocabulary
                        classification and retrieval without task-specific labels

Three changes, in order of how quickly they will help.

Change the metric first, because you cannot fix what you are not measuring. Report precision
and recall at your operating point, and average precision over the full curve. For defect
detection, also report recall at a precision level the process can tolerate — if operators
can inspect 10% of flagged parts, the meaningful number is recall at that flag rate.

Tune the threshold. A model trained with cross-entropy on imbalanced data outputs
well-calibrated probabilities that rarely exceed 0.5 for the rare class, so the default
threshold suppresses nearly all positive predictions. Sweeping the threshold and choosing
based on the actual cost of a missed defect versus a false alarm will likely improve recall
substantially with no retraining at all. This is often the entire fix.

Then address training. Focal loss down-weights easy negatives so that the gradient is
dominated by the hard and rare cases, and it was designed for exactly this regime. Class
weighting in the loss is a simpler alternative. Either is preferable to oversampling, which
distorts the probability calibration you need for threshold tuning.

Finally, your augmentation is generic. For defect detection, augmentation should reflect the
variation defects actually exhibit — scale, contrast against the substrate, partial occlusion,
position within the part. Flips and rotations teach invariances that may not even hold if
defects have a characteristic orientation relative to the manufacturing process.

One thing to verify before any of this: check whether the defects you are missing are
visually distinguishable at your input resolution. If the model downsamples a 4000-pixel
image to 224 pixels, a small defect may occupy less than one pixel after resizing, in which
case no amount of loss engineering helps and the answer is tiling the image and running
detection per tile.
