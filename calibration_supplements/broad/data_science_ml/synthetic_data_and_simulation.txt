## Why Synthesize, and What It Can and Cannot Fix

Synthetic data is generated rather than collected, and the reasons to use it fall into a few
distinct categories that call for different methods. Scarcity — the real data does not exist in
sufficient quantity, particularly for rare events. Cost — collecting or labelling is expensive
while generating is cheap. Privacy — the real data cannot be shared. Coverage — the real data
lacks the edge cases the system must handle. Control — an experiment requires knowing the ground
truth exactly, which real data never provides.

The critical constraint is that synthetic data contains only the information put into the generator.
A generator trained on real data can reproduce and interpolate its patterns; it cannot introduce
patterns absent from its training source. This means synthetic data multiplies the utility of
existing information and does not create new information, and any claim that it solves a data
scarcity problem needs to specify which of these two things is meant.

The corollary is that synthetic data inherits the biases of its source. A generator trained on a
dataset that underrepresents a population produces synthetic data that underrepresents it, usually
more severely, because generative models allocate capacity to the modes and smooth away the tails.
Using synthesis to address representation problems requires explicitly conditioning generation on
the underrepresented categories rather than expecting the generator to fix the imbalance.

  privacy              real data cannot be shared
  coverage             edge cases absent from real data
  control              exact ground truth for controlled experiments
  the fundamental constraint  a generator contains only the information given to
                       it
  interpolation not creation  patterns can be recombined, not invented

  mode collapse toward the majority  generative models smooth away tails

Q: A team synthesizes additional examples of a rare class from a model trained on the existing
imbalanced data, and classifier performance on that class does not improve. Why?
A. The synthesizer is broken
B. The generator learned the rare class from the same few examples the classifier already had, so
   its samples carry no information the classifier lacked; the bottleneck is information, not
   example count
C. Not enough synthetic data was generated
D. The classifier is too small
Reasoning: Generating more samples from a model fit to a handful of real examples produces
variations on those examples. The classifier already had access to that information. Real
additional examples, a generator informed by domain knowledge, or a physics-based simulator can
add information; resampling a fitted model cannot. Answer: B.

## Generation Methods

Rule-based and procedural generation constructs data from explicit specifications: grammars,
templates, physical models, or programmatic composition. Its advantages are complete control,
guaranteed label correctness, unlimited volume, and the ability to produce configurations that have
never occurred. Its limitation is that it produces only what was specified, so the realism gap is
exactly the gap between the specification and reality.

Simulation is procedural generation with a physical or behavioural model underneath — rendering
engines for images, physics engines for dynamics, agent models for behaviour, discrete event models
for systems. It is the dominant approach where the underlying process is well understood, and its
quality is bounded by the fidelity of the model rather than by any statistical property.

Learned generative models fit a distribution to real data and sample from it. Diffusion models
dominate for continuous modalities, autoregressive models for sequences, and each produces samples
that resemble the training distribution closely. Their advantage is realism without needing to
specify the process; their disadvantages are that labels must come from somewhere, that
out-of-distribution generation is unreliable, and that memorization of training examples is a real
possibility with privacy consequences.

## Sim-to-Real Transfer

Training in simulation and deploying in reality is the central problem in robotics and in several
perception domains, and the obstacle is the reality gap — the systematic difference between
simulated and real observations and dynamics. A policy or model that exploits any artifact of the
simulator will fail on contact with reality, and simulators have many artifacts.

Domain randomization is the technique that works most reliably. Rather than making the simulation
match reality precisely, it randomizes the simulation's parameters — textures, lighting, friction,
masses, sensor noise, latency — across a range wide enough that reality falls somewhere inside it.
The model then cannot rely on any particular parameter value and learns features robust across the
whole range, so reality is just another sample from a distribution it has already seen.

The complementary approach is system identification: measuring the real system's parameters and
tuning the simulator to match. It produces a more accurate simulator and is limited by what can be
measured and by the aspects of reality the simulator cannot represent at all. In practice both are
used — identify what can be identified, randomize what cannot — and the residual gap is closed by
fine-tuning on a small amount of real data.

Sim-to-real:
  the reality gap      systematic differences in observation and dynamics
  simulator artifacts  a model will exploit them and fail on transfer
  domain randomization  randomize parameters so reality is inside the training
                       distribution
  what to randomize    appearance, physics, sensor characteristics, timing
  the randomization range  wide enough to contain reality, narrow enough to
                       remain learnable
  system identification  measure and match the real parameters
  the combination      identify what you can, randomize what you cannot
  real fine-tuning     a small amount of real data closing the residual gap
  domain adaptation    aligning feature distributions between simulated and real
  the unmodelled residual  aspects reality has that the simulator does not
                       represent at all; randomization does not help with these
  the evaluation rule  measure on real data, always; simulated evaluation of a
                       simulation-trained model measures only self-consistency
  latency and control  timing differences between simulator and hardware are a
                       frequently overlooked component of the gap
  progressive transfer  simulation, then a controlled real setting, then
                       deployment; each stage catching a different failure class

## Privacy-Preserving Synthesis

Synthetic data is frequently proposed as a privacy solution: generate data resembling the real data
and share that instead. The reasoning is intuitive and the guarantee it provides by default is
none. A generative model fit to real records can memorize and reproduce them, and even without
verbatim reproduction, the synthetic data reflects the training data closely enough that membership
inference — determining whether a particular record was in the training set — is often successful.

A formal guarantee requires training the generator under differential privacy, which bounds how much
any single record can influence the output. This provides a quantified guarantee at a real utility
cost, and the cost falls unevenly: common patterns survive and rare ones — which are exactly the
records most identifiable and often most interesting — are suppressed. That suppression is the
privacy mechanism working as intended and it is also a limitation on what the synthetic data can be
used for.

Empirical privacy evaluation should accompany any synthetic release regardless of the method.
Nearest-neighbour distance between synthetic and real records detects near-duplicates. Membership
inference attacks estimate whether training membership is detectable. Attribute inference tests
whether sensitive fields can be predicted from released ones. A synthetic dataset that has not been
tested this way is a claim rather than a guarantee.

  the intuition        share generated data instead of real data
  the default guarantee  none
  memorization         verbatim reproduction of training records
  membership inference  detecting whether a record was in the training set
  differential privacy  a formal bound on any single record's influence
  the utility cost     real, and it falls on rare patterns
  the uneven suppression  rare records are both the most identifiable and often
                       the most interesting
  the privacy-utility frontier  the honest way to present the tradeoff
  nearest-neighbour testing  detects near-duplicate synthetic records
  attribute inference  predicting sensitive fields from released ones
  the testing obligation  untested synthesis is a claim, not a guarantee
  regulatory position  synthetic data is not automatically outside data
                       protection scope; the determination depends on whether
                       individuals remain identifiable
  the practical recommendation  formal guarantees where the data is sensitive,
                       empirical testing always, and explicit documentation of
                       what was and was not verified

## Evaluation and Failure Modes

Evaluating synthetic data means answering three separate questions, and conflating them is the
usual source of overclaiming. Fidelity asks whether the synthetic data resembles the real
distribution. Utility asks whether models trained on it perform well on real data. Privacy asks
whether it leaks information about individuals. High fidelity does not imply high utility — a
generator can match marginal distributions while destroying the correlations a model needs — and
high utility does not imply privacy.

Utility is the measure that matters most for machine learning applications, and the definitive test
is direct: train on synthetic, evaluate on real, and compare against training on real. The reverse
comparison — train on real, evaluate on synthetic — measures whether the synthetic data is
representative and is a useful complementary check. Both should be run because they fail
differently.

The failure modes worth screening for recur across domains. Mode collapse produces samples covering
only part of the distribution, which is invisible in aggregate statistics and obvious in a coverage
analysis. Distributional shift means the synthetic data differs systematically in ways that matter
downstream. Correlation destruction means the marginals are right and the joint structure is wrong.
Label noise from an imperfect generation process silently caps achievable accuracy. And the most
insidious is the feedback loop: training a generator on data that included previous synthetic
output progressively narrows the distribution over generations.

Evaluation and failures:
  three questions      fidelity, utility, privacy; distinct and often conflated
  fidelity             resemblance to the real distribution
  utility              performance on real data after training on synthetic
  privacy              leakage about individuals

  the reverse test     train real, evaluate synthetic; checks representativeness

                       generators fail
  coverage analysis    whether the full support is represented

  distributional shift  systematic difference in a downstream-relevant direction

  the feedback loop    generators trained on prior synthetic output progressively
