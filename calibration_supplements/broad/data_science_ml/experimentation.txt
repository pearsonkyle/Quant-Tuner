## Why Experiments and What They Measure

Online controlled experiments are the most reliable tool available for establishing that a
change caused an outcome, and their reliability comes entirely from randomization. Random
assignment makes treatment and control groups equivalent in expectation on every attribute —
those measured, those unmeasured, and those nobody thought of — so the difference in outcomes
estimates the causal effect without requiring a model of what else might differ. No
observational adjustment achieves this, because adjustment handles only the confounders you
measured.

The consistent empirical finding across large experimentation programmes is humbling: most
changes have no detectable effect, a substantial minority are harmful, and a small fraction
produce the gains. Published figures from several large technology companies converge on
roughly a third of changes being positive, a third neutral, and a third negative. This is the
strongest argument for experimenting: teams cannot reliably predict which of their changes
work, and the ones that seem most obviously beneficial include a meaningful share of the
harmful ones.

The unit of randomization must be chosen deliberately. Randomizing by user gives a consistent
experience and captures cumulative effects, and it is the default for anything a user might
notice. Randomizing by session or request gives more units and therefore more power, and it
breaks down when the treatment produces a visibly inconsistent experience or when effects
accumulate across sessions. Randomizing by cluster — geography, account, or social group — is
required when interference between units is present, at a substantial cost in effective
sample size.

Experiment design essentials:
  randomization unit    user > session > request; choose where interference stops
  hash-based assignment deterministic from user ID and experiment ID; stable across
                        sessions and independent between experiments
  A/A test              run before launch; validates the assignment and the
                        analysis pipeline, and should show no effect
  sample ratio check    the highest-value diagnostic; a mismatch invalidates
                        everything downstream
  fixed sample size     computed in advance from the minimum detectable effect
  minimum detectable effect  the smallest change that would alter a decision;
                        driven by business value, not by statistical convenience
  randomization seed    independent across experiments to avoid carryover
  exposure logging      log when a user actually encountered the change, not when
                        they were assigned; analysing on assignment dilutes the
                        effect with users who never saw it

The distinction between assignment and exposure deserves emphasis because it produces a
consistent bias. If a change appears on a page only ten percent of users visit, analysing all
assigned users dilutes the effect by a factor of ten and may render a real effect
undetectable. Analysing only exposed users introduces a different problem, since exposure may
itself be affected by the treatment — a change making a page load faster increases the number
of users who reach it, so the exposed populations differ. The clean approach is to trigger
the analysis on the first point where treatment and control users are still equivalent and
where the treatment becomes relevant, which requires logging that trigger explicitly.

Q: An experiment shows a 2% lift in the primary metric with p = 0.03, and the team wants to
ship. The most important thing to check first is:
A. Whether the effect is larger in any segment
B. Sample ratio mismatch and whether the analysis was pre-specified
C. Whether the p-value would survive a Bonferroni correction
D. The confidence interval width
Reasoning: A sample ratio mismatch means the groups are not comparable and no effect estimate
from the experiment is trustworthy, regardless of significance. Pre-specification matters
because a result selected from many metrics or many analysis choices has a much higher false
positive rate than the nominal p-value suggests. Both are threshold questions that determine
whether the other analyses mean anything. Answer: B.

## Metrics

Metric selection determines what an experimentation programme optimizes toward, and choosing
badly produces a system that reliably improves the wrong thing. The primary metric should be
a single measure directly reflecting the hypothesis, decided before the experiment runs.
Secondary metrics explain the mechanism. Guardrail metrics detect harm the primary metric
would not reveal, and they should be monitored on every experiment regardless of hypothesis.

The tension between short-term and long-term metrics is the central difficulty. Metrics
measurable within an experiment's duration — clicks, sessions, immediate conversion — are
often poor proxies for what the business cares about, which is retention and lifetime value
over months. A change that increases clicks by making content more sensational may reduce
long-term retention, and an experiment lasting two weeks cannot see that. Approaches include
holdback groups maintained for months, surrogate metrics validated against long-term outcomes
on historical data, and explicit degradation experiments that establish the sensitivity of
long-term metrics to short-term ones.

Metric properties worth checking:
  sensitivity          moves enough to detect realistic effects at feasible sample
                       sizes; an insensitive metric is unusable regardless of its
                       relevance
  directionality       an improvement is unambiguously good; metrics where an
                       increase can be good or bad are not decision metrics
  low variance         high variance metrics require enormous samples; the ratio
                       of variance to effect size determines feasibility
  not easily gamed     a metric optimized directly diverges from what it proxied
  computable quickly   available within the experiment window
  guardrails           latency, error rate, unsubscribes, complaints, support
                       contacts; catch harm outside the hypothesis
  count vs ratio       ratio metrics have delta-method variance and cannot be
                       treated as simple means
  capping / winsorizing  heavy-tailed metrics like revenue per user are dominated
                       by outliers; capping reduces variance greatly at a small
                       bias cost

Ratio metrics deserve specific mention because they are analysed incorrectly often. A metric
such as clicks per session, computed as total clicks divided by total sessions, is a ratio of
two random quantities rather than an average over independent units. Treating it as a simple
mean and computing a standard error accordingly understates the variance and produces
false positives. The delta method gives the correct variance, accounting for the covariance
between numerator and denominator, and this correction matters most exactly when the treatment
affects the denominator — which is common, since many changes affect session counts.

Overall evaluation criteria combine several metrics into a single decision quantity by
weighting them. The value is that it forces the tradeoffs to be made explicitly and in advance
rather than argued after results arrive, when the arguing is contaminated by knowing which
metrics moved. The difficulty is agreeing the weights, which is a business decision rather
than a statistical one and is best made by the people who own the outcome.

## Power and Variance Reduction

Statistical power is the probability of detecting an effect that exists, and underpowered
experiments are the most common defect in practice. An underpowered experiment usually fails
to detect a real effect, and worse, on the occasions it does reach significance it
necessarily overstates the magnitude, because only large observed effects clear the threshold
at small sample sizes. Both failures are costly: real improvements are discarded, and the ones
that ship carry inflated expectations.

Sample size is determined by the minimum detectable effect, the metric's variance, the
significance level, and the desired power. The relationship worth internalizing is that
required sample size scales with the inverse square of the detectable effect: halving the
effect you wish to detect requires four times the sample. This is why detecting small effects
is so expensive and why the minimum detectable effect should be set by what would change a
decision rather than by optimism.

Variance reduction is the highest-leverage technique available, because reducing variance is
equivalent to increasing sample size at no cost in traffic or time. The most effective method
is CUPED — using pre-experiment data as a covariate. Each user's outcome is adjusted by their
pre-period behaviour, removing the component of variance attributable to users simply being
heavy or light users, which is typically the dominant source of variance in engagement
metrics.

Power and variance techniques:
  sample size          scales as 1/MDE^2; halving the target effect quadruples
                       the requirement
  CUPED                regress out a pre-experiment covariate; commonly 30-50%
                       variance reduction on engagement metrics, sometimes more
  stratification       block on strong predictors before randomization
  post-stratification  adjust after the fact; nearly as effective and simpler
  capping              limit the influence of extreme values on heavy-tailed
                       metrics
  variance-weighted    weight units by their pre-period variance
  paired designs       where applicable, comparing a unit to itself removes
                       between-unit variance entirely
  longer duration      increases sample but also introduces novelty decay and
                       seasonality
  more traffic         the expensive option; exhaust the others first

Novelty and primacy effects complicate duration decisions. A visible change may show an
inflated effect initially because users notice it and explore, or a depressed effect because
they must relearn a familiar interface. Both decay over days to weeks. Running an experiment
only until significance is reached therefore risks measuring the transient rather than the
steady state, and the diagnostic is to plot the effect over time: an effect trending toward
zero or reversing indicates novelty, and an effect that is flat indicates the measurement is
stable.

[assistant] Not feasible as specified. A 0.5% relative change on a 4% baseline is an absolute

The binding constraint is that sample size scales with the inverse square of the effect size,
so small relative effects on small baselines are extraordinarily expensive. Even a 40%
variance reduction from CUPED only brings this to about 92 weeks.

Three ways forward, and I would push hardest on the first.

Reconsider the minimum detectable effect. The right question is not what change you hope for
but what change would alter your decision. If a 0.5% relative lift would not by itself justify
shipping and maintaining the feature, you do not need to detect it. A 3% relative effect needs
about 4 weeks; a 5% needs about 1.5 weeks. Ask what lift would make this worth doing and size
for that.

Apply variance reduction regardless. CUPED on a pre-period conversion covariate is close to
free and typically gives 30-50% here. Also check whether capping or a more sensitive proxy
metric is available — sometimes a leading indicator with lower variance detects the same
underlying change far more cheaply, provided you have validated that it tracks conversion.

Consider whether conversion is the right primary metric. If the change affects a specific step
in a funnel, measuring that step directly is far more sensitive than measuring end-to-end
conversion, because the intermediate metric has less noise from everything downstream. You
lose the direct business interpretation and gain the ability to detect the effect at all,
which is usually the better trade for an iteration decision.

One thing not to do: run it until it happens to be significant. With a fixed sample size not
reached, peeking inflates your false positive rate substantially — repeatedly checking at
p < 0.05 reaches significance eventually on pure noise. If you need to monitor as it runs,
use sequential testing with always-valid p-values or an alpha-spending schedule.

## Sequential Testing, Interference, and Pitfalls

The peeking problem is that repeatedly testing accumulating data at a fixed threshold
inflates the false positive rate dramatically. A fixed-sample test at the 5 percent level
assumes a single analysis at a predetermined sample size; checking daily and stopping when
significance appears can push the actual false positive rate above 30 percent, because random
walks cross a threshold eventually.

Sequential methods solve this properly. Group sequential designs pre-specify a small number
of interim analyses with adjusted thresholds via an alpha-spending function. Always-valid
inference — sequential probability ratio tests, mixture sequential tests, confidence sequences
— provides bounds valid at every moment, so a practitioner may look continuously and stop
whenever the result is conclusive, at the cost of requiring somewhat more data than a fixed
design for the same power. For an organization where stakeholders will look at dashboards
daily regardless of instruction, always-valid methods are the honest choice.

Interference violates the assumption that one unit's treatment does not affect another's
outcome, and it breaks experiments in specific settings. Marketplace experiments are the
clearest case: giving treated sellers better placement necessarily takes impressions from
control sellers, so the measured difference overstates the effect that would occur if the
change were applied to everyone. Social networks have the same problem through direct
interaction. Shared resources — a cache, a recommendation model trained on both arms, a
fixed inventory or budget — create it too.

Interference and its mitigations:
  marketplace           treated and control compete for the same demand or supply
  network               social interaction between arms
  shared model          a model trained on pooled data learns from both arms
  shared inventory      a fixed pool depleted by both arms
  cluster randomization randomize groups within which interference occurs
  switchback            alternate treatment for the whole system over time
                        intervals; handles marketplace interference at the cost
                        of temporal confounding
  budget-split          allocate separate resource pools per arm
  two-sided designs     randomize both supply and demand
  bias direction        interference usually inflates the measured effect, so a
                        naive result is an upper bound

Several other pitfalls recur often enough to name. Multiple comparisons across many metrics
inflate false positives, and the remedy is pre-specifying a primary metric and applying
false discovery rate control to the rest. Segment mining after the fact — searching
subgroups until one shows an effect — has no error control at all and should be treated as
hypothesis generation requiring a fresh test. Survivorship in the analysis population,
where the treatment affects who appears in the data, breaks comparability. And carryover
between sequential experiments on the same users, particularly when a prior experiment
changed behaviour persistently, contaminates the next.

## Quasi-Experimental Methods and Practice

When randomization is impossible — a change that must apply to everyone, a market-level
intervention, a policy already implemented — quasi-experimental designs exploit structure to
approximate it, with weaker guarantees that must be stated honestly.

Difference-in-differences compares the change over time in a treated group against the change
in an untreated one, differencing out both fixed group differences and common time shocks. It
rests on the parallel trends assumption — that the groups would have moved together absent
treatment — which is untestable for the treatment period and should be supported by showing
parallel pre-period trends. Regression discontinuity exploits a threshold rule, comparing
units just above and below a cutoff, which are plausibly similar in everything but treatment;
it has strong internal validity and applies only near the threshold. Synthetic control
constructs a weighted combination of untreated units matching the treated unit's pre-period
trajectory and is the standard approach for a single treated market. Instrumental variables
use a source of variation affecting treatment but not the outcome directly, and the exclusion
restriction is untestable and usually the weak point.

Practice and organization:
  experiment platform    self-service assignment, logging, and analysis; the
                         infrastructure determines the volume of experimentation
  guardrails by default  every experiment monitors latency, errors, and harm
                         metrics without the team opting in
  automated SRM check    run on every experiment; block analysis on failure
  pre-registration       hypothesis, primary metric, sample size, and analysis
                         plan before launch
  standard analysis      one implementation everyone uses; ad-hoc analysis
                         introduces inconsistent methods and unreviewed choices
  institutional memory   a searchable record of past experiments and outcomes;
                         prevents repeating tests and preserves negative results
  negative results       record them; they are the majority and they are the most
                         useful part of the archive
  effect size culture    report intervals and magnitudes, not binary verdicts
  ship criteria          agreed before results; who decides and on what basis

The organizational point that matters most is that trust in the experimentation system is
its main asset and is easily lost. A platform that produces a result someone can plausibly
dispute — because the sample ratio was off, because the metric definition was ambiguous,
because the analysis was done differently than last time — invites relitigation of every
result, and once teams believe they can argue with the data they will argue with the results
they dislike and accept the ones they like. Automated checks, a single standard analysis, and
pre-specification are what make the output credible enough to act on, and their value is
organizational as much as statistical.
