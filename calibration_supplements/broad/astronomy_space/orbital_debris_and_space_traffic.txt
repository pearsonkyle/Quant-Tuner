## The Debris Environment

Orbital debris is any human-made object in orbit that serves no useful purpose, and the population
spans from intact derelict spacecraft and spent rocket bodies down to paint flecks. The size
distribution is steeply weighted toward small objects, and the hazard distribution is not the same
as the population distribution: large objects are few and are the reservoir that generates future
debris, while small objects are numerous and are what actually strikes operating spacecraft.

Debris originates from several distinct mechanisms. Mission-related objects are released
deliberately during operations — separation hardware, covers, and similar items. Fragmentation
events, whether from collisions, deliberate destruction, or the explosion of residual propellant or
pressurized systems in derelict upper stages, produce the largest single contributions. Surface
degradation releases paint and insulation over years of exposure. And a substantial contribution
comes from a small number of individual events, so the population's history is dominated by
specific incidents rather than by a steady accumulation.

The energy involved makes size a poor guide to danger. Orbital velocities are several kilometres per
second and closing speeds in a collision can approach twice that, so a centimetre-scale fragment
carries kinetic energy comparable to a substantial explosive charge and will destroy a spacecraft.
A millimetre fragment penetrates and damages. Only sub-millimetre particles are routinely survivable,
and the shielding that stops them works by breaking the impactor into a spreading cloud with a thin
outer layer before a spaced inner wall absorbs it.

Q: A spacecraft is shielded against millimetre-scale debris. Why is shielding not the general
solution to the debris problem?
A. Shielding is too expensive
B. Kinetic energy scales with mass, so centimetre-scale objects carry energy comparable to
   explosives at orbital closing speeds; shielding against them would require impractical mass, which
   is why larger objects are tracked and avoided instead
C. Shielding does not work in vacuum
D. Debris is too fast to shield against at any size
Reasoning: Whipple-type shielding is effective and mass-efficient for small particles because it
converts a compact impactor into a diffuse cloud. The energy scales with mass, so an object a factor
of ten larger in each dimension carries a thousand times the energy, and no practical shield
addresses it. The response above that threshold is tracking and manoeuvring. Answer: B.

## Orbit Regimes and Their Behaviour

Debris behaviour depends strongly on altitude, and the regimes are qualitatively different. Low
Earth orbit retains a residual atmosphere whose drag removes objects over time, with the lifetime
ranging from days at the lowest altitudes to centuries at the upper end of the regime. Drag varies
substantially with solar activity, since a more active Sun heats and expands the upper atmosphere,
so a debris object's lifetime depends on where in the solar cycle it spends its time.

Medium altitudes, where navigation constellations operate, have essentially no drag, and objects
there persist for many thousands of years. Geostationary orbit is a unique resource because it is a
single ring at one specific altitude where a satellite remains fixed relative to the ground, and the
slots along it are a finite allocated resource. Objects there also persist indefinitely and drift
along the ring, so a derelict geostationary satellite periodically passes through every occupied slot.

The consequence is that mitigation requirements differ by regime. Low orbit objects can be disposed
of by lowering them into a regime where drag removes them within a bounded period, and the
convention has been twenty-five years with pressure to shorten it substantially. Geostationary
objects cannot be deorbited economically and are instead raised into a graveyard orbit above the
operational ring. Medium altitudes have no cheap disposal option at all, which is an unresolved
problem as constellations there grow.

## Tracking, Catalogues, and Conjunction Assessment

Tracking debris means detecting objects and maintaining orbit estimates for them. Radar dominates in
low orbit, where the range is short enough for returns from small objects, and optical telescopes
dominate at higher altitudes, where objects are detected by reflected sunlight against the night sky.
The detection threshold is roughly ten centimetres in low orbit and considerably larger higher up,
so the catalogued population is a small fraction of the total and statistical models estimate the
rest from sampling observations and returned surfaces.

Orbit determination from tracking produces a state estimate with an associated covariance describing
its uncertainty, and the uncertainty is anisotropic — much larger along the orbit than across it,
because a small error in the orbital period accumulates into a large along-track position error. The
covariance grows with the time since the last observation and shrinks when new observations arrive,
and it depends on how well the object's drag characteristics are known, which for a tumbling
fragment of unknown shape and mass is poorly.

Conjunction assessment propagates catalogued objects forward, identifies close approaches, and
computes a probability of collision from the combined covariance and the objects' sizes. The
resulting probability has a counterintuitive property: it is not monotonic in the uncertainty. A very
large covariance spreads the probability over a wide volume and yields a low collision probability,
so a poorly tracked object can appear safe. Reducing the uncertainty by tasking additional
observations can therefore raise the computed probability, which is the correct behaviour and
confuses everyone the first time they encounter it.

## Collision Physics and the Cascade

A hypervelocity collision between orbiting objects is not a mechanical impact but a shock event.
The materials behave as fluids at the pressures involved, both objects are largely fragmented, and
the resulting cloud of debris spreads along and around the original orbits. A single collision
between two intact objects produces thousands of trackable fragments and vastly more untrackable
ones, and the fragments occupy a range of orbits centred on the collision point.

The cascade concern is that the debris population above a certain density becomes self-sustaining:
collisions generate fragments faster than drag removes them, so the population grows even with no
further launches. The modelling suggests that some altitude bands may already be at or near this
condition, which means that stopping launches would not stabilize the environment there and active
removal would be required.

The number of objects that must be removed to stabilize a regime is smaller than intuition suggests
and the targets are specific. Because large intact derelicts are the reservoir that future collisions
would fragment, removing a handful of the highest-risk objects per year — massive derelict upper
stages in congested bands — has a disproportionate effect on the long-run population. This is a more
tractable problem than removing debris generally, and it is still unsolved because no legal,
financial, or operational mechanism exists to do it.

## Mitigation, Remediation, and Coordination

Mitigation prevents debris creation and is far cheaper than remediation. The established practices
are passivation — venting residual propellant and discharging batteries in derelict stages so they
cannot explode — designing for disposal at end of life, limiting deliberately released hardware,
and demonstrating a high probability of successful disposal. These are guidelines in most
jurisdictions and increasingly conditions of launch licensing, which is the mechanism that gives them
force.

Remediation removes existing debris and remains largely demonstration-stage. The proposed methods
include capture by net, harpoon, or robotic arm followed by a controlled deorbit, drag augmentation
by deploying a large surface, and contactless methods using ion beams or lasers to impart momentum.
Each faces the same difficulties: the targets are tumbling, uncooperative, of uncertain condition,
and legally owned by someone else, and the cost per object removed is high enough that no commercial
model works without a public mechanism.

Space traffic coordination is the operational layer, and it is under-institutionalized relative to
the traffic level. Operators receive conjunction warnings and decide independently whether to
manoeuvre, with no authority resolving cases where both operators might manoeuvre into each other or
where one is unresponsive. Automated collision avoidance in large constellations changes the problem
again, since an operator that manoeuvres frequently and automatically is difficult for others to
predict. The direction of development is toward shared catalogues, standardized data exchange, and
coordination mechanisms, and the gap between the traffic growth rate and the institutional
development rate is the actual risk.
