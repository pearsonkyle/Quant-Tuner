## The Radio Sky

Radio astronomy observes wavelengths from roughly a millimetre to tens of metres, and the reason
this window exists is that the atmosphere is transparent across it while being opaque to most of
the spectrum. The window is bounded below by molecular absorption, particularly water vapour,
which is why millimetre observatories are placed on high dry sites, and above by the ionosphere
reflecting frequencies below its plasma frequency.

What radiates at radio wavelengths differs fundamentally from what radiates optically, which is
why the radio sky looks nothing like the optical one. Thermal emission from stars is negligible at
these wavelengths because the blackbody spectrum falls steeply, so the bright radio sources are
non-thermal: synchrotron emission from relativistic electrons in magnetic fields, which produces
the power-law spectra characteristic of supernova remnants, radio galaxies, and the galactic
plane. The sky's brightest radio sources are largely invisible optically and vice versa.

Spectral lines provide a second and equally important category. The twenty-one centimetre line of
neutral hydrogen arises from a hyperfine transition — a spin flip — which is enormously forbidden
and therefore extremely weak per atom, and hydrogen is abundant enough that it is nonetheless the
most important line in astronomy. It traces neutral gas throughout galaxies, its Doppler shift
gives velocity, and it penetrates dust that blocks optical observation entirely, which is how the
Milky Way's spiral structure was mapped.

The radio sky and emission mechanisms:
  atmospheric window   ~1 mm to tens of metres; bounded by molecular absorption
                       below and the ionosphere above
  synchrotron          relativistic electrons in magnetic fields; power law,
                       polarized; the dominant continuum mechanism
  free-free            thermal bremsstrahlung in ionized gas; HII regions
  thermal dust         rises steeply at millimetre wavelengths; cold dust in star
                       forming regions
  cosmic microwave background  the relic radiation; a 2.725 K blackbody
  21 cm line           neutral hydrogen hyperfine transition; extremely weak per
                       atom and dominant because hydrogen is abundant
  molecular lines      CO as the standard tracer of molecular gas, since molecular
                       hydrogen itself is nearly unobservable
  masers               naturally occurring coherent amplification; extremely
                       bright and compact, and used as distance and dynamical
                       probes
  recombination lines  transitions between very high atomic levels; radio
                       frequencies
  dust penetration     radio passes through dust that blocks optical entirely;

  brightness temperature  the temperature of a blackbody producing the observed
                       intensity; the conventional unit, and it can exceed any
                       physical temperature for non-thermal emission

Q: The 21 cm hydrogen line arises from an extremely forbidden transition with a spontaneous decay
timescale of millions of years. It is nonetheless the most important line in radio astronomy
because:
A. The transition is actually common
B. Hydrogen is so abundant that even an extraordinarily low per-atom rate produces a detectable
   signal from the enormous number of atoms
C. It is amplified by masing
D. It is at a convenient frequency
Reasoning: A single atom would take of order ten million years to make the transition, and a
column of interstellar gas contains a vast number of atoms, so the aggregate emission is readily
detectable. This is a general pattern in astrophysics: forbidden transitions are observable
precisely because the low densities prevent collisional de-excitation and the path lengths are
enormous. Answer: B.

## Single Dish Observing and Sensitivity

A radio telescope's angular resolution is set by diffraction, at approximately the wavelength
divided by the aperture diameter, and because radio wavelengths are a million times longer than
optical ones, a radio dish achieves resolution a million times worse than an optical telescope of
the same size. A hundred-metre dish at twenty-one centimetres resolves about seven arcminutes,
which is worse than the unaided human eye achieves optically. This is the central problem radio
astronomy had to solve.

Sensitivity, by contrast, is excellent and is governed by the radiometer equation. The noise in a
measurement falls as the square root of the product of bandwidth and integration time, so a weak
source is detected by observing longer and over a wider band. The system temperature, combining
the receiver's noise with contributions from the sky, the ground, and the atmosphere, sets the
starting point, and reducing it through cryogenic cooling of the front end is the single most
valuable investment in a receiver.

Antenna properties connect what arrives to what is measured. The effective area is the physical
area times an efficiency accounting for illumination taper, surface accuracy, and blockage. The
beam pattern has a main lobe whose width gives the resolution and sidelobes that admit radiation
from other directions — including from the ground, which is warm and bright, so sidelobe
suppression matters more than it would if the surroundings were cold.

Single dish observing:
  resolution           ~ wavelength over diameter; poor at radio wavelengths
  radiometer equation  noise falls as one over the square root of bandwidth times
                       time
  system temperature   receiver plus sky plus ground plus atmosphere
  cryogenic cooling    reduces receiver noise substantially; the highest-value
                       receiver investment
  effective area       physical area times efficiency
  aperture efficiency  illumination, surface accuracy, blockage
  surface accuracy     errors must be small compared to the wavelength; this is
                       what limits a dish's usable frequency
  beam pattern         main lobe plus sidelobes; sidelobes admit ground radiation
  spillover            the feed seeing past the dish edge to the warm ground
  pointing accuracy    a fraction of the beam width; harder at shorter wavelengths
  gravitational deformation  a large dish deforms as it tilts; homologous designs
                       deform into another paraboloid rather than losing focus
  on-off switching     alternate between source and blank sky to remove the
                       system's own contribution
  focal plane arrays   several feeds mapping simultaneously; multiplies survey
                       speed

The surface accuracy requirement deserves emphasis because it determines a dish's frequency
range. Reflector errors must be a small fraction of the wavelength — conventionally under a
sixteenth — for the reflected wavefronts to add coherently, so a dish with a surface accurate to
half a millimetre works to about a centimetre wavelength and no shorter. This is why millimetre
telescopes are smaller than centimetre ones, why they are enclosed or placed at high altitude
where thermal deformation is manageable, and why active surface control with hundreds of
actuators is used on the largest millimetre dishes.

## Interferometry and Aperture Synthesis

Interferometry solves the resolution problem by combining signals from separated antennas. Two
antennas separated by a baseline produce interference fringes whose pattern encodes information
about the source at an angular scale set by the wavelength divided by the baseline length —
so the resolution is determined by the separation rather than by the individual antenna size. A
kilometre baseline achieves the resolution of a kilometre aperture.

Each baseline measures a single Fourier component of the sky brightness distribution, and this
correspondence is the theoretical foundation. The van Cittert-Zernike theorem establishes that the
correlated signal between two antennas — the visibility — is the Fourier transform of the sky
brightness at a spatial frequency set by the baseline. Measuring many baselines samples many
Fourier components, and inverse transforming gives the image.

Aperture synthesis exploits Earth's rotation to multiply the sampling. As the Earth turns, the
projected baseline between a fixed pair of antennas changes orientation and length relative to the
source, so one pair traces an arc through the Fourier plane over hours. An array of n antennas
provides n times n minus one over two baselines simultaneously, each tracing its own arc, and the
accumulated coverage after a full track approximates that of a filled aperture.

Interferometry:
  baseline             the separation between two antennas; sets the resolution
  visibility           the correlated signal; a Fourier component of the sky
  van Cittert-Zernike  visibility is the Fourier transform of the brightness
                       distribution
  uv plane             the spatial frequency plane sampled by the baselines
  aperture synthesis   Earth rotation sweeps the baselines through the uv plane
  n(n-1)/2 baselines   from n antennas simultaneously
  short baselines      large angular scales; their absence means extended emission
                       is not recovered
  zero spacing problem  an interferometer cannot measure the total flux, since no
                       baseline is zero; combining with single dish data fills it
  long baselines       fine detail
  configuration        compact for sensitivity to extended structure, extended for
                       resolution
  dirty image          the transform of incompletely sampled visibilities;
                       convolved with the array's point spread function
  CLEAN                iterative deconvolution assuming the sky is a collection of
                       point sources; the standard algorithm
  self-calibration     use the source itself to solve for antenna phase errors;
                       transformed the achievable dynamic range
  VLBI                 baselines across continents; microarcsecond resolution,
                       with signals recorded and correlated afterward

Self-calibration deserves particular emphasis because it changed what interferometry could
achieve. Atmospheric fluctuations introduce phase errors that vary per antenna and per moment, and
correcting them from an external calibrator is limited by how quickly the atmosphere changes and
how far the calibrator is. Self-calibration uses the target itself, exploiting the fact that the
number of unknown antenna phases is far smaller than the number of baselines, so the system is
overdetermined and both the image and the phase errors can be solved for iteratively. The dynamic
range achievable improved by orders of magnitude, and it is why modern radio images can show faint
structure adjacent to bright sources.

The zero spacing problem is worth understanding because it produces a characteristic artifact. No
baseline is shorter than the physical separation of two antennas, so the largest angular scales
are never measured, and smooth extended emission is therefore missing from the image. An
interferometer image of an extended source shows its structure while systematically
underrepresenting its total flux, sometimes producing negative regions around it. Combining
interferometer data with single dish observations, which measure exactly the missing large scales,
is the standard remedy.

## Receivers, Calibration, and Interference

A radio receiver amplifies an extraordinarily weak signal without adding much noise of its own,
and the first amplifier dominates the whole chain's noise performance by the Friis relation.
This is why the low-noise amplifier is cryogenically cooled to a few tens of kelvin or below, and
why any loss ahead of it — a lossy cable, a poorly matched connection, a warm component — degrades
the sensitivity directly and cannot be recovered afterward.

The heterodyne architecture mixes the received signal with a local oscillator to shift it to a
lower intermediate frequency where amplification, filtering, and digitization are easier. Direct
sampling at the observing frequency is increasingly practical at lower frequencies as digitizers
improve, and it eliminates a source of instability and spurious signals.

Calibration converts the measured quantity into astronomical units and removes instrumental
effects. Amplitude calibration typically injects a stable noise source of known temperature and
references the measurement to it. Bandpass calibration removes the frequency-dependent response.
Phase calibration for interferometers observes a nearby unresolved source periodically to track
atmospheric and instrumental phase drift. Polarization calibration removes the instrument's own
polarization response, which is essential since the astronomical signals of interest are
frequently only a few percent polarized.

Receivers, calibration, and interference:
  Friis noise relation  the first stage dominates; losses before it are
                       unrecoverable
  cryogenic front end  a few tens of kelvin or below
  heterodyne           mix to a lower intermediate frequency
  direct sampling      digitize at the observing frequency; increasingly practical
  noise diode          a stable injected reference for amplitude calibration
  bandpass calibration  removes the frequency-dependent response
  phase calibration    periodic observation of a nearby unresolved source
  polarization calibration  removes the instrument's own response; essential for
                       signals a few percent polarized
  flux scale           tied to a small number of stable primary calibrators
  radio frequency interference  transmitters, radar, satellites, and unintentional
                       emitters; the field's dominant practical problem
  RFI mitigation       flagging affected data, excision in time and frequency,
                       spatial nulling, and shielding
  radio quiet zones    legal protection around major observatories
  protected bands      allocated to radio astronomy; increasingly encroached upon
  satellite constellations  a growing and largely unmitigated problem, since the
                       transmitters are above the telescopes and cannot be
                       shielded against

Radio frequency interference deserves the practical emphasis because it has become the field's
limiting factor at many frequencies. A radio telescope is designed to detect signals many orders
of magnitude below what any communications receiver handles, so a transmitter that is negligible
by any other standard saturates it completely. The mitigations — flagging, excision, and spatial
nulling — discard data and work only when the interference is intermittent or localized. Satellite
constellations present a qualitatively harder problem because the sources are above the telescope,
so terrestrial shielding and geographic isolation provide no protection, and the affected
frequencies include bands allocated to radio astronomy.

## Pulsars, Transients, and Facilities

Pulsars are rotating neutron stars whose beamed emission sweeps across the line of sight,
producing pulses of extraordinary regularity. Millisecond pulsars, spun up by accretion from a
companion, rival atomic clocks in long-term stability, and this makes them precision instruments
rather than merely interesting objects.

Pulsar timing exploits that stability. Measuring pulse arrival times over years, and fitting a
model accounting for the pulsar's rotation, its motion, the Earth's motion, and the propagation
through the interstellar medium, achieves precision at which small effects become measurable. The
orbital decay of a binary pulsar was the first evidence for gravitational waves, matching general
relativity's prediction to better than a part in a thousand. And a pulsar timing array — many
pulsars monitored simultaneously — is sensitive to nanohertz gravitational waves through the
correlated timing residuals they induce, which is a detection technique using the galaxy as the
instrument.

Dispersion is the propagation effect that both complicates and informs. The interstellar medium is
an ionized plasma whose refractive index depends on frequency, so lower frequencies arrive later.
The delay is proportional to the integrated electron density along the path, so measuring it gives
the dispersion measure — which serves as a distance estimate and, for fast radio bursts,
established that they originate far outside the galaxy.

  pulsar               a rotating beamed neutron star; extraordinary regularity
  millisecond pulsar   spun up by accretion; rivals atomic clocks
  pulsar timing        arrival times over years; precision instrument
  binary pulsar        orbital decay matched gravitational wave emission to
                       better than a part in a thousand
  pulsar timing array  nanohertz gravitational waves from correlated residuals
  dispersion measure   frequency-dependent delay; integrated electron density

  scintillation        interstellar turbulence causing intensity variation; the

  Faraday rotation     magnetized plasma rotating the polarization; measures the

The shift to computational beam forming deserves the closing note because it represents a change
in what a telescope is. A traditional dish points mechanically and observes one direction. A
phased array of simple fixed dipoles has no moving parts, and the beam is formed by combining the
digitized signals with appropriate delays — which means the pointing is a computation, several
beams can be formed simultaneously in different directions, and the whole sky can in principle be
imaged at once. The instrument's capability becomes a function of the available computing rather
than of the mechanical structure, and the constraint shifts from steel to data rate and processing
throughput. For the next generation of arrays the data volume is the defining engineering problem
rather than the collecting area, which inverts the relationship that held for the field's first
seventy years.
