## The Sun and the Solar Wind

The Sun drives essentially every aspect of the near-Earth space environment, and its output has
two components with different characteristics. Electromagnetic radiation is steady to within a
fraction of a percent in total, with far larger variation in the ultraviolet and X-ray bands
that matter for atmospheric heating and ionization. The solar wind is a continuous outflow of
plasma, and it varies enormously in speed, density, and magnetic field.

The solar wind exists because the corona is hot enough — over a million kelvin — that the Sun's
gravity cannot retain it, so it expands outward continuously at speeds of 300 to 800 kilometres
per second. Why the corona is so much hotter than the photosphere beneath it remains an active
problem, with magnetic reconnection and wave heating the leading candidates. The wind carries the
Sun's magnetic field outward, and because the Sun rotates while the wind moves radially, the field
is drawn into a spiral.

The eleven-year solar cycle modulates all of this. Sunspot number, flare frequency, coronal mass
ejection rate, and ultraviolet output all rise and fall together, with the magnetic polarity
reversing each cycle so the full magnetic period is twenty-two years. The consequences for
spacecraft are direct: at solar maximum the upper atmosphere is heated and expands, increasing
drag on low orbits by more than an order of magnitude, and energetic particle events become far
more frequent.

The distinction between flares and coronal mass ejections matters for forecasting because their
arrival times differ by orders of magnitude. A flare's electromagnetic radiation arrives in eight
minutes, so its effects — increased ionization disrupting radio propagation and increased drag —
occur without warning. A coronal mass ejection's plasma takes one to three days, so its arrival
can be predicted once observed, which is what makes space weather forecasting possible at all.
Energetic particles fall between, arriving within tens of minutes and giving little useful
warning to an unshielded system.

Q: A large solar flare is observed. The time available to protect a spacecraft from its direct
electromagnetic effects is:
A. About three days
B. Approximately zero, since the radiation travels at light speed and the observation is the
   arrival
C. About one hour
D. Twelve hours
Reasoning: The flare is observed by the same electromagnetic radiation that constitutes its
direct effect, so the observation and the arrival are simultaneous. Warning is possible only for
the slower components — energetic particles arriving in tens of minutes and the coronal mass
ejection arriving in days. This is why radiation-hardened design matters more than operational
response for flare effects. Answer: B.

## Magnetosphere and Radiation Belts

Earth's magnetic field deflects the solar wind, carving a cavity called the magnetosphere. The
boundary on the sunward side, the magnetopause, sits where the field's magnetic pressure balances
the solar wind's dynamic pressure, typically around ten Earth radii and compressible to much less
during a strong event. A bow shock forms upstream where the supersonic wind is decelerated. On
the night side the field is drawn out into a long tail extending well beyond lunar distance.

Magnetic reconnection is the process coupling the solar wind to the magnetosphere. When the
interplanetary field has a southward component, opposite to Earth's field at the dayside, the
field lines merge and reconnect, opening a path for solar wind energy and particles to enter.
This is why the orientation of the interplanetary field is the primary determinant of geomagnetic
activity, and why a fast coronal mass ejection with a northward field can pass with little effect
while a slower one with a southward field produces a major storm.

The radiation belts are regions of trapped energetic particles held by the magnetic field. The
inner belt, from roughly 1,000 to 6,000 kilometres, contains high-energy protons and is
relatively stable. The outer belt, from roughly 13,000 to 60,000 kilometres, contains energetic
electrons and varies by orders of magnitude on timescales of hours during storms. Both are
consequences of the same trapping mechanism: charged particles spiral along field lines, bounce
between mirror points near the poles where the field is stronger, and drift slowly around the
Earth.

The South Atlantic Anomaly deserves specific attention because it dominates the radiation
environment for low Earth orbit. Earth's magnetic field is offset and tilted relative to its
rotation axis, so over the South Atlantic the field is weaker and the inner radiation belt dips
to altitudes where spacecraft operate. A satellite in low orbit receives most of its total
radiation dose and experiences most of its single event upsets during the several passes per day
through this region, which is why anomaly counters correlate so strongly with position and why
some instruments are routinely powered down during passes.

## Space Weather and Its Effects

Geomagnetic storms occur when a coronal mass ejection or a high-speed stream couples efficiently
to the magnetosphere, driving currents, energizing particles, and heating the upper atmosphere.
The effects propagate to systems both in space and on the ground, and the ground effects are the
ones with the largest economic exposure.

Geomagnetically induced currents are the mechanism for ground effects. A changing magnetic field
induces an electric field in the conducting Earth, which drives currents through any long
conductor connected to it — power transmission lines, pipelines, railway signalling, and
communication cables. In power grids these quasi-direct currents saturate transformer cores,
causing overheating, harmonic distortion, and in severe cases transformer failure. The 1989
Quebec event blacked out a province in ninety seconds, and the 1859 Carrington event, far larger,
occurred before electrical infrastructure existed.

The satellite effects divide by mechanism. Increased atmospheric density during a storm raises
drag on low orbits, which decays orbits faster and degrades the accuracy of conjunction
predictions — a storm in early 2022 caused the loss of dozens of newly launched satellites that
had not yet raised their orbits. Surface and internal charging deposit charge that discharges
destructively. Single event effects from energetic particles upset or damage electronics. And
increased ionospheric variability degrades navigation and radio propagation.

Deep dielectric charging deserves emphasis because it defeats the intuitive protection.
Surface charging is addressed by making surfaces conductive and grounding them, which equalizes
potentials. Energetic electrons of several megaelectronvolts penetrate through the structure and
deposit inside insulating materials — cable dielectrics, circuit board substrate, potting
compound — where no surface treatment reaches. Charge accumulates until the local field exceeds
the breakdown strength, producing an internal discharge that couples into nearby circuits. The
mitigations are limiting the electron flux by shielding, ensuring that insulators have a
conductive path to bleed charge, and avoiding ungrounded conductors buried within insulation.

## Vacuum, Thermal, and Debris

The vacuum environment produces effects that have no terrestrial equivalent. Outgassing releases
volatile species from materials, and those species condense on cold surfaces — preferentially on
optics and on thermal control surfaces, which are the coldest and the most sensitive.
Contamination requirements therefore constrain material selection, and standards specify limits
on total mass loss and on collected volatile condensable material.

Atomic oxygen is the dominant erosion mechanism in low Earth orbit. The residual atmosphere at
these altitudes is mostly atomic rather than molecular oxygen, and the spacecraft's orbital
velocity gives each impact several electronvolts of energy, which is sufficient to break chemical
bonds. Polymers and carbon erode measurably over a mission, coatings degrade, and the ram-facing
surfaces are affected far more than others. Protective coatings and material selection are the
mitigations.

Orbital debris has become a primary design consideration. Objects in orbit collide at relative
velocities of up to fifteen kilometres per second, at which a one-centimetre fragment carries the
kinetic energy of a small car at highway speed. Millimetre-scale debris is too small to track and
numerous enough to be a real risk, addressed by shielding; centimetre-scale is largely untracked
and unshieldable; larger objects are tracked and avoided by manoeuvre.

The Kessler syndrome deserves the closing emphasis because it describes a threshold effect
rather than a gradual degradation. Each collision generates fragments, each fragment can cause
further collisions, and above a critical debris density the collision rate becomes
self-sustaining regardless of whether further objects are launched. Certain altitude bands are
approaching or within that regime by some estimates. The mitigations are not
optional-with-consequences but genuinely preventive: reliable post-mission disposal, passivation
to prevent explosive fragmentation, collision avoidance, and limiting the release of mission-
related objects. The difficulty is that the incentives are diffuse — every operator bears a share
of the cost of debris generated by any of them — which makes it a commons problem requiring
coordination rather than a technical problem requiring a solution.
