## The Two-Body Problem

Two point masses under mutual gravitation reduce exactly to a one-body problem: the
centre of mass moves uniformly, and the relative position obeys r'' = -mu r / |r|^3
with mu = G(M + m), the standard gravitational parameter. For a spacecraft around a
planet the spacecraft's mass is negligible and mu is a property of the central body
alone, known far more precisely than either G or M individually — which is why mission
software uses tabulated mu values rather than computing GM.

The solution is a conic section with the central body at one focus, r = p/(1 + e cos
nu), where p is the semi-latus rectum, e the eccentricity, and nu the true anomaly
measured from periapsis. Eccentricity classifies the orbit: e = 0 is a circle, 0 < e <
1 an ellipse, e = 1 a parabola, and e > 1 a hyperbola. This is Kepler's first law,
derived rather than observed, and it holds only for an inverse-square force — any other
power law gives orbits that precess rather than closing.

Two conserved quantities do most of the work. Specific angular momentum h = r cross v
is constant because gravity is central, which immediately gives Kepler's second law:
the radius sweeps equal areas in equal times, so a body moves fastest at periapsis and
slowest at apoapsis. Specific orbital energy is the vis-viva quantity, and combining it
with the geometry yields the single most-used equation in the subject.

Core two-body relations:
  vis-viva            v^2 = mu (2/r - 1/a)
  specific energy     E = v^2/2 - mu/r = -mu/(2a)
  circular speed      v_c = sqrt(mu / r)
  escape speed        v_esc = sqrt(2 mu / r) = sqrt(2) * v_c
  period              T = 2 pi sqrt(a^3 / mu)          Kepler's third law
  periapsis radius    r_p = a(1 - e)
  apoapsis radius     r_a = a(1 + e)
  semi-major axis     a = (r_p + r_a)/2
  angular momentum    h = sqrt(mu a (1 - e^2)) = r v cos(flight path angle)

The vis-viva equation deserves particular emphasis because nearly every maneuver
calculation reduces to applying it twice and differencing. It says speed depends only
on current radius and semi-major axis — not on eccentricity, not on where you are in
the orbit except through r. Note what the energy expression implies: total energy
depends only on a. Two orbits with wildly different eccentricities but the same
semi-major axis have identical energy and identical period, which is the key to
understanding why raising apoapsis and raising periapsis cost the same energy for the
same change in a.

Kepler's equation, M = E - e sin E, relates mean anomaly (which advances linearly in
time) to eccentric anomaly (which is geometric). It is transcendental and has no closed
form, so propagating a position to a future time requires iteration — Newton's method
converges quickly except near e = 1, where the derivative degenerates and specialized
formulations are used. This small transcendental obstacle is why orbit propagation is a
numerical rather than an algebraic exercise, and it is the historical origin of a great
deal of numerical analysis.

Q: Two satellites orbit Earth with the same semi-major axis, one nearly circular and
one highly eccentric. Compared to each other, their orbital periods are:
A. Equal
B. Shorter for the eccentric one
C. Longer for the eccentric one
D. Dependent on their masses
Reasoning: Kepler's third law gives T = 2 pi sqrt(a^3/mu), which depends only on the
semi-major axis. Eccentricity affects the distribution of speed around the orbit but
not the period, and satellite mass is negligible. Answer: A.

Q: A spacecraft in a circular orbit fires prograde briefly. Immediately afterward:
A. The whole orbit expands uniformly
B. The burn point becomes periapsis and the opposite side rises to a new apoapsis
C. The orbit becomes circular at a larger radius
D. The period is unchanged
Reasoning: An impulsive burn changes velocity but not position, so the burn point
remains on the new orbit at the same radius. Since speed increased there, that point is
now the fastest point, which is periapsis, and the orbit rises to a higher apoapsis
half a revolution later. Energy increased so a increased and the period lengthened.
Answer: B.

## Orbital Elements and Reference Frames

Six numbers specify an orbit and a position on it. Semi-major axis a gives size and
energy. Eccentricity e gives shape. Inclination i is the tilt of the orbital plane
relative to the reference plane — the equator for Earth orbits, the ecliptic for
heliocentric ones. Right ascension of the ascending node, capital Omega, orients the
line where the orbit crosses the reference plane going north. Argument of periapsis,
omega, locates periapsis within the orbital plane measured from that node. True anomaly
nu locates the spacecraft along the orbit at a given epoch.

The parameterization degenerates in exactly the cases that occur most often in practice.
For a circular orbit periapsis is undefined, so omega and nu are individually
meaningless while their sum, the argument of latitude, remains well defined. For an
equatorial orbit the ascending node is undefined, so Omega is meaningless while the
longitude of periapsis, Omega plus omega, survives. Since the most common orbits are
near-circular and many are near-equatorial, production software uses equinoctial
elements, a nonsingular reparameterization that avoids these degeneracies entirely.

Inclination determines coverage and cost. A launch from latitude phi cannot directly
reach an inclination below phi without a plane change, since the launch site's
latitude sets the maximum latitude of the resulting ground track. Launching due east
from the equator gives an equatorial orbit and captures the full 465 m/s of Earth's
rotational speed as a free contribution; launching from Kennedy Space Center at 28.5
degrees latitude gives 28.5 degrees inclination minimum and about 409 m/s of assist;
polar and sun-synchronous launches get essentially none and pay a real performance
penalty.

Common Earth orbit regimes:
  LEO          160-2000 km; short periods (~90 min), high drag, cheap access
  SSO          ~600-800 km, i ~98 deg, retrograde; J2 precession matches Earth's
               orbital rate so the local solar time of the ground track is fixed
  MEO          ~20,200 km, 12 h period; the GNSS constellation altitude
  GEO          35,786 km, i = 0, period exactly one sidereal day; appears fixed
               over one longitude
  GTO          elliptical transfer with apogee at GEO altitude
  Molniya      a ~26,600 km, e ~0.74, i = 63.4 deg; the critical inclination zeroes
               apsidal precession, giving long dwell over high northern latitudes
  HEO/Lunar    beyond GEO; three-body effects become significant

The sidereal day distinction matters for GEO and is a frequent error. A geostationary
satellite matches Earth's rotation relative to the stars, 23 h 56 m 4 s, not the 24 h
solar day. Using the solar day gives a radius error of a few tens of kilometres and a
satellite that drifts steadily in longitude — small in relative terms, operationally
disqualifying.

## Maneuvers and Transfers

An impulsive maneuver idealizes a burn as an instantaneous velocity change, valid when
the burn duration is short compared with the orbital period. The delta-v budget is the
currency of mission design because the rocket equation makes it exponentially
expensive: delta_v = v_e ln(m_0/m_f), so propellant mass fraction grows exponentially
with required delta-v. Exhaust velocity v_e equals I_sp times standard gravity, which
is why specific impulse dominates propulsion selection — chemical bipropellants reach
around 450 s in vacuum, ion thrusters exceed 3,000 s, and the difference reshapes what
missions are possible.

The Hohmann transfer moves between coplanar circular orbits using two burns and is
minimum-delta-v for radius ratios below about 11.94. Burn prograde at the inner orbit
to raise apoapsis to the target radius, coast half an ellipse, then burn prograde again
at apoapsis to circularize. Above that ratio the bi-elliptic transfer wins: burn to an
apoapsis far beyond the target, perform a cheap plane-and-periapsis adjustment where
speed is low, then drop back. The bi-elliptic saves delta-v at the cost of enormously
longer transfer time, which is usually the reason it is not used.

Maneuver formulas:
  Hohmann, r1 -> r2
    dv1 = sqrt(mu/r1) * (sqrt(2 r2/(r1+r2)) - 1)
    dv2 = sqrt(mu/r2) * (1 - sqrt(2 r1/(r1+r2)))
    transfer time = pi * sqrt(((r1+r2)/2)^3 / mu)
  plane change, angle theta at speed v
    dv = 2 v sin(theta/2)
  combined plane change and speed change
    dv = sqrt(v1^2 + v2^2 - 2 v1 v2 cos theta)
  circularization at apoapsis
    dv = v_circular(r_a) - v_apoapsis
  rocket equation
    dv = I_sp * g0 * ln(m_0 / m_f)

Plane changes are brutally expensive and the formula shows why: the cost is
proportional to the orbital speed at which you perform it. A 30-degree plane change in
LEO at 7.7 km/s costs about 4 km/s, comparable to reaching orbit in the first place.
Three mitigations follow directly. Perform plane changes at apoapsis where speed is
lowest, which is why GTO-to-GEO burns combine circularization with the inclination
change in a single vector sum rather than doing them separately. Combine the maneuvers,
since the vector sum is always less than the scalar sum. Or exploit natural
perturbations — differential nodal regression from J2 can rotate the plane for free
given enough time, which is how constellations phase their planes.

Orbital rendezvous is counterintuitive because thrusting toward a target does not close
the distance. To catch a target ahead of you in the same orbit, you must slow down: a
retrograde burn lowers your orbit, which shortens your period, so you gain angular
position each revolution and rise to meet the target later. Speeding up sends you to a
higher, slower orbit and you fall behind. The Clohessy-Wiltshire equations linearize
relative motion in a rotating frame near a circular reference orbit, producing the
characteristic elliptical relative trajectories that govern close-proximity operations
and docking.

## Perturbations

The two-body solution is an idealization. Real orbits evolve under perturbing
accelerations, and which ones matter depends entirely on altitude.

Earth's oblateness is the dominant perturbation for most Earth orbits. The equatorial
bulge, quantified by the J2 zonal harmonic of about 1.083e-3, produces a torque that
causes two secular effects. The orbital plane regresses, with the node moving westward
for prograde orbits at a rate proportional to cos(i), and the argument of periapsis
rotates within the plane at a rate proportional to (5 cos^2 i - 1). Both are exploited
rather than merely tolerated. Sun-synchronous orbits choose a retrograde inclination
near 98 degrees so the nodal regression is +0.9856 degrees per day, exactly matching
Earth's mean motion about the Sun, which fixes the local solar time of every equator
crossing and gives consistent lighting for imaging. Molniya orbits choose the critical
inclination of 63.4 degrees, where 5 cos^2 i - 1 vanishes and apsidal rotation stops,
so apogee stays over the northern hemisphere indefinitely.

Atmospheric drag dominates below about 600 km and is the least predictable
perturbation, because upper-atmosphere density varies by more than an order of
magnitude with solar activity over the eleven-year cycle and by large factors within a
single geomagnetic storm. Drag removes energy, shrinking the semi-major axis; because
lower orbits are faster, the paradoxical result is that drag *speeds the satellite up*
while removing energy from the orbit. Decay is self-accelerating, since a lower orbit
encounters denser air, so a satellite's altitude declines slowly for years and then
reenters over days. Ballistic coefficient, mass over drag area times drag coefficient,
determines the rate, which is why deployable drag sails are a viable passive
deorbit mechanism.

Perturbation magnitudes and where they matter:
  J2 oblateness          dominant in LEO through GEO; secular node and apsidal drift
  higher zonals, tesseral J3, J22 and beyond; J22 drives GEO longitude drift toward
                         two stable points at 75 E and 105 W
  atmospheric drag       dominant below ~600 km; negligible above ~1000 km
  lunisolar gravity      significant above ~15,000 km; drives GEO inclination growth
                         of about 0.85 deg/year, requiring north-south stationkeeping
  solar radiation pressure  matters for high area-to-mass ratios; dominant for
                         solar sails, significant for large GEO satellites
  relativistic           tiny but real; GPS clocks require both special and general
                         relativistic corrections totalling +38 microseconds/day
  tidal / non-gravitational  thermal recoil, outgassing; the Pioneer anomaly's
                         eventual explanation

The GPS relativistic correction is a useful concrete case. Satellite clocks run slow by
about 7 microseconds per day from special-relativistic time dilation at orbital speed,
and fast by about 45 microseconds per day from the weaker gravitational potential at
20,200 km altitude, netting +38 microseconds per day. Uncorrected, this accumulates to
a positioning error of about 10 kilometres per day. The clocks are deliberately offset
in frequency before launch to compensate, making GNSS the most widely deployed
practical application of general relativity.

Stationkeeping budgets follow from these perturbations. A GEO satellite spends roughly
50 m/s per year on north-south stationkeeping to fight lunisolar inclination growth,
and only 2-5 m/s per year on east-west to counter the J22 longitude drift. Since
north-south is an order of magnitude more expensive, satellites near end of life are
often allowed to drift in inclination — inclined-orbit operation — trading ground
antenna tracking complexity for years of additional service.

## Three-Body Effects and Interplanetary Trajectories

The three-body problem has no general closed-form solution, but the restricted circular
case, where a negligible mass moves in the field of two bodies in circular mutual
orbit, admits five equilibrium points in the rotating frame. L1, L2, and L3 lie on the
line through the two primaries and are saddle points — unstable, requiring
stationkeeping of roughly 1-10 m/s per year, but the instability is gentle and the
associated halo and Lissajous orbits are practical. L4 and L5 lead and trail the
smaller primary by 60 degrees and are genuinely stable for mass ratios below about
1/24.96, which is why Jupiter's Trojan asteroids accumulate there and why Earth has
captured at least one Trojan.

The Sun-Earth Lagrange points are prime observatory real estate. Sun-Earth L1 gives an
uninterrupted view of the Sun and is where solar monitors sit. Sun-Earth L2, 1.5
million kilometres anti-sunward, lets a spacecraft shield Sun, Earth, and Moon behind a
single sunshade while maintaining a constant thermal environment and continuous
downlink, which is why the major infrared and microwave observatories are placed there.
Earth-Moon L2, beyond the far side, provides continuous communication relay for far-side
lunar surface operations.

Interplanetary trajectory design uses the patched-conic approximation: treat the
trajectory as a sequence of two-body arcs, heliocentric between planets and planetocentric
inside each planet's sphere of influence, matching velocity at the boundaries. The
sphere of influence radius scales as a(m/M)^{2/5}, about 925,000 km for Earth. The
approximation is crude — it discontinuously switches the central body — but it is
accurate enough for mission architecture and provides the initial guess for the
high-fidelity numerical optimization that follows.

Interplanetary quantities:
  hyperbolic excess speed   v_inf; speed relative to the planet at infinity
  characteristic energy     C3 = v_inf^2; the standard launch performance metric
  sphere of influence       r_SOI = a (m_planet / M_sun)^{2/5}
  gravity assist turn angle sin(delta/2) = 1/(1 + r_p v_inf^2 / mu_planet)
  max heliocentric dv       2 v_inf from a single flyby
  synodic period            1/T_syn = |1/T_1 - 1/T_2|
  Earth-Mars synodic        ~25.6 months between launch windows
  Hohmann Earth-Mars        ~5.6 km/s C3, ~259 day transfer

Gravity assists exchange momentum with a planet. In the planet's frame the flyby is a
hyperbola and only rotates the velocity vector, preserving its magnitude; in the Sun's
frame that rotation changes the heliocentric speed, and the planet gains or loses an
utterly negligible compensating amount. The maximum gain from one encounter is 2 v_inf,
achieved for a 180-degree turn, which requires a low v_inf and a close approach at a
massive planet. This is what makes outer-planet missions feasible at all — Voyager 2's
Jupiter-Saturn-Uranus-Neptune sequence would have been impossible with chemical
propulsion alone, and Cassini used Venus twice, Earth, and Jupiter to reach Saturn.

Launch windows follow from synodic periods. Earth-Mars transfer opportunities recur
every 25.6 months, and because Mars' orbit is appreciably eccentric, the delta-v cost
varies substantially between windows on a roughly 15-year cycle. A mission that misses
its window waits over two years, which is why interplanetary launch campaigns operate
under schedule pressure that has no equivalent in Earth-orbital work, and why launch
periods are designed with daily windows spanning several weeks with a corresponding
performance margin.
