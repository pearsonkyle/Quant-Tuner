## Operations Concepts

Mission operations is the discipline of flying a spacecraft after launch, and it is shaped by
constraints that have no equivalent in terrestrial systems. The vehicle cannot be repaired.
Communication is intermittent, delayed, and bandwidth-limited. Every command is irreversible in
the sense that an incorrect one may consume propellant, damage hardware, or place the vehicle in
a state from which recovery is difficult. And the people operating it were frequently not the
people who designed it.

The concept of operations is the document that defines how the mission will be flown, and it
should be written during design rather than after, because it drives requirements. How often
will the spacecraft be contacted, and therefore what onboard autonomy is needed between
contacts? What happens when a contact is missed? How much data will be produced and can it be
downlinked? How long may the vehicle remain in a safe mode before the mission is jeopardized?
Each of these is an operations question with hardware consequences, and a design finalized
without answering them produces a spacecraft that is expensive or impossible to operate.

Autonomy versus ground control is the central architectural tradeoff. Ground control keeps
decisions with humans who have full context and requires contact, which for a low Earth orbit
satellite with a single ground station may be four short passes per day. Onboard autonomy
handles situations between contacts and requires that the fault detection logic be correct,
since an autonomous response to a misdiagnosed condition can be worse than no response. The
practical resolution in most missions is autonomy for time-critical safety responses and ground
control for everything else.

Light time changes the character of operations fundamentally as distance increases. A low
Earth orbit satellite can be commanded interactively, with an operator observing the response
within a second. A Mars mission has a round-trip delay of eight to forty-eight minutes, so no
interactive control is possible and everything must be sequenced in advance with onboard logic
handling contingencies. An outer planet mission plans days ahead. This is why deep space
missions carry far more onboard autonomy than their computing hardware would suggest is
comfortable, and why entry, descent, and landing at Mars is entirely autonomous — the vehicle
has landed or crashed before the ground learns it began.

Q: A Mars lander is executing entry, descent, and landing while Earth is 12 light-minutes
away. The ground's role during this sequence is:
A. To send corrective commands as needed
B. To monitor only; the entire sequence must be autonomous because any command would arrive
   long after the outcome
C. To abort if telemetry indicates a problem
D. To control the parachute deployment
Reasoning: The entire sequence lasts a few minutes and the one-way light time is twelve, so
the vehicle has completed the sequence before the first telemetry from its beginning reaches
Earth. Every decision must be made onboard from onboard sensing. This is why entry, descent,
and landing is the highest-risk phase of any Mars mission and why it is validated so heavily
in simulation. Answer: B.

## The Ground Segment

The ground segment comprises the antennas that communicate with the spacecraft, the networks
carrying data to and from them, the control centre where operations are conducted, and the
systems that process and distribute the resulting data.

Ground stations are located to provide the coverage the orbit requires. A polar-orbiting
satellite is visible from a high-latitude station on nearly every orbit, which is why such
stations are disproportionately valuable. A geostationary satellite is permanently visible from
a station within its footprint. Deep space missions require large antennas at widely separated
longitudes so that at least one always faces the target, which is the architecture of the
international deep space networks with their complexes spaced roughly 120 degrees apart.

Link budget determines what data rate is achievable, and it is computed as a sum of gains and
losses in decibels from transmitter to receiver. Free-space path loss dominates and grows with
the square of distance, which is why data rates fall so sharply with range — a mission at
Jupiter has roughly a thousandth of the received power of an identical one at Mars at
comparable geometry. The available margin above the minimum required signal-to-noise ratio is
what permits operation through rain, at low elevation angles, and with degraded equipment.

  ground station        antenna, transmitter, receiver, tracking

The spacecraft simulator deserves emphasis because it is what makes safe operations possible.
Every command sequence of consequence is executed against a model of the spacecraft before it
is transmitted, and the model's fidelity determines what classes of error are caught.
Constraint violations, resource conflicts, and state-dependent command rejections are all found
this way. The recurring risk is that the simulator's model diverges from the actual spacecraft
as the vehicle ages, as parameters are updated, or as onboard software is patched, so keeping
the simulator synchronized with flight is an ongoing operations task rather than a
pre-launch one.

## Telemetry, Commanding, and Flight Dynamics

Telemetry is the stream of measurements from the spacecraft describing its state:
temperatures, voltages, currents, pressures, attitude, mode, memory contents, and instrument
status. It is packetized, often compressed, and typically prioritized so that critical
housekeeping data is transmitted even when bandwidth is constrained. Limit checking flags
values outside expected ranges, and the operations team's routine work is largely reviewing
trends rather than instantaneous values, because a parameter drifting slowly toward a limit is
far more informative than one currently within it.

Commanding sends instructions to the spacecraft, and the safeguards reflect the irreversibility
of the medium. Commands are validated against the spacecraft's state and against constraints
before transmission. Critical commands require two-step arm-and-execute sequences so that a
single erroneous transmission cannot trigger them. Command authentication prevents unauthorized
or corrupted commands from being accepted. And the ground maintains a model of the expected
onboard state so that a command's preconditions can be checked, with verification that the
command was received and executed as expected.

Flight dynamics determines and predicts where the spacecraft is and where it is pointing.
Orbit determination fits a trajectory to tracking measurements — range, range rate, and angles
— producing a state estimate and its uncertainty. Orbit propagation predicts forward, requiring
a force model including gravity harmonics, atmospheric drag, solar radiation pressure, and
third-body effects, with the accuracy limited by the least well modelled of these. For low
orbits that is atmospheric density, which varies by more than an order of magnitude with solar
activity and is the reason low-orbit predictions degrade rapidly.

Conjunction assessment has become a routine operational burden as the orbital population has
grown. Screening predicted trajectories against a catalogue identifies close approaches, and the
decision to manoeuvre rests on the estimated probability of collision rather than on the miss
distance, because a large miss distance with large uncertainty can carry more risk than a small
one that is precisely known. The counterintuitive consequence is that improving tracking
accuracy can raise the computed collision probability, since a shrinking uncertainty region that
still contains the other object concentrates the probability rather than dispersing it.

## Anomaly Response and Planning

Anomalies are inevitable and the response is procedural rather than improvised. The
first priority is spacecraft safety: place the vehicle in a stable configuration, which
frequently means allowing or commanding entry into safe mode, before attempting diagnosis. The
second is preserving evidence, which means capturing telemetry and avoiding changes that
destroy the state needed to understand what happened. Only then does diagnosis proceed, and
recovery follows a reviewed plan rather than an operator's judgment in the moment.

Safe mode is the designed response to an unrecognized condition. The spacecraft orients its
solar arrays to the Sun, points its antenna toward Earth or begins a search, sheds non-essential
loads, and waits. It is deliberately simple and heavily tested, because it is the state the
vehicle must be able to reach from any condition. Missions have been saved by a well-designed
safe mode and lost by one that made an assumption which did not hold in the actual failure.

Fault management design is the counterpart to this on the spacecraft. The detection logic must
distinguish genuine faults from transient sensor noise, since responding to noise causes
unnecessary safe mode entries that cost mission time. The isolation logic must correctly
identify which component failed, since responding to the wrong one may disable a healthy unit
while leaving the failed one active. And the recovery action must be safe under a misdiagnosis,
which is why conservative responses that preserve the vehicle are preferred over clever ones
that attempt to continue the mission.

Anomaly and planning practice:
  safety first          stabilize before diagnosing
  preserve evidence     capture telemetry; avoid destroying the failure state
  anomaly review board  a structured process for diagnosis and recovery approval
  fault tree            systematic enumeration of possible causes
  reproduce in simulation  confirm the diagnosis before committing to a recovery
  recovery procedure    written, reviewed, and simulated before uplink
  lessons learned       fed back into procedures and into future designs
  planning cycle        science requests, constraint checking, sequence generation,
                        validation, uplink
  constraints           power, thermal, data volume, pointing, contact windows,
                        instrument duty cycles, propellant
  oversubscription      requests routinely exceed capacity; prioritization is a

                        for complex missions

The planning cycle is where the mission's science return is actually determined, and it is
fundamentally a constrained optimization. Requests for observations exceed what the spacecraft
can execute, and the constraints — power available in this orbit, thermal limits on how long an
instrument may operate, onboard storage before the next downlink, slew time between targets,
keep-out zones protecting sensitive instruments from the Sun — interact in ways that make
manual planning infeasible for complex missions. Automated planners handle this, and the
operational skill is in expressing the constraints correctly, since a missing constraint
produces a plan that violates it and a spurious one silently discards achievable science.

[assistant] The events cluster at eclipse exit over the South Atlantic, which is a strong
enough correlation to work with — 0.91 with orbit position and 0.88 with eclipse exit is not
coincidence across nine events.

Three hypotheses fit that pattern, and they are distinguishable from data you likely already
have.

Radiation upset in the South Atlantic Anomaly is the first. That region has elevated trapped
particle flux at low altitude, and single event upsets in the attitude determination processor
or its memory would produce exactly this: attitude error spikes clustered geographically. Check
whether your error-correcting memory is logging corrected errors, and whether the upset counter
increments during these passes. If it does, the fix is architectural — memory scrubbing at
higher frequency, and treating a single attitude error sample as insufficient to trigger safe
mode.

Sun sensor blinding at the terminator is the second and would explain the eclipse-exit
correlation specifically. Emerging from eclipse, the sensor sees a rapidly changing
illumination geometry, and earth albedo near the terminator can produce a spurious sun vector.
If your attitude determination weights the sun sensor heavily, a bad vector produces a large
apparent attitude error. Check the sun sensor's raw output during these events, and whether the
error persists or resolves within seconds.

Thermal transient is the third. Eclipse exit produces a rapid temperature change, and if a
structural element or the sensor mount has a thermal gradient, the sensor's alignment shifts.
This would show as an attitude error that correlates with temperature rate of change rather
than with position, which distinguishes it from the first two.

Regardless of which it is, one change is worth making now: your safe mode trigger appears to
fire on a threshold crossing. Requiring the error to persist for several consecutive samples
before triggering would reject transients while still catching genuine attitude loss, and it
would likely stop the safe mode entries even before you identify the cause. Verify the
persistence duration against how fast a real attitude excursion would develop, so you do not
delay a genuine response.

## Mission Phases and Data

Missions proceed through phases with distinct operational characteristics. Launch and early
orbit is the highest-risk period, with continuous coverage, a compressed sequence of critical
events — separation, attitude acquisition, solar array deployment, first contact — and a team
prepared for immediate response. Commissioning checks out each subsystem and instrument
progressively, calibrating and characterizing against pre-launch expectations. Routine
operations follows an established cadence with reduced staffing. Extended mission continues
past the primary objectives with an ageing vehicle and typically reduced resources. Disposal
concludes the mission.

Disposal is a requirement rather than an option in current practice. Low orbit satellites must
deorbit or be placed in an orbit that decays within a specified period, now widely being reduced
from twenty-five years toward five. Geostationary satellites are boosted to a graveyard orbit
above the operational belt, which requires reserving propellant that could otherwise extend the
mission — a real tension since the disposal manoeuvre must be performed while the vehicle is
still controllable, so it is executed before the propellant is exhausted rather than after.

Data processing converts downlinked telemetry into usable products through defined levels.
Raw data is reconstructed and time-tagged. Calibrated data has instrument characteristics
removed and physical units applied. Derived products apply geophysical or scientific
algorithms. Each level is archived, because reprocessing with improved calibration is routine
and requires the lower levels to be preserved.

Mission phases and data:
  launch and early orbit  highest risk; continuous coverage, critical sequence
  commissioning           subsystem and instrument checkout and calibration
  routine operations      established cadence, reduced staffing
  extended mission        ageing vehicle, reduced resources, often the highest
                          scientific return per dollar
  disposal                deorbit or graveyard; propellant must be reserved

  Level 1                 calibrated, physical units, geolocated

The archive deserves the final word because it is the mission product that persists longest.
Spacecraft operate for years and their data is used for decades, which means the format,
metadata, and documentation determine whether the investment remains accessible. Data archived
in an undocumented binary format with the calibration knowledge held only by the original team
becomes unusable within a decade of that team dispersing. Standard formats, self-describing
metadata, documented processing provenance, and archived processing software are what make
long-term reuse possible, and they are consistently underfunded relative to their contribution
to the mission's eventual scientific return.
