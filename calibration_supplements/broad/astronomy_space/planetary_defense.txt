## Small Body Populations

The solar system contains an enormous population of small bodies left over from its formation, and
their distribution reflects the dynamical history that placed them. The main asteroid belt between
Mars and Jupiter holds the majority of catalogued asteroids, sculpted by Jupiter's gravity into
families and gaps at resonant orbital periods. The Kuiper belt beyond Neptune holds icy bodies, and
the Oort cloud far beyond it is the inferred source of long-period comets.

Near-Earth objects are those whose orbits bring them close to Earth's, and they are not a separate
population but main-belt asteroids and extinct comets that have been perturbed into new orbits.
Resonances with Jupiter act as escape routes, delivering material inward over millions of years,
which means the near-Earth population is continuously replenished and any given object has been in
its current orbit for a geologically short time.

Composition varies systematically and matters for both science and deflection. Stony objects are the
most numerous among near-Earth objects. Carbonaceous objects are darker, more primitive, and
underrepresented in discovery precisely because they reflect little light. Metallic objects are rarer
and much denser. And a substantial fraction of asteroids above a few hundred metres appear to be
rubble piles — gravitationally bound aggregates rather than monolithic rock — which changes how they
respond to any attempt to move them.

Q: A newly discovered near-Earth asteroid is dark and was found only when it was relatively close.
This suggests:
A. It recently formed
B. It is likely carbonaceous, and its low reflectivity is exactly why it was not detected earlier;
   optical surveys are biased toward bright objects, so dark objects of a given size are
   systematically underrepresented in the catalogue
C. It is very small
D. It came from outside the solar system
Reasoning: Optical detection depends on reflected sunlight, so brightness confounds size and albedo.
A dark object must be substantially larger than a bright one to be equally detectable. This bias is
a known limitation of visible-light surveys and is the principal argument for infrared survey
instruments, which detect thermal emission and are far less albedo-dependent. Answer: B.

## Discovery Surveys and Orbit Determination

Finding near-Earth objects is a survey problem: repeatedly image large areas of sky, detect points
that move against the star background between exposures, and link detections across nights into
orbits. The observable population is limited by the survey's depth, its sky coverage rate, and the
observing geometry — objects in the direction of the Sun are effectively invisible from the ground,
which is a permanent blind spot for a class of approaching objects.

Orbit determination from a short observation arc is poorly constrained, and the resulting
uncertainty is the central quantity in impact assessment. A few nights of observation give an orbit
with a large uncertainty region that grows rapidly when propagated forward. Additional observations
shrink it, and recovering the object in archival imagery from previous oppositions — a precovery —
can extend the arc by years and collapse the uncertainty dramatically. Radar ranging, where the
object passes close enough, improves the orbit by orders of magnitude in a single observation.

The uncertainty region propagated to a future close approach forms an elongated volume, and the
probability of impact is the fraction of that volume intersecting Earth. This is why impact
probabilities characteristically rise and then fall as observations accumulate: the uncertainty
region shrinks while still containing Earth, so the fraction rises, and then Earth falls outside it
entirely and the probability drops to zero. A rising probability is therefore the expected behaviour
of an object that will miss, not evidence that it will hit.

## Impact Risk Assessment

Impact consequence scales steeply with size because kinetic energy scales with mass and therefore
with the cube of diameter. Objects a few metres across enter the atmosphere regularly and produce
bright fireballs. Tens of metres can produce an airburst causing local damage. Hundreds of metres
produce regional devastation and, if oceanic, tsunami. A kilometre or more approaches globally
consequential, and above a few kilometres the effects are civilization-scale.

Frequency scales inversely, and the relationship between size and interval is what determines where
the risk actually concentrates. Small impacts are frequent and individually minor; large ones are
catastrophic and rare. Integrating consequence against frequency shows that expected annual damage
is dominated by neither extreme but by intermediate sizes — objects large enough to cause regional
damage and small enough to occur on timescales of centuries, which is precisely the size range that
is least completely catalogued.

The completeness picture reflects survey effort. Objects above a kilometre are largely catalogued,
with the survey having found the great majority and none of the known ones posing a threat this
century. Objects in the hundred-metre range are substantially incomplete. Objects in the tens of
metres are almost entirely undiscovered, and the events that have actually occurred in recent decades
were in this range and were not detected in advance.

## Deflection Techniques

Deflecting an object requires changing its velocity, and the required change is remarkably small
when applied long in advance. A velocity change of centimetres per second, applied years before a
predicted impact, accumulates into a position change of thousands of kilometres. The relationship is
linear in lead time, which is why every deflection technique's feasibility depends more on warning
time than on the energy available.

Kinetic impact is the technique that has been demonstrated. A spacecraft strikes the object at high
relative velocity, transferring momentum. The transfer exceeds the spacecraft's own momentum because
ejected material carries additional momentum away, and the multiplication factor depends on the
object's structure and composition in ways that were uncertain until measured directly. The
demonstrated result confirmed both the technique's viability and that the multiplication factor can
be substantial for a rubble-pile target.

Gravity tractor uses a spacecraft's own gravitational attraction, station-keeping near the object and
pulling it slowly. The force is tiny and the technique requires years, and its advantage is precision
and complete independence from the object's structure — it works identically on a rubble pile and a
monolith, which no impulsive technique does. Nuclear options deliver the largest energy and are
reserved for short-warning or large-object scenarios, with a standoff burst vaporizing surface
material to produce thrust being preferred over a surface or subsurface detonation that risks
fragmenting the object into multiple still-threatening pieces.

## Atmospheric Entry and Response Planning

An object entering the atmosphere is decelerated by drag and heated intensely, and what happens next
depends on its strength and entry angle. Weak objects fragment at high altitude, converting kinetic
energy into a blast wave in the air rather than a ground impact. Strong objects — metallic bodies
particularly — survive to the surface. The airburst mode is the more common outcome for stony objects
in the tens-of-metres range, and it produces damage over a wide area from the blast wave without any
crater at all.

The damage mechanisms differ by scale and by mode. An airburst produces overpressure and thermal
radiation, with window breakage and injuries extending far beyond any structural collapse. A ground
impact produces a crater, seismic effects, and ejecta. An ocean impact produces a wave whose
propagation and coastal amplification are the dominant concern and are difficult to model reliably.
Very large impacts inject material into the stratosphere with global climatic effects that dominate
the direct damage entirely.

Response planning distinguishes sharply by warning time. Decades permits characterization,
deflection design, and execution with verification. Years permits a deflection attempt with less
margin. Months permits evacuation planning and civil preparation. Days permits only warning and
sheltering. Since the most likely event in any given period is a small object detected shortly
before entry, civil defence preparation for that case has better expected value than any deflection
capability, and the two are complementary rather than competing.
