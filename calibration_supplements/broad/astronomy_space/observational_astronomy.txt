## Coordinates, Time, and the Sky

The equatorial coordinate system projects Earth's latitude and longitude onto the sky.
Declination is the angular distance north or south of the celestial equator, from -90 to
+90 degrees, and behaves exactly like latitude. Right ascension is the angular
coordinate along the equator measured eastward from the vernal equinox, conventionally
in hours, minutes, and seconds where 24 hours spans the full circle, so one hour of
right ascension equals 15 degrees. The hour convention exists because the sky rotates
at about 15 degrees per hour, making right ascension differences directly readable as
observing time.

Precession means these coordinates drift. Earth's axis traces a cone with a period of
roughly 26,000 years, so the equinox moves and the coordinate grid moves with it. Any
position must therefore be qualified by an epoch — J2000.0 is the current standard, with
ICRS the modern realization tied to distant quasars rather than to Earth's orientation
at all. Mixing epochs produces position errors of tens of arcminutes over decades, which
is enormous by modern standards and is a genuine source of failed observations when old
catalogue positions are used without conversion.

The horizontal system of altitude and azimuth is what a telescope on the ground actually
points to, and it depends on the observer's location and the time. Converting between
equatorial and horizontal requires the local sidereal time, which tracks the stars
rather than the Sun. A sidereal day is 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds — about four
minutes shorter than a solar day, because Earth must rotate slightly more than 360
degrees to bring the Sun back to the meridian after having moved along its orbit. This
is why a given star rises four minutes earlier each night and why the visible
constellations change through the year.

Coordinate and time reference:
  right ascension       hours:min:sec, eastward from the vernal equinox; 1h = 15 deg
  declination           degrees north/south of the celestial equator
  epoch                 J2000.0 standard; ICRS is the modern quasar-based frame
  altitude / azimuth    local, time-dependent; what the mount actually points to
  hour angle            HA = LST - RA; zero at the meridian, the best time to observe
  airmass               sec(z) approximately; 1.0 at zenith, 2.0 at 60 deg zenith
                        angle, rising steeply below ~20 deg altitude
  sidereal day          23h 56m 04s
  Julian date           continuous day count; JD 2451545.0 = 2000 Jan 1.5 TT
  angular units         1 deg = 60 arcmin = 3600 arcsec;  1 rad ≈ 206265 arcsec

The number 206,265 — arcseconds per radian — is the most useful constant in
observational astronomy, because it converts between the small-angle physical and
angular scales constantly. An object of physical size s at distance d subtends s/d
radians, which is 206265 s/d arcseconds. It is also where the parsec comes from: the
distance at which one astronomical unit subtends one arcsecond, about 3.086e16 metres or
3.26 light-years. Parallax distance in parsecs is simply the reciprocal of the parallax
angle in arcseconds, which is why the unit exists at all.

Q: A star has a measured parallax of 0.025 arcseconds. Its distance is:
A. 25 pc   B. 40 pc   C. 0.025 pc   D. 400 pc
Reasoning: Distance in parsecs is 1/parallax in arcseconds, so 1/0.025 = 40 pc, about
130 light-years. Parallax measurement is the only direct geometric distance method and
the foundation of the entire distance ladder; Gaia has extended reliable parallaxes to
microarcsecond precision, reaching across much of the galaxy. Answer: B.

## Magnitudes and Photometry

The magnitude system is logarithmic, inverted, and historically arbitrary, which makes
it perpetually confusing and unavoidable. It originates with Hipparchus ranking naked-eye
stars from first magnitude (brightest) to sixth (faintest), later formalized so that a
difference of five magnitudes corresponds to exactly a factor of 100 in flux. One
magnitude is therefore a factor of 100^(1/5) ≈ 2.512, and brighter objects have smaller —
sometimes negative — magnitudes.

Apparent magnitude measures how bright an object appears from Earth, conflating its
intrinsic luminosity with its distance. Absolute magnitude is the apparent magnitude the
object would have at a standard distance of 10 parsecs, so it measures intrinsic
luminosity. The difference between them, the distance modulus m - M = 5 log10(d/10 pc),
is the workhorse of distance determination: measure the apparent magnitude, know the
absolute magnitude by some means, and the distance follows. Everything in the cosmic
distance ladder is a method for learning M.

Photometry through filters gives colour, and colour gives temperature. A blue-sensitive
filter and a red-sensitive filter measure different parts of the spectrum, and their
magnitude difference — the colour index — is a proxy for the effective temperature of
the emitting surface, since hot objects peak at shorter wavelengths. The B-V index is
about -0.3 for the hottest O stars, 0.0 for A stars, 0.65 for the Sun, and above 1.5 for
cool M dwarfs. The colour-magnitude diagram built from these quantities is the
Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, the single most information-dense plot in astronomy.

Photometry reference:
  magnitude difference     m1 - m2 = -2.5 log10(F1/F2)
  flux ratio               F1/F2 = 10^(-0.4 (m1 - m2))
  distance modulus         m - M = 5 log10(d_pc) - 5
  with extinction          m - M = 5 log10(d_pc) - 5 + A
  absolute magnitude       apparent magnitude at 10 pc
  Sun                      V = -26.74 apparent, +4.83 absolute
  full Moon                V ≈ -12.7
  naked-eye limit          V ≈ +6 in dark sky
  Vega system              Vega defined as ~0 in all bands
  AB system                zero point tied to a constant flux density; preferred
                           for modern surveys
  signal-to-noise          SNR = S / sqrt(S + n_pix (B + D + R^2))

That last expression is the CCD equation and it governs every exposure-time
calculation. The signal S grows linearly with exposure time while the noise from source
photons grows as its square root, so signal-to-noise improves as sqrt(t) in the
source-limited regime — meaning four times the exposure for twice the precision. In the
background-limited regime, typical for faint objects, the background term dominates and
the same sqrt(t) scaling holds but with a much larger constant. Read noise R enters
once per readout regardless of exposure length, which is why many short exposures are
worse than one long exposure for faint targets, and why the optimal strategy is
exposures long enough that background noise exceeds read noise but short enough to
avoid saturation and tracking errors.

Extinction from dust dims and reddens light, and neglecting it causes distances to be
systematically overestimated. Dust scatters and absorbs blue light preferentially, so
distant objects appear both fainter and redder than they are. The reddening is what
makes correction possible: comparing an object's observed colour to its expected
intrinsic colour gives the colour excess, and a fairly universal extinction law converts
that into the total dimming. In the galactic plane extinction can exceed 30 magnitudes
in visible light, which is why the galactic centre is studied in infrared and radio,
where dust is transparent.

## Telescopes and Optics

A telescope does two things: it collects light and it resolves detail. Light-gathering
power scales with the square of the aperture diameter, so a 10-metre telescope collects
100 times the light of a 1-metre. This is the primary reason apertures keep growing, and
it is why aperture rather than magnification is the meaningful specification — a claim
about magnification says nothing about whether an object will be visible at all.

Angular resolution is limited by diffraction. The Rayleigh criterion gives the minimum
resolvable separation as 1.22 lambda/D radians, which for visible light and a 1-metre
aperture is about 0.13 arcseconds. Resolution improves with aperture and degrades with
wavelength, which is why radio telescopes must be enormous or must be linked
interferometrically to achieve comparable resolution — a radio array's resolution is set
by the separation between its elements rather than by the size of any one, which is how
very-long-baseline interferometry achieves microarcsecond resolution using dishes
scattered across continents.

Reflectors dominate modern astronomy over refractors for several converging reasons.
Mirrors have no chromatic aberration, since reflection is wavelength-independent, while
lenses focus different colours at different points unless corrected with multiple
elements. Mirrors can be supported across their entire back surface, while a lens can
only be held at its edge and sags under its own weight beyond about a metre. And only
one surface must be figured to optical precision rather than two per element. The largest
refractor ever built is about 1 metre; segmented mirrors now exceed 10 metres and 30-metre
class instruments are under construction.

Optical configurations and their tradeoffs:
  Newtonian         parabolic primary, flat diagonal; simple, cheap, coma off-axis
  Cassegrain        parabolic primary, hyperbolic secondary; long focal length in a
                    short tube
  Ritchey-Chretien  hyperbolic primary and secondary; coma-free wide field, the
                    professional standard
  Schmidt-Cassegrain corrector plate plus spherical mirrors; compact, popular for
                    amateur use, some field curvature
  refractor         no central obstruction, high contrast; chromatic aberration
                    unless apochromatic, size-limited
  focal ratio       f/D; low f-number gives a wide field and fast imaging, high
                    f-number gives higher magnification and easier optical tolerance
  plate scale       206265 / f_mm arcsec per mm at the focal plane

Aberrations each have a characteristic signature that identifies them at the eyepiece or
in an image. Spherical aberration puts light from different annuli at different focal
points, producing a soft image that will not come to a sharp focus anywhere — the defect
that afflicted Hubble before correction. Coma makes off-axis stars look like small
comets with tails pointing radially outward. Astigmatism gives stars that elongate in
perpendicular directions on either side of focus. Field curvature means the focal
surface is not flat, so the centre and edge cannot be simultaneously in focus on a flat
detector. Chromatic aberration produces coloured halos. Diagnosing which is present is
mostly a matter of examining star images inside and outside focus.

[assistant] It fits with enormous room to spare, and you are significantly oversampled.

Your focal length is 1000 mm, giving a plate scale of 206 arcsec/mm, so each 3.76 micron
pixel covers 0.776 arcseconds. Your field of view is about 81 by 54 arcminutes — the
galaxy occupies roughly 5% of the frame width. That is a lot of empty sky, though it
does give you plenty of stars for calibration and plate solving.

The sampling is the part worth acting on. Under 2.5 arcsecond seeing, your stars will be
about 3.2 pixels across at full width half maximum. Nyquist sampling requires about 2
pixels per FWHM, so you are oversampled by roughly 60%. Oversampling is not a
correctness problem but it costs you: you are spreading the same photons over more
pixels, so each pixel has lower signal and read noise is incurred more times per unit of
collected light. Faint detail suffers.

Two straightforward improvements. Bin 2x2 — this gives 1.55 arcsec/pixel, still adequately
sampled at 1.6 pixels per FWHM under your seeing, and quadruples the signal per pixel
while reading out once per binned pixel. On a CMOS sensor the read-noise benefit is
smaller than on a CCD since binning is usually done in software, but the signal-to-noise
per pixel still improves and files shrink by a factor of four.

Alternatively use a focal reducer to bring you to about f/3.5, which would give roughly
1.1 arcsec/pixel natively, a brighter image per pixel, and shorter exposures for the same
depth. This is the better option if you image a lot of extended faint objects.

One caution: your Dawes limit is 0.58 arcseconds, so the optics can in principle resolve
far finer detail than the 2.5 arcsecond seeing permits. On an exceptional night with 1.2
arcsecond seeing your native sampling would be closer to correct, so if you bin, do it in
software after capture rather than in hardware — that keeps the option open.

## Detectors and Spectroscopy

Charge-coupled devices and CMOS sensors convert photons into measurable charge through
the photoelectric effect. Quantum efficiency — the fraction of incident photons producing
a detected electron — reaches above 90% in modern back-illuminated devices, against a few
percent for photographic emulsion, which is the single technological change that
transformed observational astronomy in the 1980s. The device is linear over most of its
range, meaning measured signal is proportional to incident light, which is what makes
quantitative photometry straightforward.

Noise sources are distinct and must be handled separately. Photon shot noise is
irreducible, arising from the Poisson statistics of photon arrival, with standard
deviation equal to the square root of the count. Read noise is added once per readout by
the amplifier, typically a few electrons in modern devices. Dark current accumulates
thermally in proportion to exposure time and roughly doubles every 6-7 degrees Celsius,
which is why scientific cameras are cooled. Fixed-pattern effects — pixel-to-pixel
sensitivity variation, dust shadows, vignetting — are systematic rather than random and
are removed by calibration rather than by averaging.

The standard calibration sequence removes each systematic in turn. Bias frames, taken at
zero exposure, capture the electronic offset. Dark frames, taken with the shutter closed
at the same exposure and temperature as the science frames, capture thermal signal. Flat
fields, taken on a uniformly illuminated surface, capture the multiplicative response
variation including dust and vignetting. The reduction is then science minus bias minus
dark, all divided by the normalized flat. Getting the order wrong, or flat-fielding
before subtracting the additive terms, leaves residual structure that limits precision.

Calibration and reduction:
  bias        zero-second exposure; electronic offset, additive
  dark        shutter closed, matched exposure and temperature; thermal, additive
  flat        uniform illumination; response variation, multiplicative
  reduction   (raw - bias - dark) / normalized_flat
  cosmic rays sharp single-pixel events; rejected by median-combining dithered
              frames, or by morphology-based algorithms on single frames
  dithering   shift pointing slightly between exposures so detector defects land on
              different sky positions and average out
  stacking    combines frames; SNR improves as sqrt(N); sigma-clipping rejects
              transient outliers
  plate solve matches detected stars to a catalogue to derive an exact world
              coordinate solution

Spectroscopy disperses light by wavelength and is where most astrophysics actually
happens, since an image tells you where something is while a spectrum tells you what it
is made of, how hot it is, how fast it is moving, and often how massive it is. A
diffraction grating or prism spreads the light, and the resolving power R = lambda /
delta_lambda characterizes the instrument. Low resolution, R of a few hundred,
classifies objects and gives redshifts. Medium resolution, a few thousand, measures
line strengths and abundances. High resolution, tens of thousands and above, resolves
line profiles and measures radial velocities to metres per second — the precision that
makes Doppler exoplanet detection possible.

Spectral lines encode enormous information. Their wavelengths identify elements, since
each has a unique pattern. Their shift gives radial velocity through the Doppler
relation, with cosmological redshift for distant objects. Their strength gives abundance
and excitation conditions. Their width encodes temperature through thermal broadening,
pressure through collisional broadening, and rotation or turbulence through kinematic
broadening. Emission lines indicate hot diffuse gas, absorption lines indicate a cooler
medium in front of a continuum source, and the distinction is Kirchhoff's laws applied
to astrophysical sources.

## Atmosphere, Surveys, and Modern Practice

The atmosphere degrades ground-based observation in several distinct ways. Absorption
blocks entire wavelength ranges — ultraviolet by ozone, much of the infrared by water
vapour, and everything shortward of the near-ultraviolet — which is why space telescopes
exist and why infrared observatories are placed on high dry sites. Emission from the
sky itself, including airglow and scattered light, sets the background against which
faint sources must be detected. Refraction bends light and displaces objects toward the
zenith, differentially with wavelength, producing atmospheric dispersion that elongates
star images near the horizon.

Seeing is the most operationally important effect: turbulent cells of varying refractive
index scramble the incoming wavefront, so a point source is smeared into a blob typically
0.5 to 2 arcseconds across regardless of aperture. Beyond a modest size, larger
telescopes therefore gain light-collecting power but not resolution from the ground. The
site's median seeing is the primary criterion for observatory placement, which is why
major facilities sit on isolated high peaks with laminar airflow — Mauna Kea, the
Atacama, La Palma.

Adaptive optics defeats seeing by measuring the distorted wavefront hundreds of times
per second and correcting it with a deformable mirror. A bright reference source is
required, and since one rarely lies near the target, laser guide stars create an
artificial reference by exciting sodium atoms in the mesosphere. The correction is best
in the infrared, where the wavefront distortion is a smaller fraction of a wavelength and
the isoplanatic patch — the angular region over which the correction remains valid — is
larger. A well-corrected 8-metre telescope reaches its diffraction limit, resolving
detail an order of magnitude finer than the natural seeing permits.

Observational limits and mitigations:
  atmospheric absorption   space, or high dry sites for infrared
  sky background           dark sites, narrowband filters, space
  seeing                   adaptive optics, lucky imaging, speckle interferometry,
                           or space
  light pollution          site selection, narrowband filters; a growing problem
                           near populated areas
  satellite trails         increasingly severe with large constellations; mitigated
                           by masking and by scheduling around predicted passes
  radio interference       protected frequency bands, remote radio-quiet zones

Modern astronomy is increasingly survey-driven and data-dominated rather than
target-driven. Rather than pointing at chosen objects, wide-field surveys image large
sky areas repeatedly and build catalogues that thousands of researchers mine. Gaia
measured positions, parallaxes, and proper motions for nearly two billion stars. The
Vera Rubin Observatory will image the entire southern sky every few nights, producing
tens of terabytes nightly and millions of transient alerts. The scientific bottleneck has
shifted from photon collection to data management, classification, and the automated
triage of alert streams — which is why machine learning has become a standard tool in
the field, applied to variable-star classification, transient identification, photometric
redshift estimation, and gravitational lens discovery.

Multi-messenger astronomy adds channels beyond electromagnetic radiation. Neutrino
detectors observed the burst from Supernova 1987A hours before the light arrived, since
neutrinos escape the collapsing core promptly while photons diffuse out. Gravitational
wave detectors observed the neutron star merger GW170817 in coincidence with a
short gamma-ray burst and a kilonova, confirming the origin of heavy elements and
constraining the speed of gravitational waves to match light to within one part in
10^15. Cosmic rays provide a fourth channel, though magnetic deflection destroys their
directional information except at the highest energies. Combining channels constrains
physics that no single one can, and the coordination problem — distributing alerts fast
enough for other facilities to respond within hours — has become a substantial part of
the field's infrastructure.
