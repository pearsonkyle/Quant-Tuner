## Radiation Mechanisms

High-energy astrophysics studies the most energetic processes in the universe, and
interpreting what is observed requires knowing which physical process produced the radiation.
The spectrum's shape is the primary diagnostic, because each mechanism produces a
characteristic form that persists across enormously different sources.

Thermal emission from an optically thick source is a blackbody, whose spectrum depends only on
temperature. The peak wavelength moves inversely with temperature, so a source peaking in X-rays
is at millions of kelvin. Thermal bremsstrahlung — braking radiation from electrons deflected by
ions in a hot ionized gas — produces a flat spectrum with an exponential cutoff at the thermal
energy, and it is what the hot gas in galaxy clusters emits.

Synchrotron radiation comes from relativistic electrons spiralling in a magnetic field, and it
produces a power-law spectrum rather than a thermal one because the electron energy distribution
is itself a power law. This is the signature of non-thermal particle acceleration, and its
presence establishes that some process has accelerated particles far beyond thermal energies.
Synchrotron emission is also polarized, which distinguishes it observationally and provides a
probe of the magnetic field geometry.

Inverse Compton scattering upscatters low-energy photons to high energy when they encounter
relativistic electrons, which is the reverse of ordinary Compton scattering where a high-energy
photon loses energy to an electron. It is how the highest-energy photons in many sources are
produced, and the synchrotron self-Compton process — where the same electron population produces
synchrotron photons and then upscatters them — gives a characteristic two-humped spectrum seen
in blazars.

Radiation mechanisms and their signatures:
  blackbody            optically thick thermal; peak set by temperature alone
  bremsstrahlung       hot ionized gas; flat with an exponential cutoff
  synchrotron          relativistic electrons in a magnetic field; power law,
                       polarized; establishes non-thermal acceleration
  inverse Compton      relativistic electrons upscatter photons; produces the
                       highest energies
  synchrotron self-Compton  the same electrons do both; a two-humped spectrum
  cyclotron            non-relativistic electrons in a field; discrete lines,
                       measuring the field strength directly
  line emission        atomic transitions; identifies composition, temperature,
                       ionization state, and velocity
  fluorescence         iron K-alpha at 6.4 keV; broadened and skewed by
                       relativistic effects near a black hole, which is how the
                       spin is measured
  pair production      photon-photon above the threshold; limits how compact a
                       gamma-ray source can be
  Compton drag         limits particle energies in dense radiation fields

The iron K-alpha line deserves particular attention because it is the most direct probe of
strong gravity available. Iron fluoresces at a known rest energy, and a line produced in an
accretion disc close to a black hole is broadened by Doppler shifts from orbital motion and
skewed by gravitational redshift, producing a distinctive asymmetric profile. The extent of the
red wing depends on how close to the black hole the disc extends, which depends on the spin —
so fitting the line profile measures a black hole's spin from an atomic transition.

Q: A source shows a power-law spectrum extending over several decades in frequency, with
significant linear polarization. The emission mechanism is most likely:
A. Blackbody
B. Synchrotron radiation from relativistic electrons in a magnetic field
C. Thermal bremsstrahlung
D. Atomic line emission
Reasoning: A blackbody has a specific curved shape set by one temperature and is unpolarized.
Bremsstrahlung is flat with an exponential cutoff rather than a broad power law. Polarization
requires an ordered magnetic field, and a broad power law requires a power-law electron energy
distribution — both are the defining characteristics of synchrotron emission. Answer: B.

## Accretion

Accretion onto a compact object is the most efficient energy-release process known apart from
matter-antimatter annihilation, and it powers most high-energy sources. Material falling toward
a compact object releases gravitational potential energy, and the efficiency depends on how deep
into the potential well it falls before the energy is radiated. For a neutron star this is about
ten percent of the rest mass energy, and for a black hole it ranges from roughly six percent for
a non-rotating one to over thirty percent for a maximally rotating one — compared with about
0.7 percent for hydrogen fusion.

Angular momentum is the obstacle that makes accretion structured rather than radial. Infalling
material almost always has some angular momentum, so it cannot fall directly in and instead
settles into an orbiting disc. Accretion then requires transporting angular momentum outward so
that material can move inward, and identifying the mechanism was a long-standing problem —
ordinary molecular viscosity is far too weak. The magnetorotational instability provides it: a
weak magnetic field in a differentially rotating disc becomes unstable, generating turbulence
that transports angular momentum efficiently.

The Eddington limit sets the maximum steady accretion rate. As accretion luminosity rises, the
outward radiation pressure on infalling material grows, and at the Eddington luminosity it
balances gravity so that further accretion is halted. The limit is proportional to the accretor's
mass, which means a given source has a maximum luminosity set by its mass — and observing a
source above that limit implies either beaming, a larger mass than assumed, or genuinely
super-Eddington accretion through a geometry that lets radiation escape without impeding the
inflow.

Accretion physics:
  efficiency           ~10% for a neutron star, 6-32% for a black hole depending
                       on spin; fusion is ~0.7%
  angular momentum     the obstacle; requires outward transport for inward flow
  magnetorotational instability  the transport mechanism; magnetic fields in
                       differential rotation
  thin disc            efficient radiator, geometrically thin, optically thick;
                       the standard picture at moderate rates
  thick / advective    at very low or very high rates; energy advected inward
                       rather than radiated
  Eddington luminosity  radiation pressure balances gravity; proportional to mass
  super-Eddington      possible with anisotropic geometry or beaming
  innermost stable orbit  no stable circular orbit below it; its radius depends on
                       spin, which is how spin measurements work
  boundary layer       where the flow meets a neutron star's surface; absent for a
                       black hole, and its absence is observational evidence for a
                       horizon
  jets                 collimated relativistic outflows; the launching mechanism
                       involves magnetic fields and rotation and is not fully
                       settled
  variability          light-crossing time bounds the emitting region's size; rapid
                       variability implies a compact source

The light-crossing argument deserves emphasis because it constrains source sizes without
resolving them. A source cannot vary coherently on a timescale shorter than light takes to cross
it, because different parts could not coordinate. A source varying on a timescale of
milliseconds is therefore smaller than a light-millisecond, about 300 kilometres — which for an
object radiating the luminosity of many suns implies an extraordinary energy density and was
among the earliest arguments that compact objects power these sources.

## Compact Object Systems and Transients

X-ray binaries consist of a compact object accreting from a companion star, and they divide by
the companion's mass in a way that determines the accretion mechanism. High-mass systems have a
massive companion whose strong stellar wind is captured, giving persistent emission. Low-mass
systems have a companion overflowing its Roche lobe, transferring material through the inner
Lagrange point into a disc, and these frequently show outbursts as disc instabilities trigger
episodic accretion.

Distinguishing a neutron star from a black hole accretor is done by the presence or absence of a
surface. A neutron star has one, so material accumulating on it eventually ignites in a
thermonuclear flash producing a type I X-ray burst, and its rotating magnetic field can produce
pulsations. A black hole has neither, so bursts and pulsations are absent — and the systematic
absence of surface phenomena in the sources whose mass exceeds the neutron star limit is
observational support for event horizons.

Supernovae divide into two physically distinct classes despite a spectroscopic classification
that predates the understanding. Core-collapse supernovae occur when a massive star's iron core
exceeds its degeneracy limit and collapses, with the resulting neutrino burst and rebound
ejecting the envelope. Thermonuclear supernovae occur when a white dwarf approaching the
Chandrasekhar limit ignites runaway carbon fusion, unbinding the star entirely. The second class
is more useful cosmologically because the explosion occurs at a consistent mass, giving a
standardizable luminosity.

Compact objects and transients:
  high-mass X-ray binary  wind accretion from a massive companion; persistent
  low-mass X-ray binary   Roche lobe overflow; often transient
  type I X-ray burst      thermonuclear flash on a neutron star surface; proves a
                          surface exists
  pulsations              a rotating magnetized neutron star
  black hole evidence     absence of surface phenomena, mass above the neutron
                          star limit, and now direct imaging and gravitational
                          waves
  state transitions       accretion geometry changes with rate; hard and soft
                          spectral states with hysteresis
  quasi-periodic oscillations  frequencies near the orbital timescale at the inner
                          disc; a probe of strong gravity
  core-collapse supernova  massive star iron core collapse; neutrino-driven
  thermonuclear supernova  white dwarf carbon detonation; standardizable candle
  kilonova                neutron star merger; produces heavy elements by rapid
                          neutron capture
  gamma-ray burst, long   collapse of a rapidly rotating massive star
  gamma-ray burst, short  compact object merger; confirmed by the 2017 multi-
                          messenger event
  tidal disruption        a star torn apart by a supermassive black hole;
                          illuminates otherwise quiescent nuclei
  fast radio burst        millisecond radio transients; some repeat, magnetars are
                          the leading source class, and the mechanism remains open

The 2017 neutron star merger is worth stating as a case because it settled several questions at
once. Gravitational waves and a short gamma-ray burst arrived within two seconds, confirming that
compact object mergers produce short bursts and constraining the speed of gravitational waves to
match light to within one part in ten to the fifteenth. The subsequent kilonova showed spectral
signatures of heavy element production by rapid neutron capture, establishing mergers as a
dominant source of elements heavier than iron. And the combination of the gravitational wave
distance with the host galaxy's redshift provided an independent measurement of the expansion
rate.

## Active Galactic Nuclei and Jets

An active galactic nucleus is a supermassive black hole accreting at a rate producing
luminosity comparable to or exceeding the host galaxy's stars. The energy source is accretion
rather than fusion, which is established by the efficiency required — the luminosity and the
variability timescale together imply an energy release per unit mass that fusion cannot supply.

The unified model explains the observational zoo as one object viewed from different angles. A
central black hole with an accretion disc is surrounded by a torus of obscuring material, and
whether the broad emission lines from fast-moving gas near the black hole are visible depends on
whether the line of sight passes through the torus. Viewing along a jet gives yet another
appearance, dominated by relativistically beamed emission from the jet itself. This reduces
Seyfert types, radio galaxies, quasars, and blazars substantially to orientation plus intrinsic
luminosity and accretion rate.

Relativistic beaming is what makes blazars extreme. Emission from material moving toward the
observer at close to light speed is boosted in apparent luminosity by a large factor, is
compressed in observed timescale, and is blueshifted. The consequences include apparent
superluminal motion — where a jet component appears to move across the sky faster than light
because it is moving nearly toward us and nearly keeping pace with its own emitted light — which
is a projection effect rather than a violation of relativity.

Active galactic nuclei:
  energy source        accretion onto a supermassive black hole
  unified model        one object; appearance depends on orientation
  obscuring torus      hides the broad line region from some viewing angles
  broad lines          fast gas close to the black hole; visible when unobscured
  narrow lines         slower gas further out; visible from most angles
  Seyfert types        type 1 shows broad lines, type 2 does not
  quasar               high luminosity; outshines the host galaxy
  blazar               viewed along the jet; beamed, variable, polarized
  radio loud vs quiet  whether powerful jets are present; the cause of the
                       dichotomy is not settled
  superluminal motion  apparent faster-than-light jet motion; a projection effect
                       from motion nearly toward the observer
  M-sigma relation     black hole mass correlates with the host bulge velocity
                       dispersion; implies coupled growth
  AGN feedback         outflows heating and expelling gas; regulates star
                       formation in the host and is required for galaxy formation
                       models to reproduce observed galaxy masses

AGN feedback deserves emphasis because it connects the smallest scales to the largest. A black
hole occupies a negligible fraction of a galaxy's volume and mass, and its accretion releases
enough energy to unbind a substantial fraction of the galaxy's gas. Simulations of galaxy
formation without this feedback produce galaxies far more massive than observed, because gas
cools and forms stars unchecked. Including energy injection from the nucleus regulates it and
reproduces the observed distribution — which is why the M-sigma correlation between black hole
mass and host properties is interpreted as a signature of coupled growth rather than as a
coincidence.

## Cosmic Rays, Neutrinos, and Detection

Cosmic rays are charged particles arriving from space, predominantly protons, with an energy
spectrum extending to energies far beyond any accelerator. Their charge means magnetic fields
deflect them, so their arrival directions do not point at their sources except at the very
highest energies where deflection becomes small. This is why identifying cosmic ray sources has
been so difficult despite a century of observation.

The spectrum has features that constrain the acceleration mechanisms. It follows a power law
with a steepening at the knee around three petaelectronvolts, interpreted as the limit of
galactic accelerators, and a flattening at the ankle around a few exaelectronvolts, interpreted
as a transition to extragalactic sources. Above about fifty exaelectronvolts the spectrum should
cut off because protons interact with cosmic microwave background photons over intergalactic
distances — the GZK cutoff — which is observed and which limits the distance the highest-energy
particles can have travelled.

Neutrinos solve the pointing problem because they are neutral and interact weakly, so they
travel undeflected and unabsorbed from their sources. That same weak interaction makes them
extraordinarily difficult to detect, requiring instrumented volumes of a cubic kilometre of ice
or water to catch enough. High-energy astrophysical neutrinos have been detected and at least one
has been associated with a flaring blazar, establishing that at least some are produced in
active galactic nuclei.

Cosmic rays, neutrinos, and instruments:
  composition          mostly protons, some heavier nuclei, a small electron
                       component
  spectrum             a power law with a knee at ~3 PeV and an ankle at ~3 EeV
  GZK cutoff           interaction with the microwave background above ~50 EeV;
                       limits propagation distance
  magnetic deflection  destroys directional information except at the highest
                       energies
  shock acceleration   diffusive acceleration at supernova remnant shocks; the
                       leading galactic mechanism
  air showers          a primary particle cascades in the atmosphere; detected by
                       surface arrays and by fluorescence
  neutrino advantage   neutral and weakly interacting; points back to the source
  neutrino detection   cubic-kilometre instrumented ice or water; Cherenkov light
                       from secondary particles
  X-ray telescopes     grazing incidence optics, since normal-incidence mirrors do
                       not reflect X-rays
  gamma-ray detection  above ~100 MeV by pair conversion in space; above ~100 GeV
                       by atmospheric Cherenkov telescopes on the ground
  gravitational waves  kilometre-scale laser interferometers; strain sensitivity
                       below one part in 10^21
  multi-messenger      combining electromagnetic, neutrino, gravitational wave,
                       and cosmic ray channels; constrains physics no single
                       channel can

Grazing incidence optics deserve a note because they explain why X-ray telescopes look nothing
like optical ones. X-rays are absorbed rather than reflected at normal incidence, and reflect
efficiently only at very shallow angles — a few degrees or less. X-ray telescopes therefore use
nested cylindrical mirror shells that the incoming rays graze, which gives a small collecting
area for a large physical size and is why X-ray telescopes have far less effective area than
their dimensions suggest. The same physics is why focusing becomes progressively harder at higher
photon energy, and why hard X-ray and gamma-ray instruments use coded masks and pair-conversion
tracking rather than focusing optics at all.
