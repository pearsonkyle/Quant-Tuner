## Mission Design and System Engineering

Spacecraft design is dominated by coupling. Mass, power, thermal, propulsion,
communications, and attitude control are not independent subsystems that can be optimized
separately — every kilogram added requires structure to support it, propellant to move it,
power to run it, and radiator area to cool it, and each of those additions costs mass in
turn. This mass spiral is why spacecraft engineering is fundamentally a systems discipline
and why margins are managed at the system level rather than per subsystem.

The mission concept drives everything downstream. Orbit selection determines eclipse
duration and therefore battery sizing, determines communication geometry and therefore
antenna and link budget, determines radiation exposure and therefore parts selection,
determines thermal environment and therefore radiator area. Changing an orbit late in design
is not a parameter adjustment; it invalidates most of the subsystem work.

Margins exist because estimates are wrong in a predictable direction. Mass grows through
development as details are resolved, historically by twenty to thirty percent from
preliminary design to launch, so mass margin is held explicitly and released as the design
matures. Power margin, data margin, link margin, propellant margin, and schedule margin are
tracked the same way. A programme that consumes its margin early has, in effect, discovered
that its design does not close, and the honest response is descope rather than optimism.

Design drivers and their couplings:
  orbit                eclipse fraction, radiation dose, thermal cycling, link
                       geometry, delta-v requirements, deorbit obligations
  mass                 launch cost, propellant, structure, and every other budget
  power                array area, battery capacity, thermal dissipation
  thermal              radiator area, coatings, heater power, component placement
  pointing             sensor and actuator selection, structural stiffness,
                       flexible mode interaction
  data volume          downlink rate, contact time, onboard storage, compression
  lifetime             propellant, radiation dose, component wear-out, redundancy
  reliability          redundancy strategy, parts grade, test programme
  cost and schedule    the constraints that actually terminate programmes

Technology readiness level is the standard vocabulary for maturity, running from basic
principles observed at level one through flight-proven at level nine. Its practical use is
risk management: a mission whose critical path depends on a level-four technology is
carrying schedule risk that a review board will identify, and the mitigation is either
maturing the technology on a separate path or holding a conventional backup. The recurring
programmatic failure is a design that depends on several immature technologies
simultaneously, since their risks compound rather than average.

Q: A spacecraft design adds 15 kg of instrument mass late in development. The most likely
total system impact is:
A. Exactly 15 kg
B. Substantially more than 15 kg, because structure, power, propellant, and thermal all
   grow in response
C. Less than 15 kg, since margins absorb it
D. None, if mass margin remains
Reasoning: Added instrument mass requires structural support, likely additional power and
therefore array and battery mass, additional propellant to achieve the same delta-v, and
possibly additional radiator area. Historical growth factors for this mass spiral range from
roughly 1.5 to 3 depending on how much propulsion is involved. Answer: B.

## Structures and the Launch Environment

The launch is the most severe mechanical environment the spacecraft ever experiences, and
most structural design is driven by it rather than by anything encountered in orbit — where
the vehicle is essentially weightless and loads are tiny.

Quasi-static loads combine steady acceleration with low-frequency transients, reaching
perhaps six to nine g axially depending on the vehicle. Acoustic loading during liftoff and
transonic flight is intense broadband pressure, which couples most strongly into large
lightweight panels such as solar arrays and antenna dishes. Random vibration transmitted
through the structure is specified as a power spectral density and is what drives fatigue
and connector failure. Shock from stage separation and fairing jettison is very high
frequency and low duration, harmless to structures and destructive to relays, crystals, and
brittle components.

Stiffness requirements are usually more binding than strength. Launch vehicles specify
minimum fundamental frequencies for the payload — typically tens of hertz laterally and
somewhat higher axially — so that the spacecraft's modes do not couple with the vehicle's
control system or structural dynamics. A design that meets its stress margins and fails its
frequency requirement must be stiffened, which adds mass in a way that strength-driven sizing
would not have.

Structural and environment considerations:
  quasi-static load     ~6-9 g axial; sizes primary structure
  acoustic              ~140 dB overall; drives large lightweight surfaces
  random vibration      PSD specification; fatigue and connector failure
  pyroshock             thousands of g at high frequency; damages brittle parts
  frequency requirement typically >35 Hz lateral, >25-100 Hz axial per vehicle
  factor of safety      1.25-1.4 on yield for qualification hardware, higher for
                        untested or brittle materials
  materials             aluminium for cost and machinability, CFRP for stiffness
                        and low CTE, titanium for fittings, invar for optical
                        benches
  outgassing            total mass loss <1%, volatile condensable <0.1%;
                        contamination of optics and thermal surfaces
  venting               trapped volumes must vent during ascent or burst
  test programme        protoflight or qual + acceptance; test as you fly

Thermal expansion is a structural problem in space in a way it rarely is on the ground.
Temperature swings of a hundred degrees or more across an orbit, combined with materials of
differing expansion coefficients, produce distortion and stress at every joint between
dissimilar materials. Optical instruments requiring micron stability use low-expansion
materials such as invar, ULE glass, or carefully laid-up carbon fibre, and are often
kinematically mounted so that the mount does not transmit distortion from the supporting
structure — three points defining position without over-constraint.

## Power and Thermal Control

Electrical power comes from solar arrays for anything inside roughly Jupiter's orbit,
radioisotope generators beyond, and batteries for eclipse and peak loads. Solar array sizing
starts from the solar constant at the mission's distance, multiplied by cell efficiency,
packing factor, incidence angle losses, temperature derating, and end-of-life degradation
from radiation. That last factor is substantial: arrays in the radiation belts can lose
fifteen to thirty percent of their output over a multi-year mission, and the array must be
sized for end of life rather than beginning, so it is oversized and dissipates the excess
early in the mission.

Batteries carry the spacecraft through eclipse and supply transient loads exceeding array
capacity. Sizing depends on eclipse duration, load, and permitted depth of discharge, which
is limited by cycle life — a low Earth orbit spacecraft experiences about 5,500 eclipse
cycles per year, so depth of discharge must be shallow, typically twenty to thirty percent,
to reach a multi-year life. A geostationary satellite has only two eclipse seasons per year
with about ninety cycles, so much deeper discharge is acceptable, and the battery is
correspondingly smaller relative to its load.

Thermal control in vacuum has only one heat rejection mechanism: radiation. There is no
convection and no conduction to an ambient medium, so every watt dissipated internally must
leave as infrared emission from a surface with a view to space. Radiator area therefore
scales with dissipated power divided by the fourth power of the achievable temperature,
which is why high-power spacecraft have large radiators and why cryogenic instruments need
enormous ones or active cooling.

Power and thermal quantities:
  solar constant        1361 W/m^2 at 1 AU; scales as 1/r^2
  triple-junction cell  ~30% efficiency at beginning of life
  array sizing          include packing, cosine loss, temperature, and EOL
                        degradation; size for end of life
  battery DOD           20-30% for LEO cycling, 60-80% for GEO seasonal
  eclipse, LEO          up to ~36 min of a ~95 min orbit
  radiative rejection   Q = eps sigma A (T^4 - T_space^4)
  surface properties    alpha/eps ratio governs equilibrium temperature; a low
                        ratio runs cold, a high ratio runs hot
  MLI                   multilayer insulation; effective emittance ~0.01-0.03
  heat pipe             moves heat isothermally without power; the workhorse
  louvers               passive variable emittance
  heaters               thermostatically controlled; a real and often
                        underestimated power budget item
  survival heaters      keep components above minimum temperature in safe mode

The absorptivity-to-emissivity ratio is the key surface parameter and it is worth
internalizing. Absorptivity governs how much sunlight a surface takes in; emissivity governs
how well it radiates infrared out. A surface with low absorptivity and high emissivity — white
paint, optical solar reflectors — runs cold and is used for radiators. High absorptivity and
low emissivity — polished metal — runs hot. Both properties degrade in orbit through
ultraviolet exposure and atomic oxygen erosion, generally in the direction of increasing
absorptivity, so thermal designs must accommodate the end-of-life values, which means the
spacecraft is too cold at the start and heaters make up the difference.

In deep space a radiator sees a 4 K background and the sizing is straightforward. In a 600
km orbit your radiator also sees Earth, which radiates about 237 W/m^2 of infrared and
reflects roughly 120 W/m^2 of albedo when sunlit. Those are absorbed heat loads working
against you, so the effective sink temperature is around 255 K rather than near zero, and the
available temperature difference for rejection collapses.

Two numbers to hold onto. At beginning of life with an optical solar reflector at
absorptivity 0.09 you need 1.42 square metres. At end of life, with absorptivity degraded to
0.21 by ultraviolet exposure and contamination, you need 2.06. Size for end of life — 2.2
with margin — and accept that the radiator is oversized initially, which is handled with
heaters or a variable-conductance heat pipe.

Three design choices that will affect this substantially:

Point the radiator away from Earth if you can. An anti-nadir or orbit-normal facing radiator
sees far less Earth infrared and albedo, and can cut the required area by thirty to forty
percent. This usually competes with instrument pointing and antenna placement, which is
exactly the kind of system-level coupling that has to be resolved early.

Use optical solar reflectors or second-surface mirrors rather than white paint. Paint
degrades much faster in ultraviolet, so its end-of-life absorptivity is worse and the
beginning-to-end-of-life spread is larger.

Consider whether 20 C is a real requirement. Radiator area scales roughly with the inverse
of the fourth power of the rejection temperature, so allowing 35 C instead of 20 would cut
the area by nearly a quarter. If the limit is set by a component that could be thermally
isolated and cooled separately, that is often a better trade than growing the radiator.

Finally, budget the heater power. With a radiator sized for end of life you will need
heaters early in the mission and during low-dissipation modes, and that load is frequently
omitted from the power budget until late.

## Attitude Determination and Control

Attitude control orients the spacecraft, and the requirements span an enormous range — from
a few degrees for a communications satellite pointing an antenna at Earth, to
sub-arcsecond stability for an astronomical observatory. Cost and complexity scale steeply
with that requirement.

Determination requires measuring orientation against known references. Sun sensors are
simple and coarse. Magnetometers work only in a planetary magnetic field and give moderate
accuracy. Horizon sensors track the Earth's infrared limb. Star trackers image a field,
identify the pattern against a catalogue, and deliver arcsecond-level attitude — the highest
accuracy available and the standard for demanding missions. Gyroscopes measure angular rate
with high bandwidth and drift over time, so they are always fused with an absolute reference,
typically in a Kalman filter that uses the gyro for short-term propagation and the star
tracker for long-term correction and gyro bias estimation.

Control requires torque, and the available actuators differ in whether they consume
propellant and in whether they can accumulate momentum. Reaction wheels exchange momentum
with the spacecraft body and are the standard for precision pointing, consuming only
electrical power. Their limitation is that external disturbance torques — gravity gradient,
aerodynamic drag, solar pressure, magnetic — are secular, so the wheels spin up over time and
eventually saturate, requiring momentum to be dumped to the environment using magnetic
torquers or thrusters.

Attitude subsystem components:
  sun sensor           coarse, cheap, needs sunlight; ~0.1-1 degree
  magnetometer         needs a planetary field; ~1 degree
  horizon sensor       Earth limb; ~0.1 degree
  star tracker         ~1-10 arcsec; the precision standard
  gyroscope            high rate bandwidth, drifts; fused with absolute sensors
  GPS receiver         position, time, and with multiple antennas, attitude
  reaction wheel       fine torque, no propellant, saturates
  control moment gyro  much higher torque than a wheel; complex, has singularities
  magnetic torquer     no propellant, weak, needs a field; used for desaturation
  thruster             high torque, consumes propellant, coarse
  gravity gradient     passive stabilization to the local vertical; free, coarse
  spin stabilization   passive gyroscopic stiffness; simple and constraining

Disturbance torques determine the momentum accumulation rate and therefore the desaturation
frequency and propellant budget. In low orbits aerodynamic drag dominates, acting on the
offset between the centre of pressure and centre of mass. At higher altitudes solar radiation
pressure takes over, acting on the same geometric offset with respect to the illuminated
area. Gravity gradient torque acts on any non-spherical inertia distribution and is
proportional to the inertia difference and the inverse cube of orbital radius. Magnetic
torque acts on the spacecraft's residual dipole. Minimizing the offset between centre of
pressure and centre of mass at design time directly reduces the propellant needed for
attitude control over the mission.

## Propulsion, Communications, and Reliability

Propulsion is governed by the rocket equation, which makes specific impulse the dominant
figure of merit because propellant mass fraction grows exponentially with required delta-v
divided by exhaust velocity. Chemical systems provide high thrust and modest specific
impulse, suitable for launch, for orbit insertion where a burn must complete quickly, and
for attitude control. Electric propulsion provides very high specific impulse and very low
thrust, so it takes months to accomplish what a chemical stage does in minutes, and it does
so with a fraction of the propellant — a trade that is worthwhile whenever time is
available.

Propulsion options:
  cold gas             Isp 50-70 s; simple, safe, low performance
  monopropellant       hydrazine, Isp ~230 s; reliable, toxic, common for attitude
                       control and small manoeuvres
  bipropellant         Isp 300-450 s; the workhorse for orbit insertion
  solid                Isp ~280 s; simple, high thrust, cannot be throttled or
                       stopped
  Hall thruster        Isp 1500-3000 s; the electric propulsion workhorse
  ion engine           Isp 3000-5000 s; even lower thrust
  green monoprop       reduced toxicity alternatives to hydrazine; simplifies
                       ground handling substantially
  electrothermal       resistojet or arcjet; modest improvement over monoprop
  solar sail           no propellant at all; tiny thrust, unlimited duration

Communications is governed by the link budget, which accounts for every gain and loss from
transmitter to receiver in decibels. Transmit power and antenna gains add; free-space path
loss, which grows as the square of both distance and frequency, dominates the losses;
atmospheric and pointing losses subtract further. The result is compared against the
receiver's noise floor to give a signal-to-noise ratio, and the link margin is what remains
above the minimum required for the chosen modulation and coding.

The path loss scaling with frequency is why deep space missions face such severe data
constraints and why they nonetheless use high frequencies. Higher frequency increases path
loss, and it increases achievable antenna gain for a fixed aperture by even more, since gain
scales as the aperture area divided by wavelength squared. The net effect favours higher
frequency when the aperture is fixed, which is why missions moved from S-band to X-band to
Ka-band, and why optical communication — with wavelengths four orders of magnitude shorter —
offers enormous gain at the cost of much tighter pointing requirements.

Reliability engineering for spacecraft rests on the fact that repair is impossible. The
techniques are redundancy, derating, parts screening, and testing. Redundancy may be
cold-standby, saving power at the cost of switchover time, or hot, providing instant
failover with continuous power. Cross-strapping allows either of two redundant units to
connect to either of two downstream units, protecting against a failure that would otherwise
take out a whole string. Single-point failures are catalogued explicitly and either
eliminated or accepted with justification.

The radiation environment drives parts selection and fault tolerance. Total ionizing dose
accumulates and gradually degrades semiconductors until they fail, which sets mission
lifetime for a given shielding. Displacement damage degrades solar cells and optical
detectors. Single event effects are caused by individual energetic particles: an upset flips
a memory bit and is corrected by error-correcting memory and periodic scrubbing; a latchup
triggers a parasitic conducting path that destroys the part unless power is cycled quickly,
which is why latchup protection circuits monitor current and remove power automatically; a
burnout destroys a power device outright. Rad-hard parts tolerate far more and cost orders of
magnitude more with generations-old performance, which is why many modern missions use
commercial parts with architectural mitigation — redundant computation, watchdogs, frequent
memory scrubbing, and a safe mode the spacecraft falls into whenever anything is
inconsistent.
