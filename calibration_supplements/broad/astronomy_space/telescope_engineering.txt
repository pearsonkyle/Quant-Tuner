## Optical Design and Fundamental Limits

A telescope collects light and forms an image, and its two primary specifications follow directly
from its aperture. Collecting area scales with the square of the diameter, which determines how
faint an object can be detected in a given time. Angular resolution scales inversely with diameter,
set by the diffraction limit, which determines how finely detail can be distinguished. Both push
toward larger apertures, and cost scales considerably faster than diameter, which is why telescope
history is largely a history of making large mirrors affordable.

Refracting designs use lenses and suffer from chromatic aberration — different wavelengths focusing
at different points — which can be partially corrected with multiple elements and never eliminated.
They are also limited in size because a large lens can only be supported at its edge and sags under
its own weight. Reflecting designs use mirrors, have no chromatic aberration at all since reflection
is wavelength-independent, and can be supported across the back, which is why every large telescope
is a reflector.

The standard reflecting configurations trade off differently. A two-mirror arrangement with a
parabolic primary and hyperbolic secondary gives excellent on-axis images and coma that grows
rapidly off axis, limiting the usable field. Adding correction — either a modified primary and
secondary pair or a corrector lens group near the focus — trades some on-axis perfection for a much
wider well-corrected field, which is what survey telescopes need since their value is proportional
to field of view times aperture.

Q: A ground-based telescope with a four-metre aperture routinely delivers images no sharper than a
one-metre telescope would. The reason is:
A. The optics are poorly figured
B. Atmospheric turbulence limits the resolution to the seeing disk, typically around one arcsecond,
   which is far worse than the diffraction limit of either aperture; the larger telescope collects
   more light but does not resolve better without correction
C. The detector is inadequate
D. Diffraction increases with aperture
Reasoning: Above roughly ten to twenty centimetres of aperture at a typical site, atmospheric
turbulence rather than diffraction sets the image size. Larger apertures gain sensitivity and gain
resolution only with adaptive optics, interferometry, or placement above the atmosphere.
Answer: B.

## Detectors and Photon Statistics

The charge-coupled device and its complementary-metal-oxide successors converted astronomy from
photographic to digital and improved quantum efficiency by more than an order of magnitude. A modern
detector converts a large fraction of arriving photons into measurable charge, records the result
linearly across a wide range, and can be read out with very low noise, and each of those properties
matters for different observations.

The noise budget determines what limits an observation and therefore what to change to improve it.
Shot noise from the source scales as the square root of the signal. Sky background contributes its
own shot noise and dominates for faint extended sources. Dark current accumulates with time and is
suppressed by cooling. Read noise is added once per readout and dominates for very short exposures.
Knowing which term dominates tells you whether to expose longer, cool further, bin pixels, or take
fewer longer exposures instead of many short ones.

Detector calibration removes instrumental signatures in a defined order. A bias frame captures the
electronic offset. A dark frame captures thermally generated charge over the exposure time. A flat
field captures the pixel-to-pixel sensitivity variation and the vignetting and dust shadows of the
optical path. Applying these in the right order, with the right scaling, is routine and mechanical,
and getting it wrong produces subtle photometric errors that survive into the science.

## Adaptive Optics and Atmospheric Correction

The atmosphere degrades ground-based images because turbulent cells with slightly different
temperatures have slightly different refractive indices, so a wavefront arriving flat from a distant
source is corrugated by the time it reaches the telescope. The result is a blurred and constantly
changing image, characterized by the seeing — the resulting image size, typically around one
arcsecond at a good site and better at the best ones.

Adaptive optics measures the wavefront distortion and corrects it in real time with a deformable
mirror. A wavefront sensor samples the incoming wavefront hundreds to thousands of times per second,
a control computer computes the correction, and actuators behind a thin mirror deform it to
compensate. The correction must be faster than the turbulence evolves, which sets the loop rate, and
the number of actuators must match the number of turbulent cells across the aperture, which sets the
system's complexity as the square of the diameter.

The system needs a bright reference source in the same direction as the target, and most targets do
not have one nearby. Laser guide stars solve this by exciting sodium atoms in the upper atmosphere
to create an artificial reference, which works for the higher-order correction and cannot sense
overall tilt, since the laser's own outbound path shifts with it. A faint natural star is therefore
still required for tilt, which is a much less restrictive requirement.

  isoplanatic angle    the angle over which the correction is valid; small,

  wavefront sensor     samples the distortion at high rate

  actuator count       scales as the square of the diameter
  the wavelength dependence  correction is far easier in the infrared, which is
                       why adaptive optics matured there first
  natural guide star   a bright reference near the target; rarely available
  laser guide star     an artificial reference in the sodium layer

  multi-conjugate systems  several deformable mirrors correcting different
                       altitudes; widens the corrected field
  interferometry       combining separated apertures for resolution set by their
                       separation rather than their size; extraordinary resolution
                       and a very limited field

## Mounts, Pointing, and Space Constraints

A telescope must point accurately and track the sky's apparent motion. Equatorial mounts have one
axis aligned with the Earth's rotation, so tracking is a single constant-rate motion and the field
does not rotate. Altitude-azimuth mounts are mechanically simpler and far more practical at large
size, and they require coordinated motion on both axes plus a third rotation to counteract field
rotation, which is why they became feasible only with computer control.

Pointing accuracy is achieved through a pointing model rather than through mechanical perfection.
The model fits a set of terms — axis misalignment, non-perpendicularity, flexure of the tube with
elevation, encoder eccentricity — to observations of many stars across the sky, and the resulting
corrections bring a mount with visible mechanical imperfections to arcsecond pointing. The model
must be re-derived after any mechanical change, which is a maintenance obligation that is
occasionally forgotten with confusing consequences.

Space telescopes trade the atmosphere's degradation for a set of severe constraints. Mass and volume
are limited by the launch vehicle, which drives segmented and deployable designs. Thermal control is
harder without convection and matters more, since optics must hold their figure across the orbital
temperature cycle. There is no servicing for most missions, so redundancy and conservative design
replace repair. And pointing must be achieved without any ground contact, using reaction wheels and
star trackers, with the accumulated momentum periodically dumped.

  re-derivation        required after mechanical changes

  the dome contribution  local turbulence from the enclosure and from the

  orbit selection      low Earth orbit for access, Lagrange points for thermal
                       stability and continuous observation

## Spectrographs, Calibration, and Data

Spectroscopy disperses light by wavelength and extracts far more physical information than imaging:
composition, temperature, density, ionization state, radial velocity, and magnetic field strength
all leave signatures in a spectrum. The instrument's resolving power — wavelength divided by the
smallest resolvable wavelength difference — determines which of these are accessible, and it trades
directly against the light reaching each detector element.

The standard design disperses light with a grating and images the result. A slit or fibre defines
what enters, and the slit width trades spectral resolution against throughput. Echelle designs use
high orders and a cross-disperser to record a wide wavelength range at high resolution
simultaneously, at the cost of a complex two-dimensional format that requires careful extraction.
Integral field units capture a spectrum at every point in a small field, which is the most
information-rich configuration and produces correspondingly large data volumes.

Wavelength calibration ties detector position to physical wavelength using a source of known
emission lines, and its stability determines what velocity precision is achievable. The
extraordinary precision needed for exoplanet detection by radial velocity — of order metres per
second, which is a shift far smaller than a detector pixel — is achieved through simultaneous
reference sources, extreme environmental stabilization, and careful modelling of the instrument
profile. This is a case where the calibration strategy rather than the optics is the instrument's
defining feature.
