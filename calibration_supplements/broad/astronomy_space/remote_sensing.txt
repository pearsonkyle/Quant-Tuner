## Physical Basis

Remote sensing infers properties of a surface from electromagnetic radiation measured at a
distance. The physical basis is that materials interact with radiation in
wavelength-dependent ways determined by their composition and structure, so the spectrum of
reflected or emitted radiation carries diagnostic information about what produced it.

Passive sensing measures naturally available radiation — reflected sunlight in the visible
and near infrared, or thermal emission from the surface itself. It requires no transmitter
and is therefore simple and power-efficient, and it is constrained by illumination and by
atmospheric transparency. Active sensing supplies its own illumination, which permits
operation at night, control of the wavelength and polarization, and measurement of the return
timing, at the cost of substantial power.

Atmospheric windows determine which wavelengths are usable from orbit at all. Water vapour,
carbon dioxide, and ozone absorb strongly in specific bands, so the atmosphere is transparent
only in particular ranges. The visible and near infrared window supports optical imaging; a
thermal window near 10 micrometres supports temperature measurement; microwave wavelengths
pass through clouds and precipitation almost unimpeded, which is why radar is the only
technique that works reliably in persistently cloudy regions.

Spectral regions and what each reveals:
  visible 0.4-0.7 um      colour; vegetation appears green because chlorophyll
                          absorbs red and blue
  near infrared 0.7-1.3   vegetation reflects strongly; the sharpest diagnostic
                          feature in the whole spectrum for plants
  shortwave IR 1.3-3      moisture content, minerals, burn severity
  thermal IR 8-14         emitted radiation; surface temperature
  microwave 1 mm - 1 m    penetrates cloud; sensitive to moisture, roughness,
                          and structure
  the red edge            the abrupt reflectance rise from red to near infrared;
                          the basis of nearly every vegetation index
  water                   absorbs strongly beyond the visible; appears very dark
                          in the near infrared, which makes water masking easy
  snow and cloud          both bright in visible; snow is dark in shortwave IR
                          while cloud is not, which discriminates them

The red edge deserves emphasis because it does most of the work in vegetation remote
sensing. Chlorophyll absorbs red light for photosynthesis while the internal cellular
structure of leaves scatters near infrared strongly, producing a reflectance jump of a factor
of five or more across a narrow wavelength range. Healthy vegetation therefore has a large
difference between near infrared and red reflectance, stressed vegetation has less, and bare
soil has almost none — which is exactly what normalized difference vegetation index measures,
and why a two-band ratio captures so much about plant condition.

Q: Which sensor type can image a persistently cloudy tropical region on a routine schedule?
A. Optical multispectral   B. Thermal infrared   C. Synthetic aperture radar   D. Hyperspectral
Reasoning: Cloud is opaque at visible, near infrared, and thermal infrared wavelengths.
Microwave radiation at the wavelengths used by imaging radar passes through cloud and most
precipitation with little attenuation, and radar supplies its own illumination so it also
works at night. This is why deforestation monitoring in the tropics relies heavily on radar.
Answer: C.

## Resolution, Sensors, and Orbits

Four kinds of resolution characterize a remote sensing system, and they trade against one
another because the underlying constraint is the number of photons available per measurement.

Spatial resolution is the ground size of one pixel. Spectral resolution is the number and
width of the wavelength bands. Radiometric resolution is the number of distinguishable
brightness levels. Temporal resolution is how often the same location is observed. Improving
any one generally degrades another: narrower spectral bands collect fewer photons and so
require larger pixels or coarser radiometric precision, and a narrower swath for higher
spatial resolution means a longer revisit interval.

The distinction between spatial resolution and pixel size is worth stating because they are
often conflated. Pixel size is the sampling interval; resolution is the smallest
distinguishable feature, governed by the optics and the point spread function. An image
resampled to a finer pixel grid has smaller pixels and no additional resolution. Conversely,
objects smaller than a pixel can still be detected if they are bright enough — a small fire
raises the whole pixel's thermal signal measurably — which is sub-pixel detection and is
distinct from resolving.

Resolution and orbit characteristics:
  spatial resolution     ground sample distance; sub-metre commercial, 10-30 m
                         for public multispectral, ~1 km for daily global
  spectral resolution    panchromatic (1 broad band), multispectral (3-15),
                         hyperspectral (100s of narrow contiguous bands)
  radiometric            bit depth; 8-bit is 256 levels, 12-16 bit is standard now
  temporal               revisit interval; constellation size drives it
  swath width            trades against spatial resolution for a fixed data rate
  sun-synchronous orbit  ~700-800 km, near-polar, retrograde; the nodal precession
                         from Earth's oblateness matches the orbital motion of
                         Earth about the Sun, fixing the local solar time of every
                         equator crossing
  consistent lighting    the reason sun-synchronous orbits dominate; comparable
                         illumination across dates makes change detection valid
  geostationary          fixed over one longitude; continuous hemispheric coverage
                         at coarse resolution; the weather satellite orbit
  pushbroom              a linear detector array imaging as the platform moves;
                         the standard modern design
  whiskbroom             a scanning mirror sweeping across track; older, mechanical

Sun-synchronous orbits are the workhorse of Earth observation for a reason worth
understanding. Earth's equatorial bulge causes an orbital plane to precess, and the rate
depends on inclination. Choosing a retrograde inclination near 98 degrees makes that
precession equal to about one degree per day, matching Earth's motion around the Sun, so the
orbital plane maintains a fixed angle to the Sun. Every equator crossing then occurs at the
same local solar time, giving consistent illumination geometry across the mission — which is
what makes comparing images from different dates meaningful, since a difference in brightness
reflects a change on the ground rather than a change in sun angle.

## Optical and Thermal Imaging

Multispectral imaging measures reflectance in a handful of broad bands chosen to capture
diagnostic features. Band combinations displayed as false colour make specific properties
visible: assigning near infrared to red produces the classic composite where healthy
vegetation is bright red, water is black, and urban areas are cyan, which conveys far more
than a true-colour rendering.

Spectral indices combine bands arithmetically to isolate a property while cancelling
nuisance variation. Normalized difference indices — the difference of two bands divided by
their sum — are the standard form, and the normalization is what makes them robust: it
cancels multiplicative effects such as illumination differences and topographic shading that
would otherwise dominate a simple band difference.

Thermal infrared measures emitted radiation, which relates to temperature through the
Stefan-Boltzmann and Planck relations. The complication is emissivity: the measured radiance
depends on both temperature and how efficiently the surface emits, and these cannot be
separated from a single band. Multi-band thermal measurements allow partial separation, and
in practice emissivity is often assumed from a land cover classification, which introduces a
systematic error where the assumption is wrong.

Common indices and their use:
  NDVI      (NIR - Red)/(NIR + Red);  vegetation vigour; saturates in dense canopy
  EVI       enhanced vegetation index; corrects atmosphere and soil, resists
            saturation in dense canopy
  NDWI      water content; several variants for open water versus leaf moisture
  NDBI      built-up areas
  NBR       normalized burn ratio; NIR and SWIR, for fire severity
  NDSI      snow; exploits snow being bright in visible and dark in SWIR
  SAVI      soil-adjusted; corrects the soil background in sparse canopy
  LST       land surface temperature; needs emissivity and atmospheric correction
  albedo    broadband reflectance; a climate-relevant quantity
  tasselled cap  a rotation into brightness, greenness, and wetness axes

NDVI's saturation deserves specific mention because it is routinely ignored. The index rises
steeply as vegetation cover increases from bare soil and then flattens, so in dense canopy
substantial increases in biomass produce almost no change in NDVI. Studies comparing dense
forest sites using NDVI are therefore comparing a metric with little remaining sensitivity,
and the enhanced vegetation index or a near infrared reflectance measure is a better choice
in that regime.

## Radar and Lidar

Synthetic aperture radar transmits microwave pulses and measures the returned echo. Its
defining trick is in the name: a small physical antenna moving along the flight path
synthesizes a much larger effective aperture by coherently combining returns from many
positions, achieving along-track resolution far finer than the antenna size would allow. The
resolution in that direction is, remarkably, independent of range and approximately half the
physical antenna length.

Radar measures surface roughness and dielectric properties rather than colour, so it sees
entirely different things than optical sensors. Smooth surfaces reflect away from the sensor
and appear dark, which is why calm water is black in radar imagery. Rough surfaces scatter
back and appear bright. Corner geometries — a building meeting the ground, a ship on water —
produce double-bounce returns and appear very bright. Volume scattering within a canopy
produces intermediate returns that depend on structure. Moisture strongly affects the
dielectric constant and therefore the return, which makes radar sensitive to soil moisture
and to flooding under vegetation.

Interferometric radar exploits the phase of the return rather than only its amplitude.
Comparing the phase between two acquisitions from slightly different positions yields
topography; comparing between two dates from the same position yields surface displacement at
millimetre precision. This is how ground subsidence, volcanic inflation, and post-earthquake
deformation are measured over wide areas, and the precision is startling given that it is
achieved from orbit.

Active sensing techniques:
  SAR resolution        along-track ~ half the antenna length, independent of range
  bright in SAR         rough surfaces, corner reflectors, urban structure
  dark in SAR           smooth surfaces; calm water, roads, dry sand
  polarization          HH, HV, VH, VV; the cross-polarized channels are sensitive
                        to volume scattering and therefore to canopy structure
  band choice           X-band interacts with the canopy top, C-band with the
                        canopy, L-band penetrates to the ground and to biomass
  speckle               coherent imaging produces multiplicative noise; reduced by
                        multi-looking or filtering, at a resolution cost
  InSAR                 topography from two positions
  DInSAR                displacement from two dates; millimetre sensitivity
  coherence             decorrelation indicates surface change; useful in itself
  lidar                 laser ranging; direct 3D point clouds
  full waveform         records the whole return; canopy structure and ground
                        beneath vegetation in one measurement
  bathymetric lidar     green wavelength penetrates shallow clear water

Lidar's distinctive capability is measuring vertical structure directly. A laser pulse
striking a forest returns energy from the canopy top, from intermediate foliage, and from the
ground, and recording those separate returns yields both a terrain model beneath the canopy
and a description of the vegetation structure above it. No passive optical sensor can do
this, which is why lidar is the standard for forest biomass, for archaeological survey under
vegetation, and for high-precision terrain modelling.

## Correction, Analysis, and Applications

Preprocessing determines whether analysis is meaningful, and the standard chain addresses
distinct problems. Radiometric calibration converts raw sensor counts to physical radiance
using calibration coefficients. Geometric correction registers the image to a map projection,
using orbital and attitude data plus, for precision, ground control points. Orthorectification
additionally removes terrain-induced displacement, which is essential in mountainous areas
where relief displacement can exceed many pixels. Atmospheric correction converts
top-of-atmosphere radiance to surface reflectance. Cloud and shadow masking removes
unusable pixels, and doing it aggressively matters because a few undetected cloud edges
corrupt an index calculation badly.

Classification assigns each pixel or object to a category. Unsupervised methods cluster the
spectral space and require the analyst to label the resulting clusters. Supervised methods
train on labelled examples, and modern practice uses gradient-boosted trees or random forests
on spectral, temporal, and textural features, or convolutional networks that exploit spatial
context directly. Object-based analysis segments the image into homogeneous regions first and
classifies those, which suits high-resolution imagery where individual pixels are smaller
than the objects of interest and per-pixel classification produces a noisy speckled result.

Accuracy assessment must use independent reference data, and the confusion matrix is the
right reporting format because a single accuracy number hides the important structure.
Producer's accuracy reports how much of each true class was correctly identified; user's
accuracy reports how much of each predicted class is correct. These differ, and which matters
depends on the application — a flood map used for emergency response cares about missing
flooded areas, while one used for insurance cares about false positives.

Analysis and applications:
  confusion matrix       per-class errors; report it rather than overall accuracy
  producer's accuracy    recall per class
  user's accuracy        precision per class
  spatial autocorrelation  nearby pixels are correlated, so randomly split
                         validation samples overstate accuracy; use spatially
                         separated validation blocks
  time series analysis   trajectory-based change detection; far more robust than
                         image differencing
  data fusion            optical plus radar covers the cloud gaps and adds
                         structural information
  agriculture            crop type, yield forecasting, irrigation, stress
  forestry               deforestation alerts, biomass, fire severity
  hydrology              flood extent, snow cover, soil moisture, reservoir level
  urban                  expansion, heat islands, subsidence via InSAR
  disaster response      rapid mapping; the constraint is usually tasking and
                         delivery latency, not sensor capability
  climate                long consistent records; continuity across sensor
                         generations requires careful cross-calibration

Spatial autocorrelation in validation deserves the final emphasis because it silently
inflates reported accuracy throughout the literature. Neighbouring pixels are highly
correlated, so a validation sample drawn randomly from the same scene as the training data
contains points that are effectively duplicates of training points, and the resulting accuracy
estimate describes interpolation rather than generalization. Validating on spatially
separated blocks, or on a different scene or date entirely, gives a far lower and far more
honest number — and the gap between the two is a good measure of how much the model has
learned about the phenomenon rather than about the particular image.
