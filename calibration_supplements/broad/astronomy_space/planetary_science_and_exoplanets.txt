## Solar System Formation and Architecture

The solar system formed about 4.57 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of a
molecular cloud fragment. Conservation of angular momentum flattened the collapsing
material into a disc, with most of the mass falling to the centre to form the Sun and the
remainder accreting into planets. This nebular hypothesis explains the system's most
obvious regularities: the planets orbit in nearly the same plane, in the same direction as
the Sun's rotation, in nearly circular orbits.

Composition varies with distance because temperature did. Inside the snow line — the radius
where water ice could condense, around three astronomical units in the early solar system —
only metals and silicates were solid, so the planets that formed there are small and rocky.
Beyond it, ice was abundant and available solid mass was several times greater, so cores
grew large enough and fast enough to capture hydrogen and helium from the nebula before it
dispersed. The gas giants had to form quickly, within a few million years, because that is
how long the gas lasted.

Planetary migration has revised the picture substantially. Planets exchange angular
momentum with the disc and with each other and do not necessarily remain where they formed.
The Nice model proposes that the giant planets formed in a more compact configuration and
migrated outward, and that a resonance crossing between Jupiter and Saturn destabilized the
outer system, scattering small bodies and producing the Late Heavy Bombardment recorded in
lunar cratering. The Grand Tack hypothesis proposes that Jupiter migrated inward to about
1.5 astronomical units before Saturn's growth reversed its motion, which would explain
Mars's small size and the asteroid belt's low mass and mixed composition.

## Terrestrial Planets and Small Bodies

The terrestrial planets are differentiated — dense material sank to form a core, lighter
material rose to form a mantle and crust — which required them to be molten early, heated
by accretional impacts and short-lived radioactive isotopes. Their subsequent divergence
from one another is a study in how initial conditions and size determine long-term fate.

Mercury is nearly all core, with an anomalously high density suggesting that a giant impact
stripped much of its mantle. It has no substantial atmosphere, extreme surface temperature
swings, and a weak magnetic field indicating a partly molten core. Venus is nearly Earth's
twin in size and mass and utterly different in outcome: a runaway greenhouse driven by
carbon dioxide has left it at 462 degrees Celsius with a surface pressure 92 times Earth's,
hot enough to melt lead, with sulfuric acid clouds and no water. Mars is small enough that
it cooled quickly, lost its internal dynamo and hence its magnetic field, and had its
atmosphere gradually stripped by the solar wind — leaving abundant evidence of ancient
liquid water on a surface where liquid water is now impossible.

The comparison between Venus, Earth, and Mars is the most instructive in planetary
science because it isolates the variables. All three formed from similar material at
similar times. Venus was slightly too close, so water stayed vapour, and water vapour is a
greenhouse gas, so temperature rose, so more water evaporated — a positive feedback that
ran away and eventually allowed ultraviolet radiation to dissociate the water and hydrogen
to escape. Mars was slightly too small, so it lost its heat and its field. Earth retained
liquid water, which enabled the carbonate-silicate cycle to regulate carbon dioxide over
geological time, providing the negative feedback that has kept the climate within habitable
bounds for billions of years despite a Sun that has brightened by about thirty percent.

Small bodies preserve the early record because they were never heated enough to
differentiate or resurface. Asteroids are rocky and metallic bodies concentrated between
Mars and Jupiter, classified by spectral type into carbonaceous, silicaceous, and metallic
groups whose distribution with distance reflects the original temperature gradient. Comets
are ice-rich bodies from the outer system that develop comae and tails when solar heating
sublimates their ices, with the tails pointing away from the Sun regardless of the direction
of motion — the dust tail curved by orbital dynamics and the ion tail driven straight back
by the solar wind.

Chondrites are the most scientifically valuable objects available because they are
essentially unaltered samples of the solar nebula. Carbonaceous chondrites contain water,
organic compounds including amino acids, and calcium-aluminium inclusions that are the
oldest solids in the solar system and define its age. That amino acids arrive from space
in unaltered meteorites is directly relevant to origin-of-life questions, since it
establishes that prebiotic organic chemistry occurs abiotically and is delivered to
planetary surfaces.

## Atmospheres and Surfaces

Whether a planet retains an atmosphere is a competition between thermal escape and gravity.
A gas is retained when its thermal velocity is well below the escape velocity — the usual
rule of thumb being that the escape velocity must exceed roughly six times the
root-mean-square molecular speed for retention over the age of the solar system. Because
thermal speed depends on molecular mass, light gases escape preferentially: Earth retains
nitrogen, oxygen, and carbon dioxide, and loses hydrogen and helium continuously. This is
why the giant planets, cold and massive, retain the primordial hydrogen and helium that the
terrestrial planets could not.

Atmospheric escape has several channels beyond simple thermal loss. Hydrodynamic escape
occurs when the upper atmosphere is heated enough that it flows outward in bulk, dragging
heavier species with it — the likely history of Venus's water. Non-thermal escape includes
photochemical processes, charge exchange, and sputtering by the solar wind. That last one
is why a magnetic field matters: Earth's field deflects the solar wind, while Mars, having
lost its field around four billion years ago, has had its atmosphere eroded steadily since,
a process directly measured by orbiting spacecraft.

Greenhouse warming determines surface temperature far more than distance from the Sun
alone. An atmosphere transparent to incoming visible light and opaque to outgoing infrared
traps heat, raising surface temperature above the equilibrium value. Earth's greenhouse
effect is about 33 degrees, without which the surface would average about minus 18 degrees
Celsius and be frozen. Venus's is over 500 degrees. The effect scales nonlinearly with
greenhouse gas abundance and can run away when a feedback — evaporating water, sublimating
carbon dioxide, melting permafrost — amplifies an initial perturbation.

Tidal heating deserves emphasis because it decouples internal heat from planet size and
therefore extends the possible locations of liquid water far beyond the conventional
habitable zone. A moon in an eccentric orbit around a giant planet is flexed as the tidal
force varies, and the resulting friction generates heat. Io is the most volcanically active
body in the solar system for this reason despite being smaller than Earth's Moon. Europa,
Enceladus, and probably Titan and several others maintain subsurface liquid water oceans
under ice shells, and Enceladus vents that water into space through south polar plumes that
spacecraft have flown through and analyzed, finding salts, silica, and organic molecules.

## Exoplanet Detection

More than five thousand planets are now confirmed around other stars, and essentially
everything known about them comes from a small number of detection methods, each with
distinct sensitivities that shape what we can find and therefore what we think is common.

The transit method detects the periodic dimming as a planet crosses its star's disc. The
depth gives the planet's radius relative to the star, the period gives the orbital distance,
and repeated observations confirm the signal. It requires the orbit to be nearly edge-on
from our viewpoint, which is geometrically improbable — roughly the stellar radius divided
by the orbital radius, under one percent for an Earth-like orbit — so it finds a small
fraction of the planets present, but surveys of hundreds of thousands of stars overcome that
statistically. Transits are strongly biased toward large planets on short orbits, since
those produce deep signals and repeat frequently.

The radial velocity method detects the star's reflex motion as the planet orbits their
common centre of mass, measured as a periodic Doppler shift. It gives the orbital period
and a minimum mass — minimum because the measured velocity is only the line-of-sight
component and the orbital inclination is unknown. Combined with a transit, which constrains
inclination to near ninety degrees, radial velocity gives a true mass, and mass with radius
gives density, which is the only route to knowing whether a planet is rocky, icy, or
gaseous.

Detection methods and their biases:

  astrometry          the star's positional wobble; sensitive to massive planets

Transmission spectroscopy is what turns a detected planet into a characterized one. During
transit, a small fraction of starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere, and molecular
absorption imprints features on the spectrum. Comparing spectra in and out of transit
reveals atmospheric composition. The technique works best for large planets with low
surface gravity and hot, extended atmospheres, and it is severely limited by high-altitude
clouds and hazes, which flatten the spectrum by blocking the light path before it reaches
the molecular absorbers. Detections of water, carbon dioxide, methane, and sodium in giant
planet atmospheres are now routine; comparable measurements for small rocky planets remain
at the edge of current capability.

What you get. The depth gives the ratio of planet radius to stellar radius, so a planet
radius follows if you know the star's radius. The period comes from repetition. The
duration and the ingress/egress shape together give the impact parameter and, through the
transit's relation to orbital speed, the ratio of semi-major axis to stellar radius — which
notably yields the stellar density directly, independent of stellar models, which is a
useful cross-check. With the semi-major axis and the star's luminosity you get an
equilibrium temperature.

What you do not get. Mass, and therefore density, and therefore whether the object is
rocky, water-rich, or a small gas envelope. This matters because the radius alone is
genuinely ambiguous in the interesting range: a planet of 2.5 Earth radii could be a dense
rocky world, a water world, or a rocky core with a percent or two of hydrogen envelope, and
those are entirely different objects. The envelope dominates the radius while contributing
almost nothing to the mass.

Two important caveats. Your planet radius inherits the fractional uncertainty of the
stellar radius, which for a poorly characterized star can be twenty percent or more — so
that is often the dominant error, not the photometry. And transit-like signals have
astrophysical false positives: an eclipsing binary blended with your target in the same
aperture, or a background eclipsing binary, reproduces a shallow periodic dip. Validation
requires checking the transit shape for the V-profile typical of grazing binaries,
searching for secondary eclipses and ellipsoidal variation, and ideally obtaining
high-resolution imaging to rule out blends.

If mass is what you need and the star is too faint or too active for radial velocity,
transit timing variations are the alternative — but they require a multi-planet system with
detectable mutual perturbations, ideally near a mean-motion resonance.

## Exoplanet Populations, Habitability, and Astrobiology

The populations discovered have overturned expectations formed on a sample size of one
system. Hot Jupiters — giant planets orbiting in days — were the first found and were not
predicted, since giant planets cannot form that close and must have migrated. Super-Earths
and sub-Neptunes, with radii between Earth's and Neptune's, are the most common type of
planet found and have no solar system representative at all. Planets are extremely common:
statistical analysis indicates most stars host planets, and small planets outnumber large
ones substantially.

The radius valley is one of the more informative findings. The distribution of small planet
radii shows a deficit around 1.8 Earth radii, separating a population of likely-rocky
super-Earths below from a population of sub-Neptunes with hydrogen envelopes above. The
explanation is atmospheric loss: photoevaporation by stellar ultraviolet radiation, or
core-powered mass loss from the planet's own residual heat, strips thin envelopes
completely while thick ones survive, so intermediate cases are unstable and the population
is bimodal. This is a case where a population-level statistical feature diagnoses an
individual-planet physical process.

The habitable zone is conventionally defined as the range of orbital distances where liquid
water could exist on a rocky planet's surface given an Earth-like atmosphere. It is a
useful first filter and a crude one. Its boundaries depend on assumed atmospheric
composition and cloud behaviour, both poorly constrained. It ignores subsurface oceans
maintained by tidal heating, which vastly expands the volume where liquid water exists. And
being in the zone is necessary rather than sufficient — Venus and Mars both sit at or near
its edges and neither is habitable.

Biosignatures are the observational target of astrobiology, and the difficulty is that
almost every candidate has abiotic explanations. Molecular oxygen in an atmosphere is
suggestive because it is reactive and should be consumed without continuous replenishment,
but photolysis of water followed by hydrogen escape produces oxygen abiotically, which is
precisely what may have happened on Venus. The more robust approach is chemical
disequilibrium — the simultaneous presence of gases that should react with each other, such
as oxygen and methane — since maintaining that requires continuous production. Even then,
the inference is statistical and rests on being able to enumerate abiotic sources, which is
a claim about the completeness of our knowledge of planetary chemistry.

The honest state of the field is that no biosignature has been detected and the
observational capability for rocky planets around Sun-like stars does not yet exist. Current
instruments can characterize the atmospheres of giant planets and, with difficulty, of
rocky planets around small cool stars. The next generation of ground-based extremely large
telescopes and dedicated space missions is designed for the harder case. Meanwhile the
Fermi paradox — the tension between the apparent commonness of planets and the absence of
any evidence for other technological civilizations — remains entirely unresolved, with
proposed explanations spanning rare abiogenesis, rare complex life, rare technology, short
technological lifetimes, and detection difficulty, none of which are currently
distinguishable by evidence.
