## The Solar Interior and the Dynamo

The Sun is a self-gravitating ball of plasma in hydrostatic equilibrium, with pressure balancing
gravity at every radius, powered by hydrogen fusion in a core occupying roughly the inner quarter of
its radius. Energy generated there takes a very long time to reach the surface — hundreds of
thousands of years by random-walk diffusion of radiation through the interior — which means the
light we see was produced long before anything currently happening in the core.

The interior has two distinct energy transport regimes. The radiative zone, extending outward from
the core, transports energy by photon diffusion through material too opaque for convection to
begin. The convection zone, occupying the outer third by radius, transports energy by bulk motion
because the opacity rises enough that the temperature gradient exceeds the threshold for convective
instability. The boundary between them, the tachocline, is a thin shear layer where the rotation
profile changes character, and it is thought to be where the magnetic field is generated and stored.

Helioseismology made the interior observable. Sound waves trapped inside the Sun produce surface
oscillations with millions of distinguishable modes, and each mode samples a different depth range,
so inverting the observed frequencies recovers the internal sound speed, density, and rotation
profile. This is how the depth of the convection zone, the internal rotation, and the composition
were measured, and it is the technique that resolved the solar neutrino problem by confirming that
the standard solar model was correct and the neutrino physics was incomplete.

  differential rotation  the equator rotates faster than the poles at the surface

Q: Sunlight reaching Earth was generated by fusion in the core roughly:
A. Eight minutes ago
B. Hundreds of thousands of years ago; the eight minutes is only the travel time from the surface,
   while the energy took far longer to diffuse outward through the interior
C. One year ago
D. At the Sun's formation
Reasoning: The eight-minute figure is the light travel time from the photosphere to Earth. Energy
produced in the core random-walks through the radiative zone over a timescale usually quoted in the
hundreds of thousands of years, so the two numbers describe entirely different stages.
Answer: B.

## The Solar Atmosphere and the Corona

Above the convection zone the density falls steeply and the atmosphere divides into layers defined
by their temperature behaviour. The photosphere is the visible surface, at roughly five thousand
eight hundred kelvin, and it is a surface only in the sense that it is where the gas becomes
transparent. The chromosphere above it is thinner and hotter. The transition region is a very thin
layer where the temperature jumps by two orders of magnitude. The corona above that reaches millions
of kelvin.

The coronal heating problem is that the temperature rises outward from the surface, which appears to
violate the second law until one recognizes that the energy is not being transported thermally. The
corona is heated by magnetic energy — either by waves propagating along field lines and dissipating,
or by many small reconnection events releasing stored magnetic stress, or both — and the outstanding
question is which mechanism dominates rather than whether magnetism is responsible.

The corona's structure is entirely magnetic. Closed field regions trap plasma and appear bright in
extreme ultraviolet and X-ray images as loops. Open field regions allow plasma to escape and appear
dark, which is what a coronal hole is. Because the plasma is highly conductive, it is frozen to the
field, and this single fact — that magnetic field and plasma move together — explains most of the
observed structure and dynamics of the solar atmosphere.

  frozen-in flux       plasma and field move together in a highly conducting
                       medium

## The Activity Cycle and Sunspots

Sunspots are regions where strong magnetic field suppresses convection, reducing the local energy
transport and leaving a cooler and therefore darker area. They are not cold in absolute terms — a
sunspot in isolation would be brighter than most stars — but they are a thousand kelvin or more
cooler than their surroundings and the contrast makes them appear black.

Their number rises and falls with a period averaging about eleven years, and the pattern within each
cycle is regular enough to be diagnostic. Spots appear first at mid latitudes and emerge
progressively closer to the equator as the cycle advances, producing the characteristic butterfly
pattern when latitude is plotted against time. The magnetic polarity of leading spots is opposite in
the two hemispheres and reverses at each cycle minimum, which means the true magnetic period is
twenty-two years and the eleven-year sunspot cycle is its unsigned half.

Cycle amplitude varies substantially and is not well predicted. The historical record includes the
Maunder minimum, a period of roughly seventy years in the seventeenth century with almost no spots,
which coincided with a cool period in Europe and is the main evidence for solar influence on
terrestrial climate on that timescale. The magnitude of that influence is far smaller than the
current radiative forcing from greenhouse gases, and conflating the two is a common error.

  the eleven-year cycle  average period, with substantial variation
  butterfly diagram    spot latitude drifting equatorward through the cycle
  polarity reversal    leading-spot polarity flips each cycle
  the twenty-two year period  the true magnetic cycle
  cycle amplitude      variable and poorly predicted
  Maunder minimum      seventy years with almost no spots
  the climate question  a real but small effect compared with greenhouse forcing
  total solar irradiance  varies by about a tenth of a percent over the cycle
  ultraviolet variation  much larger fractionally; drives upper atmospheric
                       chemistry and thermospheric density
  active regions       the magnetic complexes containing spots; the source of
                       flares and eruptions
  faculae              bright magnetic regions that partially compensate the
                       darkening from spots, which is why total irradiance is
                       slightly higher at solar maximum despite more dark spots

## Flares, Eruptions, and the Solar Wind

A solar flare is a sudden release of magnetic energy through reconnection, in which field lines of
opposite direction are pushed together, break, and reconnect in a lower-energy configuration. The
released energy accelerates particles and heats plasma, producing emission across the entire
spectrum from radio to gamma rays within minutes. Flares are classified logarithmically by their
soft X-ray peak flux, with each class an order of magnitude above the previous.

Coronal mass ejections are distinct from flares though frequently associated with them. An ejection
expels billions of tonnes of magnetized plasma into interplanetary space at speeds from hundreds to
thousands of kilometres per second. It is the ejection rather than the flare that produces the
severe geomagnetic effects at Earth, because it delivers mass and magnetic field rather than only
photons, and the distinction matters for forecasting: the flare's radiation arrives in eight minutes
and the ejection takes one to three days.

The solar wind is the continuous outflow of plasma from the corona, present at all times and
divided into a slow component from the streamer belt and a fast component from coronal holes. It
carries the Sun's magnetic field outward into a spiral shape set by the outflow speed and the solar
rotation, and it inflates the heliosphere — the bubble in which the Sun's influence dominates over
the interstellar medium, extending well past the outer planets.

  magnetic reconnection  field lines breaking and reconnecting at lower energy

                       ejection
  solar energetic particles  accelerated protons and ions arriving in tens of
                       minutes; the radiation hazard
  solar wind           continuous outflow; slow and fast components
  the fast wind        from coronal holes; roughly twice the slow wind speed
  the spiral field     outflow plus rotation winds the field into a spiral
  heliosphere          the bubble where solar influence dominates
  termination shock and heliopause  where the wind slows and where it meets the

  the southward field question  a geomagnetic storm's severity depends most on
                       whether the arriving field points southward, opposing
                       Earth's, which is not predictable until the structure is
                       measured about an hour upstream

## Space Weather Effects and Forecasting

Space weather is the practical consequence of solar activity for technology and for people in
space, and its effects sort into a few mechanisms. Enhanced radiation damages spacecraft
electronics and poses a dose hazard to astronauts and, to a much smaller degree, to passengers on
polar flights. Charged particles cause spacecraft charging and single-event upsets in memory and
logic. Geomagnetic storms drive currents in the ionosphere that induce currents in long conductors
at the ground.

Geomagnetically induced currents are the effect with the largest potential consequence. A rapidly
changing magnetic field induces quasi-direct currents in power transmission lines, which flow
through transformer neutrals and drive the cores into saturation. Saturated transformers draw large
reactive currents, heat up, and can be permanently damaged, and the resulting reactive power demand
can collapse a grid's voltage. The 1989 Quebec blackout is the canonical example, and the risk is
highest at high latitudes and in regions with resistive geology.

Forecasting is limited by physics rather than by effort. The most consequential parameter — the
orientation of the magnetic field in an arriving ejection — cannot currently be measured until the
structure passes a spacecraft upstream of Earth, giving roughly thirty to sixty minutes of warning.
Longer-range prediction relies on modelling the eruption's propagation and on remote sensing of the
source region, and its skill is modest. The practical response is therefore preparedness and
mitigation procedures rather than reliance on accurate prediction.
