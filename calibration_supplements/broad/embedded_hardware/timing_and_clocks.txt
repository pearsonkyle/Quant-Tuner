## Oscillators and Frequency Stability

Every digital system runs on a clock, and the clock's quality bounds what the system can do. An
oscillator's specification has several distinct parameters that are routinely confused: frequency
accuracy is how far the output is from nominal at a reference condition; frequency stability is how
much it moves with temperature, supply, load, and time; and short-term stability describes
fluctuation within a single measurement interval.

Quartz crystals dominate because they combine good stability with low cost. Their frequency depends
on temperature along a curve set by the crystal's cut, and the standard cut for common oscillators
has a cubic characteristic that is flat near room temperature and deviates at the extremes.
Temperature compensation applies a correction from a measured temperature; oven control holds the
crystal at a fixed elevated temperature and achieves far better stability at the cost of warm-up
time and substantial power.

Ageing is the drift that occurs regardless of conditions, from stress relief and contamination
within the package, and it is the parameter that determines how often a system must be recalibrated
or resynchronized. It is largest immediately after manufacture and decreases roughly logarithmically,
which is why a period of operation before final calibration improves long-term accuracy. Atomic
references — rubidium and caesium — remove ageing almost entirely and are used where holdover
performance without an external reference is required.

Q: A board's oscillator runs consistently a few hundred parts per million high, though the crystal
is specified to twenty parts per million. The most likely cause is:
A. A defective crystal
B. Load capacitance mismatch — the circuit's loading pulls the crystal's frequency, and capacitors
   chosen without accounting for the crystal's specified load and the stray capacitance produce
   exactly this magnitude of offset
C. Temperature
D. Ageing
Reasoning: A crystal's frequency specification applies at a stated load capacitance. The oscillator
circuit's capacitors plus board and pin strays must present that load. A mismatch pulls the
frequency by far more than the crystal's own tolerance, and the error is consistent rather than
drifting, which distinguishes it from temperature and ageing. Answer: B.

## Jitter, Phase Noise, and Their Effects

Jitter is the deviation of clock edges from their ideal times, and phase noise is the same
phenomenon described in the frequency domain as noise sidebands around the carrier. Which
description to use depends on the application: digital designers work with jitter in picoseconds,
and radio and data converter designers work with phase noise in decibels relative to the carrier at
specified offsets, and converting between them is routine.

The categories of jitter matter because they have different causes and different consequences.
Random jitter arises from thermal and other noise sources, is unbounded, and is characterized by a
standard deviation. Deterministic jitter comes from identifiable mechanisms — power supply
coupling, crosstalk, and data-dependent effects in serial links — is bounded, and can be reduced by
addressing its source. Total jitter at a specified error rate combines both, and it is what a link
budget must accommodate.

The consequences differ by application in ways worth knowing. In a synchronous digital system,
cycle-to-cycle jitter eats into the timing margin and reduces the maximum usable frequency. In a
serial link, jitter closes the eye and raises the error rate. In a sampling data converter,
aperture jitter translates directly into amplitude error proportional to the input's slew rate,
which means a high-frequency input demands a far cleaner clock than a low-frequency one for the same
resolution. In an audio converter the effect is audible distortion. The same clock can be adequate
for one of these and inadequate for another.

## Clock Distribution and Phase-Locked Loops

Distributing a clock across a board or a chip introduces skew — differences in arrival time at
different destinations — and skew consumes timing margin exactly as jitter does. The distribution
network is therefore designed rather than routed: matched trace lengths, controlled impedance,
proper termination, and a topology chosen so that the paths to synchronous destinations are
equalized.

Buffers and fanout devices regenerate the clock and add their own jitter and skew, so a deep
distribution tree accumulates both. Differential signalling is preferred for high-speed clocks
because it rejects common-mode noise and radiates less, and it costs a pair of traces and a
terminated receiver. Return paths matter as much as the traces themselves — a clock trace crossing a
gap in the reference plane has no adjacent return path, which increases inductance, degrades the
edge, and radiates.

Phase-locked loops generate a clock locked in phase to a reference, multiplying or dividing its
frequency. The loop compares the phase of a divided output against the reference, filters the error,
and steers a controlled oscillator. The loop bandwidth is the key design parameter: it sets how fast
the loop tracks the reference and therefore which noise it passes and which it rejects. Reference
noise inside the loop bandwidth passes through; oscillator noise inside is suppressed and outside is
not. Choosing the bandwidth is therefore a decision about which noise source dominates where, and
the correct choice depends on the relative quality of the reference and the oscillator.

## Time Transfer and Synchronization Protocols

Synchronizing clocks across a network is a distinct problem from generating a stable local clock,
and the fundamental obstacle is that measuring the offset between two clocks requires knowing the
one-way delay between them, which cannot be measured directly. The standard exchange measures the
round trip and assumes the path is symmetric, so any asymmetry between the forward and reverse paths
appears directly as an offset error of half the asymmetry.

Network time protocols achieve millisecond accuracy over ordinary networks by exchanging timestamps
and filtering many measurements to reject those affected by queueing delay. Their accuracy is
limited by exactly the asymmetry problem, since queueing is not symmetric and varies. Precision time
protocols achieve sub-microsecond accuracy by timestamping in hardware at the physical layer, which
removes the software stack's variability, and by having the intermediate switches measure and report
their own residence time so that queueing is corrected rather than averaged over.

Satellite navigation systems provide a widely available time reference with excellent accuracy,
which is why they underpin timing in telecommunications, power grids, and financial systems. This is
also a concentration risk: the signals are weak, jamming and spoofing are demonstrated, and systems
depending on them need holdover capability and ideally an independent reference. The holdover
requirement is what determines the oscillator specification, since a system that must maintain
accuracy for a day without a reference needs a fundamentally better oscillator than one that
resynchronizes every minute.

## Real-Time Scheduling and Measurement

A real-time system is one where correctness depends on timing as well as on results, and the
distinction between hard and soft real-time is about the consequence of a missed deadline rather
than about how fast the system is. A hard deadline missed is a failure; a soft deadline missed
degrades quality. Systems are frequently specified as hard when they are soft, which drives cost
without benefit, and occasionally the reverse, which is more serious.

Schedulability analysis determines whether a set of tasks can meet their deadlines. Rate-monotonic
priority assignment gives higher priority to more frequent tasks and is optimal among fixed-priority
schemes, with a utilization bound below which schedulability is guaranteed. Earliest-deadline-first
schedules dynamically and achieves full utilization, at the cost of less predictable behaviour when
the system is overloaded. Both analyses require worst-case execution times, and obtaining them is
the hard part — measurement gives observed maxima rather than true worst cases, and static analysis
gives bounds that are safe and often very pessimistic.

Priority inversion is the failure mode that catches designers. A high-priority task blocked on a
resource held by a low-priority task is effectively running at the low priority, and if a
medium-priority task then preempts the low-priority one, the high-priority task waits for it too.
Priority inheritance — temporarily raising the holder's priority to that of the blocked task —
resolves it, and it must be enabled explicitly on most systems that offer it.
