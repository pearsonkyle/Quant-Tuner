## Cell Chemistry and Characteristics

A battery cell converts chemical energy to electrical energy through a reversible reaction between
two electrodes separated by an electrolyte. The chemistry determines nominal voltage, energy
density, power capability, cycle life, temperature tolerance, and safety behaviour, and no chemistry
optimizes all of these simultaneously — every selection is a set of tradeoffs made for a specific
application.

Lithium-ion dominates portable and vehicle applications because of energy density, and it is not one
chemistry but a family distinguished by cathode material. Cobalt-rich variants give the highest
energy density and the worst thermal stability. Iron-phosphate gives lower energy density, a
notably flat discharge curve, far better thermal stability, and longer cycle life. Nickel-rich
variants sit between. The flat discharge curve of iron-phosphate is an underappreciated engineering
consequence: it makes voltage-based state-of-charge estimation nearly useless, because a large
capacity range maps to a millivolt-scale voltage range.

The parameters that describe a cell are worth being precise about because they are routinely
confused. Capacity in amp-hours is charge, not energy; energy in watt-hours is capacity times
average voltage. C-rate expresses current as a multiple of the capacity value, so 2C on a 3 Ah cell
is 6 A. Internal resistance determines both the voltage sag under load and the heat generated, and
it rises with age and falls with temperature, which couples the thermal and ageing behaviour
together.

Q: An iron-phosphate pack reports wildly inaccurate state of charge from a voltage lookup table.
The cause is:
A. A faulty voltage sensor
B. The chemistry's discharge curve is nearly flat across most of its usable range, so small
   voltage measurement errors map to very large charge errors; coulomb counting with periodic
   endpoint correction is required instead
C. The pack is defective
D. The table is for the wrong temperature
Reasoning: A flat open-circuit voltage curve makes voltage a poor observable for charge state. The
standard approach is integrating current over time and correcting the accumulated drift at the
steep regions near full and empty, where voltage does become informative. Answer: B.

## Charging and Degradation

The standard lithium-ion charge profile has two phases. Constant current brings the cell up to its
voltage limit at a controlled rate; constant voltage then holds at that limit while the current
tapers, and charging terminates when the current falls below a threshold. Most of the capacity
enters during the constant current phase, which is why fast charging to eighty percent is much
quicker than the remaining twenty.

Charging outside the safe window causes damage that is often irreversible. Charging below freezing
plates metallic lithium on the anode rather than intercalating it, which permanently reduces
capacity and creates internal short-circuit risk; a correct charger refuses to charge a cold pack
and warms it first if it can. Overcharging drives the cathode beyond its stable range and can
initiate decomposition. Both failures are prevented by the same design principle: the charger
should treat temperature and voltage limits as hard interlocks rather than as advisory.

Degradation proceeds through several mechanisms simultaneously, and the practical levers follow
from knowing which dominates. Growth of the interface layer on the anode consumes lithium and is
accelerated by high temperature and by time at high state of charge. Mechanical stress from
repeated expansion drives particle cracking, accelerated by deep cycling and high rates. Storing at
a mid state of charge in a cool place and avoiding both full charge and full discharge in routine
use addresses most of the controllable degradation.

Charging and ageing:
  constant current phase  the bulk of the charge
  constant voltage phase  taper; slow and it completes the fill
  termination          current falling below a threshold
  fast charge to eighty  much of the time cost is in the last fifth
  cold charging        plates lithium; permanent damage and a short risk
  the interlock principle  temperature and voltage limits enforced in hardware
                       where possible
  balancing            equalizing cell voltages in a series string
  passive balancing    bleed the high cells through a resistor; simple, wasteful
  active balancing     transfer charge between cells; efficient, complex
  the weakest cell     determines pack capacity in series; balancing addresses
                       drift, not intrinsic mismatch
  interface layer growth  lithium consumed; driven by heat and high charge state
  particle cracking    mechanical; driven by deep cycles and high rates
  the practical levers  cool storage, mid state of charge, shallow cycles,
                       moderate rates
  depth of discharge   shallower cycles yield disproportionately more of them
  the throughput view  total energy delivered over life is often a better metric
                       than cycle count

## Battery Management and State Estimation

A battery management system protects the pack, estimates its state, and reports to the system. The
protection function is safety-critical and should be simple, independent, and verifiable: overvoltage,
undervoltage, overcurrent, and over- and under-temperature cutoffs, ideally with a hardware
protection layer that operates even if the microcontroller has failed. Complexity belongs in the
estimation function, not the protection function.

State of charge estimation combines two imperfect methods. Coulomb counting integrates current, which
is accurate in the short term and drifts without bound as sensor offset accumulates. Voltage-based
estimation uses the open-circuit voltage relationship, which does not drift but requires the cell to
rest before it is valid and is nearly useless on flat-curve chemistries. A Kalman filter or
equivalent combines them, trusting the integration over short intervals and correcting from voltage
when conditions allow.

State of health is harder because the quantity of interest — remaining capacity relative to new —
is only directly measurable by a full discharge, which is rarely acceptable in service. Practical
estimators track internal resistance growth, partial-cycle capacity increments, and voltage
relaxation behaviour, and they are best understood as trended indicators rather than as
measurements. A state-of-health number that moves smoothly and monotonically is more likely to be
heavily filtered than accurate.

Battery management:
  protection function  safety-critical; simple, independent, verifiable
  hardware protection  a second layer independent of firmware
  the design rule      complexity in estimation, never in protection
  coulomb counting     accurate short term, unbounded drift
  current sensor offset  the drift source; a small offset integrates to a large
                       error over a day
  open-circuit voltage  no drift, needs rest, useless on flat curves
  Kalman filtering     combining both with a cell model
  equivalent circuit model  resistance plus RC branches; enough for most
                       estimation
  state of health      capacity fade; not directly measurable in service
  resistance tracking  a usable proxy for ageing
  incremental capacity  differential analysis of partial charges; sensitive to

  cell-level monitoring  required in series strings; a single pack voltage hides
                       a failing cell entirely
  temperature sensing  multiple points; a pack has thermal gradients and the
                       hottest cell ages fastest
  logging              the data that makes a field failure diagnosable; log
                       current, voltage per cell, and temperature at a rate that
                       captures events rather than averages

## Pack Design, Thermal, and Safety

A pack is cells in series for voltage and parallel for capacity, plus interconnects, sensing,
protection, thermal management, and enclosure. The series count sets the voltage and therefore the
insulation, isolation, and safety category; the parallel count sets capacity and the fault current
available, which is frequently underestimated. A modest lithium pack can deliver hundreds of amps
into a short circuit, and every design decision about conductors and fusing follows from that
number.

Thermal management is a first-order concern rather than an afterthought. Heat generation scales
with current squared times internal resistance, so high-rate operation heats the pack rapidly.
Elevated temperature accelerates every degradation mechanism, and temperature non-uniformity across
the pack causes cells to age at different rates, which progressively worsens the imbalance the
balancing system is trying to correct. Designing for uniform temperature is as important as
designing for low temperature.

Thermal runaway is the failure mode that governs safety design. A cell driven past its stability
limit by overcharge, internal short, mechanical damage, or external heat begins exothermic
decomposition, which raises the temperature, which accelerates the decomposition. Once initiated it
cannot be stopped, so the entire safety strategy is prevention and containment: prevent the
initiating conditions, and design the pack so that one cell in runaway does not propagate to its
neighbours.

Pack design and safety:
  series count         sets voltage, insulation, and safety category
  parallel count       sets capacity and available fault current
  fault current        larger than intuition suggests; sizes conductors and fusing
  interconnect resistance  a source of imbalance and localized heating
  fusing               per-cell fusing limits the consequence of a single failure
  contactors           high-voltage disconnect; sequencing and pre-charge matter
  pre-charge           limits inrush into capacitive loads; skipping it welds
                       contactors
  isolation monitoring  detects insulation breakdown before it becomes a hazard
  heat generation      current squared times resistance
  thermal uniformity   as important as absolute temperature; gradients create
                       divergent ageing
  cooling approaches   passive, forced air, liquid, phase change; chosen by heat
                       flux and packaging
  thermal runaway      self-sustaining exothermic decomposition; unstoppable once
                       started
  propagation resistance  spacing, barriers, and venting so one cell does not
                       take the pack
  venting              directed away from occupants and electronics
  transport and regulation  lithium shipment is regulated; certification testing
                       is a schedule item, not a formality

## Harvesting and Low-Power Design

Not every system carries a battery sized for its whole life. Energy harvesting extracts power from
the environment — light, vibration, thermal gradient, radio frequency — and the defining
characteristic is that the available power is small, intermittent, and highly variable. A harvesting
design is therefore an energy budget problem rather than a power supply problem, and it needs
storage to buffer between when energy arrives and when it is needed.

The power extracted from most harvesters depends strongly on the load impedance, and maximum power
point tracking adjusts the operating point to keep it at the optimum. For a small photovoltaic cell
this can double or triple the harvested energy compared with directly charging a battery, which is
frequently the difference between a design that works and one that does not.

Low-power firmware design follows a single principle: the processor should be asleep almost all of
the time. Average current is dominated by whatever runs continuously, so a design with a fifty
microamp sleep current and a brief twenty milliamp active burst is limited by the sleep current
entirely. The corollary is that peripherals left enabled, pins floating, and pull-ups fighting
external drives frequently consume more than the processor itself, and finding them requires
measuring rather than reasoning.
