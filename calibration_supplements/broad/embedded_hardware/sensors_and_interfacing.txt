## Sensor Fundamentals and the Measurement Chain

A sensor converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal, and every specification
that matters describes how faithfully it does so. Sensitivity is the output change per unit
input. Range is the span over which the relationship holds. Resolution is the smallest
detectable change. Accuracy is closeness to the true value and is a systematic property;
precision is repeatability and is a statistical one. A sensor can be precise and inaccurate,
returning consistently wrong values, which is the case a single calibration point fixes and
that repeated measurement never will.

The measurement chain from physical quantity to usable number has several stages, and error
introduced at any stage is generally irrecoverable downstream. The sensing element responds
to the measurand. Signal conditioning amplifies, filters, and level-shifts. The converter
digitizes. Digital processing linearizes, compensates, and filters further. The chain's
overall performance is bounded by its weakest stage, and the recurring error is investing in
a high-resolution converter behind a noisy, poorly conditioned front end — the extra bits
digitize noise with great precision.

Noise and bandwidth are inseparable. Thermal noise power is proportional to bandwidth, so
restricting the bandwidth to what the signal actually occupies directly improves
signal-to-noise ratio. A sensor measuring a quantity that changes over seconds gains nothing
from a kilohertz bandwidth and pays for it in noise. Filtering must occur in analog before
digitization where aliasing is concerned, since once high-frequency content has folded into
the band no digital filter can separate it.

Sensor specification vocabulary:
  sensitivity        output per unit input
  range / span       the valid input interval
  resolution         smallest distinguishable change
  accuracy           closeness to truth; systematic
  precision          repeatability; statistical
  linearity          deviation from a straight-line response, as % of full scale
  hysteresis         output depends on approach direction
  drift              slow change with time or temperature
  offset / zero      output at zero input
  response time      typically the time to reach 63% (one time constant)
  cross-sensitivity  response to quantities other than the intended one
  self-heating       excitation power warming the element and biasing the reading

Cross-sensitivity is the specification most often ignored and most often responsible for
puzzling field behaviour. A pressure sensor responds to temperature. A gas sensor responds to
humidity. A strain gauge responds to temperature far more strongly than to the strain being
measured. Each requires either compensation using a second measurement or a differential
configuration that cancels the interfering quantity structurally — which is why so many
precision sensors are built as bridges.

Q: A 16-bit ADC is used with a sensor whose analog front end has 8 mV of noise on a 3.3 V
range. The effective resolution is closest to:
A. 16 bits   B. 12 bits   C. 9 bits   D. 8 bits
Reasoning: One LSB of a 16-bit converter over 3.3 V is 50 microvolts, far below the 8 mV of
noise. The noise spans about 160 LSBs, so roughly 7-8 bits of the converter output are noise.
Effective resolution is about 16 minus log2(160), or approximately 8.7 bits. The remaining
converter bits digitize noise. Fixing the front end is the only path to better resolution.
Answer: C.

## Temperature and Strain

Temperature is the most commonly measured physical quantity and has several sensor families
with genuinely different tradeoffs.

Thermocouples exploit the Seebeck effect, generating a small voltage at the junction of two
dissimilar metals that depends on the temperature difference between that junction and the
reference. They cover enormous ranges, are rugged and cheap, and produce only tens of
microvolts per degree, which demands careful low-noise amplification. Their defining
practical requirement is cold junction compensation: the output depends on the difference
between the measuring and reference junctions, so the reference temperature must be measured
by some other means and added back. Omitting this is the classic thermocouple error and
produces readings offset by whatever the ambient temperature happens to be.

Resistance temperature detectors use the predictable resistance change of pure metal,
usually platinum. They are the most accurate and stable option, nearly linear, and slow and
expensive. Their measurement requires excitation current, which causes self-heating, and lead
resistance becomes significant relative to the element's resistance — which is why three-wire
and four-wire configurations exist, cancelling lead resistance by measurement rather than by
assumption.

Thermistors are semiconductor devices with a large, strongly nonlinear resistance change.
The large sensitivity makes them easy to read with simple circuitry, and the nonlinearity
requires the Steinhart-Hart equation or a lookup table for accuracy. They are cheap, fast
because they can be made small, and limited in range.

Temperature sensor comparison:
  thermocouple     -200 to +1800 C, ~1-2 C accuracy, needs cold junction
                   compensation, microvolt-level signal, very rugged
  RTD (Pt100)      -200 to +600 C, 0.1 C or better, nearly linear, needs 3- or
                   4-wire connection, self-heating matters
  thermistor       -50 to +150 C, 0.1-1 C, very sensitive, strongly nonlinear,
                   cheap and fast
  silicon IC       -55 to +150 C, digital output, calibrated, trivially easy;
                   the right default when the range suffices
  infrared         non-contact, needs emissivity knowledge; reads the surface
                   emissivity as much as its temperature
  thermal time constant  the sensor measures its own temperature, not the
                   medium's; mass and coupling dominate response speed

The last line deserves emphasis because it causes more temperature measurement error than
sensor accuracy does. A sensor reports its own temperature, and its agreement with the
quantity of interest depends on thermal coupling. A probe in flowing air with poor contact
reads somewhere between the air and whatever it is radiating to and conducting through. A
sensor on a circuit board reads the board, not the enclosure. Self-heating from excitation
adds a systematic offset proportional to dissipated power and inversely proportional to
thermal conductance to the environment. Attention to mounting and to thermal path usually
matters more than choosing a better sensor.

Strain gauges measure deformation through the resistance change of a foil pattern bonded to
a surface. The gauge factor relates fractional resistance change to strain and is about two
for metal foil, meaning that a strain of one part in a million produces a resistance change
of two parts per million — an extraordinarily small signal that must be extracted from a
resistance that itself varies strongly with temperature.

The Wheatstone bridge is the solution and is worth understanding as a general technique
rather than a specific circuit. Four resistances arranged in two dividers produce an output
proportional to their imbalance, and if all four experience the same temperature change, the
change cancels in the difference. A quarter bridge with one active gauge compensates nothing
and needs a separate reference; a half bridge with two gauges in opposite strain states
cancels temperature and doubles sensitivity; a full bridge with four does better still. This
differential structure — measure a difference so that common-mode disturbances subtract out —
recurs throughout instrumentation and is the reason load cells, pressure sensors, and
precision resistive measurements are almost always bridges.

## Inertial, Magnetic, and Position Sensing

Accelerometers measure proper acceleration, which includes gravity. A device at rest reads
one g upward, not zero, and this is not an error — it measures the force required to keep the
proof mass from falling, which at rest is exactly gravity. The consequence is that separating
gravity from linear acceleration requires knowing the orientation, and knowing the orientation
requires a gravity reference, which is the circularity that makes inertial navigation hard.

Gyroscopes measure angular rate. Integrating rate gives angle, and integrating a signal with
any bias produces angle error growing linearly with time, so a gyro alone drifts without
bound. MEMS gyros have bias stability measured in degrees per hour at best and often per
minute, so unaided attitude from a MEMS gyro is useful for seconds and useless for minutes.

The complementary strengths of these sensors are why they are always fused. An accelerometer
gives an absolute gravity reference that is accurate over long periods and corrupted by any
linear acceleration in the short term. A gyroscope is accurate over short periods and drifts
over long ones. A complementary filter — high-pass the gyro-integrated angle, low-pass the
accelerometer-derived angle, and sum — combines them with two lines of code and one tuning
parameter. A Kalman filter does the same optimally, additionally estimating the gyro bias,
which is what allows drift to be actively corrected rather than merely filtered.

Inertial and position sensing:
  accelerometer      measures proper acceleration including gravity; noisy for
                     orientation under motion
  gyroscope          angular rate; integrates to angle with unbounded drift
  magnetometer       heading reference; corrupted by nearby ferrous material and
                     currents; requires hard- and soft-iron calibration
  IMU                accelerometer plus gyro; 9-axis adds magnetometer
  complementary filter  simple fusion; one tuning constant
  Kalman filter      optimal fusion with bias estimation
  encoder            incremental gives relative position and needs homing;
                     absolute gives position at power-on
  quadrature         two channels 90 degrees apart give direction and 4x
                     resolution
  resolver           rugged absolute angle for harsh environments
  Hall effect        magnetic field or proximity; contactless, robust
  LVDT               precise linear displacement, contactless, rugged
  time of flight     distance by light or sound propagation delay

Magnetometer calibration is required rather than optional and is skipped often enough to be
worth stating explicitly. Hard-iron effects are constant magnetic offsets from permanently
magnetized material near the sensor, appearing as a displaced centre in the locus of
measurements taken while rotating the device. Soft-iron effects are distortions from
ferromagnetic material that concentrates the field, turning the ideal sphere into an
ellipsoid. Calibration consists of rotating the device through many orientations, fitting an
ellipsoid to the measurements, and deriving the offset and transformation that maps it back
to a sphere. Without this, heading error of tens of degrees is normal, and the error varies
with orientation in a way that looks like random misbehaviour.

[user] My IMU-based tilt reading is stable when the robot is still but swings wildly when it
accelerates.
[assistant] tool_call imu.fusion_diagnose({"algorithm": "accel_only_tilt",
"gyro_present": true, "gyro_used": false, "accel_lp_hz": 5,
"observed_error_static_deg": 0.4, "observed_error_dynamic_deg": 22})
[tool] {"cause": "linear_acceleration_indistinguishable_from_gravity",
"fix": "complementary_or_kalman_fusion", "suggested_tau_s": 0.5,
"note": "gyro_available_but_unused"}
[assistant] The accelerometer cannot distinguish gravity from linear acceleration — both are
forces on the proof mass — so when the robot accelerates, the measured vector is the sum and
your computed tilt swings by the corresponding angle. At 0.4 degrees static error your sensor
and mounting are fine; the algorithm is the problem.

You have a gyroscope and are not using it. Fuse them with a complementary filter, which is
about five lines:

Integrate the gyro rate to get a short-term angle estimate. Compute the accelerometer-derived
angle as you do now. Then blend: the new estimate is alpha times (previous estimate plus gyro
rate times dt) plus (1 minus alpha) times the accelerometer angle. Alpha is derived from a
time constant tau and your sample period: alpha = tau / (tau + dt). Start with tau around 0.5
seconds.

What this does physically: the gyro dominates over short intervals, where it is accurate and
the accelerometer is corrupted by motion, while the accelerometer dominates over long
intervals, where it is accurate and the gyro has drifted. Your 22 degree dynamic error should
drop to a couple of degrees.

Two refinements once that works. Reject accelerometer updates when the measured magnitude
departs significantly from one g — during hard acceleration the vector is clearly not gravity,
so weight it down or skip it entirely for those samples. This is cheap and noticeably
improves behaviour under aggressive motion.

Then, if you need better, move to a Kalman filter that estimates gyro bias as a state. The
complementary filter tolerates bias by continuously pulling toward the accelerometer; a
Kalman filter identifies the bias and subtracts it, so the gyro-only interval over which the
estimate stays good extends from seconds to minutes. That matters if your robot has periods of
sustained acceleration where the accelerometer is unusable throughout.

Also lower your accelerometer low-pass from 5 Hz once fusion is in place — the filter was
compensating for the algorithm, and with proper fusion the extra lag costs you more than the
noise reduction gains.

## Optical, Environmental, and Chemical Sensing

Photodetectors convert light to electrical signal and differ mainly in sensitivity and speed.
Photodiodes are linear, fast, and require transimpedance amplification to convert their
current output to voltage. Phototransistors provide internal gain with slower response and
poorer linearity. Avalanche photodiodes provide gain through impact ionization at high
reverse bias, useful when the signal is small, at the cost of excess noise and temperature
sensitivity. Single-photon avalanche diodes operate above breakdown and detect individual
photons with picosecond timing, which is what makes direct time-of-flight ranging possible.

Optical measurement is dominated by ambient light rejection in practice. A sensor detecting
a modest signal against sunlight or fluorescent flicker must separate them, and the standard
technique is modulation: drive the source with a known carrier frequency and detect
synchronously, which rejects everything not at that frequency and in phase. This is lock-in
detection, and it can extract signals far below the noise floor. Optical filters matched to
the source wavelength, and mechanical baffling, complete the approach.

Environmental and chemical sensors:
  humidity           capacitive polymer; needs temperature compensation, drifts,
                     and can be damaged by condensation
  pressure           piezoresistive or capacitive diaphragm; absolute, gauge, or
                     differential — specify which
  gas, MOX           metal oxide semiconductor; cheap, sensitive, poorly selective,
                     needs a heater and warm-up, drifts substantially
  gas, electrochemical  selective and accurate, consumable, limited lifetime
  gas, NDIR          infrared absorption; selective and stable, more expensive;
                     the right choice for CO2
  particulate        optical scattering; reports a size-binned count converted to
                     mass by an assumed density
  pH                 glass electrode; high impedance, requires frequent
                     calibration, temperature dependent
  conductivity       needs cell constant calibration and temperature compensation
  flow               differential pressure, thermal, turbine, ultrasonic, Coriolis;
                     Coriolis measures mass flow directly and is the most accurate
                     and expensive

Low-cost gas sensors deserve a specific caution because their data is frequently
over-interpreted. Metal oxide sensors respond to a broad class of reducing gases with poor
selectivity, drift substantially over weeks, depend strongly on humidity and temperature, and
require significant heater power and warm-up time. They are useful for detecting changes and
trends in a controlled setting and are not calibrated instruments. Reporting a specific
concentration in parts per million from an uncalibrated metal oxide sensor implies a
precision the device cannot deliver, and comparing readings across devices or across weeks
without recalibration is not meaningful.

## Calibration, Compensation, and Fusion

Calibration establishes the mapping from sensor output to physical quantity. A single-point
calibration corrects offset only. Two-point calibration corrects offset and gain and is
sufficient for a linear sensor. Multi-point calibration with interpolation or a fitted
polynomial handles nonlinearity. The calibration must span the operating range, because
extrapolating a fit beyond its data is unjustified and often badly wrong.

Temperature compensation is required for nearly every precision measurement, since almost
every sensing mechanism has a temperature coefficient comparable to or larger than the effect
being measured. Compensation may be structural, as in a bridge where the effect cancels; it
may be by explicit modelling, measuring temperature alongside and applying a correction
derived from characterization; or it may be by control, holding the sensor at a fixed
temperature with a heater, which is expensive in power and effective.

Calibration practice:
  single point       offset only
  two point          offset and gain; sufficient for linear sensors
  multi point        nonlinearity; interpolate or fit
  span the range     never extrapolate a calibration
  reference standard traceability matters when absolute accuracy is claimed
  temperature comp   structural, modelled, or controlled
  aging              recalibration interval established by characterization, not
                     by assumption
  per-unit vs batch  cheap sensors vary enough that per-unit calibration is often
                     needed; store coefficients in the device
  field verification a known reference check in situ, periodically

Sensor fusion combines multiple sensors to produce an estimate better than any individually,
and it works when the sensors have complementary error characteristics. The general framework
is probabilistic: each sensor provides a measurement with an uncertainty, and the fused
estimate weights them inversely to their variances, so a precise sensor pulls the estimate
strongly and a noisy one barely moves it. The Kalman filter implements this recursively for
linear Gaussian systems, the extended and unscented variants handle nonlinearity, and
particle filters handle multimodal distributions where a single Gaussian is inadequate.

Fault detection is the part of fusion most often omitted and most valuable in deployed
systems. When multiple sensors measure related quantities, their disagreement is
information: a residual that exceeds what the noise model predicts indicates that one of them
is wrong. With three or more redundant sensors, voting identifies which. With two, the
disagreement is detectable and not attributable, which is why safety-critical systems use
three. Implementing a residual check costs little and converts a sensor failure from silently
corrupted output into a detected fault — which is the difference between a system that
degrades gracefully and one that confidently acts on a bad reading.
