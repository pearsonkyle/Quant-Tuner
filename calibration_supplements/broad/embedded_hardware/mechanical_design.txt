## Materials and Their Properties

Mechanical design selects materials and geometry to carry loads, maintain dimensions, resist the
environment, and be manufacturable at the required cost. The properties that drive selection are few
and it is worth being precise about what each one means, because they are routinely confused in
casual discussion.

Stiffness and strength are independent properties. Stiffness — the elastic modulus — governs how much
a part deflects under load and is essentially fixed for a given material family, so heat-treating
steel does not make it stiffer. Strength governs when it yields or breaks and varies enormously with
alloy and treatment. A part that flexes too much needs more material or better geometry, not a
stronger alloy, and this distinction resolves a large fraction of the design mistakes made by people
approaching mechanics from other fields.

Toughness is a third independent property: the energy absorbed before fracture. A material can be
strong and brittle, failing suddenly at high load with no warning, or weaker and tough, deforming
substantially first. For anything that might be dropped, impacted, or overloaded, toughness usually
matters more than peak strength, because the failure mode determines whether the product degrades or
shatters.

  ductile failure      deformation before fracture; gives warning
  brittle failure      sudden, at high load, no warning
  fatigue strength     the stress survivable for many cycles; far below the

Q: A bracket deflects more than acceptable under load, though it is nowhere near yielding. The
correct response is:
A. Use a higher-strength alloy
B. Change the geometry or the material family — deflection is governed by stiffness, which is
   essentially fixed for a material family, so a stronger alloy of the same metal will deflect
   identically
C. Reduce the load
D. Add a safety factor
Reasoning: Elastic deflection depends on the elastic modulus and the section geometry. Heat treating
or alloying steel changes its strength by large factors and its modulus barely at all. Increasing
the section's moment of inertia, shortening the span, or adding a support are the effective
remedies. Answer: B.

## Stress, Deflection, and Section Geometry

Simple loading cases cover most practical design and are worth knowing quantitatively. Axial
loading distributes stress over the cross-sectional area. Bending produces stress varying linearly
across the section, maximum at the outer fibres and zero at the neutral axis. Torsion produces shear
stress increasing with radius. Each has a corresponding section property that geometry can be
optimized against.

Bending is where geometry matters most and the leverage is large. The relevant property is the
second moment of area, which scales with the cube of the depth in the loading direction. Doubling a
beam's depth increases its bending stiffness eightfold while doubling its mass, which is why
structural sections put material far from the neutral axis — the I-beam, the box section, the ribbed
plate. A flat plate stiffened with a few ribs is dramatically stiffer than the same mass distributed
uniformly.

Stress concentration is the most consequential effect for durability. Any abrupt change in section —
a sharp internal corner, a hole, a notch, a thread root — locally multiplies the stress, often by a
factor of two or three, and fatigue cracks start there. Generous fillet radii at internal corners are
nearly free and are among the highest-value details in a mechanical drawing, and a sharp internal
corner in a part that sees cyclic load is a defect regardless of what the nominal stress calculation
says.

                       number

## Fasteners, Joints, and Tolerances

A bolted joint works by clamping, not by shear on the bolt. Tightening stretches the bolt elastically
and the resulting tension clamps the parts, so the joint carries load through friction between the
clamped faces while the bolt sees a nearly constant tension. This is why preload matters so much: a
properly preloaded joint sees very little cyclic stress in the bolt and survives indefinitely, and
an under-preloaded one lets the parts separate slightly under load, cycling the bolt and failing by
fatigue.

Thread engagement should be at least one diameter in steel and more in softer materials, because
insufficient engagement strips the female thread rather than breaking the bolt. Threading directly
into aluminium or plastic in a joint that will be disassembled repeatedly is a common design
mistake; threaded inserts solve it cheaply. And locking features exist because vibration loosens
fasteners through transverse micro-motion, not through rotation from vibration directly — which is
why locking compounds and prevailing-torque features work while lock washers largely do not.

Tolerances specify how much dimensions may vary, and they drive cost steeply. Every tolerance should
be as loose as the function permits, because tight tolerances require better processes, more
inspection, and higher scrap rates. Tolerance stackup — the accumulation of individual variations
across a chain of parts — is what determines whether an assembly fits, and analysing it before
manufacture rather than discovering it afterward is standard practice that is nonetheless skipped
regularly.

## Manufacturing Processes and Design for Them

Every process imposes constraints, and designing without knowing them produces parts that are
expensive or impossible to make. Machining removes material and can produce almost any external
geometry, with the constraints being tool access, internal corner radii limited by tool diameter, and
cost proportional to material removed and to setup count. Reducing the number of setups — the
reorientations needed to reach every feature — often saves more than optimizing any individual
operation.

Injection moulding is the volume process for plastics and its constraints are strict. Wall thickness
must be uniform, because thick sections cool more slowly and shrink, producing sink marks and
warping. Draft angles are required so the part releases from the mould. Undercuts require moving
mould components at substantial cost. Ribs stiffen without thickening walls and must themselves be
thinner than the wall they attach to. These rules are not preferences; violating them produces parts
with visible defects.

Sheet metal is inexpensive and highly capable within its constraints. Bends require a minimum radius
related to thickness, features must stand clear of bend zones by a minimum distance, and the flat
pattern must account for material stretching at bends. Additive processes remove most geometric
constraints and introduce their own — anisotropic strength along the build direction, surface finish
determined by layer height and orientation, and support requirements on overhangs — and their
economics favour low volume and high complexity, which is the reverse of the traditional processes.

  ribs                 thinner than the wall they stiffen
  sheet metal          inexpensive within its constraints

  bend relief          features must clear the bend zone

## Enclosures, Sealing, and Vibration

An enclosure protects the electronics from the environment and often provides the thermal path,
structural support, and user interface simultaneously. Its material and construction follow from
which of those functions dominates, and the conflicts between them — sealing against airflow, thermal
conduction against electrical isolation, access for service against ingress protection — are decided
early and are expensive to revisit.

Sealing requires a designed gland. A gasket needs a defined compression, achieved by a groove of
controlled depth and a fastener pattern close enough that the flange does not bow between fasteners,
which is the usual cause of leaks in an otherwise correct design. Ingress protection ratings specify
tested resistance to dust and water, and they are tested conditions rather than general guarantees —
a rating for temporary immersion says nothing about sustained pressure or about chemical exposure.

Vibration and shock are where mechanical and electronic design meet most directly. A circuit board
has a natural frequency determined by its dimensions, thickness, and mounting points, and excitation
near it produces amplified deflection that fatigues solder joints and cracks components. The remedies
are raising the natural frequency by adding mounting points near the centre and supporting heavy
components, or isolating the assembly on mounts tuned well below the excitation. Heavy components on
long leads are the classic failure — the mass at the end of a flexible support is a resonator, and
staking it to the board is the standard and effective fix.
