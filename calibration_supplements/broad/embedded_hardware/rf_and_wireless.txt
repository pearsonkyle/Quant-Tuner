## Propagation and the Link Budget

Radio propagation determines what is achievable, and the dominant term at any distance is
free-space path loss. It grows with the square of both distance and frequency, which means
doubling the distance costs six decibels and doubling the frequency costs another six. The
frequency dependence is a consequence of the receiving antenna's effective aperture shrinking
with wavelength for a fixed gain, and it is why higher frequency bands trade range for
bandwidth.

Real environments depart from free space in ways that dominate the design. Reflection from
surfaces creates multiple paths that arrive with different delays and combine constructively or
destructively, producing deep fades that vary with position on a scale of half a wavelength.
Diffraction bends signals around obstacles, with the effect stronger at lower frequency where the
wavelength is comparable to the obstruction. Scattering from rough surfaces and small objects
spreads energy. Absorption by materials attenuates, with water and therefore vegetation, rain,
and human bodies being significant absorbers at microwave frequencies.

Multipath fading is the most consequential of these for system design. Two paths differing by
half a wavelength arrive in antiphase and cancel, which at 2.4 GHz means a null every six
centimetres — so a stationary receiver can sit in a deep fade while a position a hand's width
away has strong signal. This is why antenna diversity works: two antennas separated by a
fraction of a wavelength are unlikely to be in a fade simultaneously, and selecting or combining
them substantially improves reliability for a small cost.

Propagation and link budget:

  distance doubling     -6 dB

                        half wavelength
  Rayleigh fading       no dominant path; deep frequent fades

                        first zone clear or diffraction loss becomes significant
  penetration loss      walls, foliage, and bodies; strongly frequency dependent
  link budget           transmit power plus gains minus losses versus receiver
                        sensitivity

  noise floor           -174 dBm/Hz at room temperature; plus the bandwidth in dB

The thermal noise floor is the reference point for every receiver calculation and is worth
having memorized. At room temperature it is -174 dBm per hertz of bandwidth, so a receiver with
one megahertz of bandwidth has a noise floor of -174 plus 60, or -114 dBm, before its own noise
figure is added. Sensitivity is that floor plus the noise figure plus the required
signal-to-noise ratio for the modulation in use, which is why narrower bandwidth and more robust
modulation both improve range — each reduces the required received power.

Q: A link operating at 2.4 GHz is moved to 5 GHz with identical antennas and transmit power.
The received power changes by approximately:
A. No change   B. -6 dB   C. -12 dB   D. +6 dB
Reasoning: Free-space path loss grows with the square of frequency for antennas of fixed gain,
so slightly more than doubling the frequency costs about 6.4 dB. In practice the loss is worse
still, because higher frequencies penetrate walls and vegetation more poorly. This is why 5 GHz
Wi-Fi has shorter range than 2.4 GHz at equal power, and why it offers more bandwidth and less
congestion in compensation. Answer: B.

## Modulation and Coding

Modulation impresses information onto a carrier by varying its amplitude, frequency, or phase,
and the choice trades spectral efficiency against required signal-to-noise ratio. Higher-order
schemes carry more bits per symbol and pack the constellation points closer together, so noise
is more likely to move a received point into a neighbouring decision region. Every additional
bit per symbol costs roughly six decibels of required signal-to-noise ratio.

This tradeoff is why modern systems use adaptive modulation. When the link is strong, a
high-order scheme maximizes throughput; when it degrades, the system falls back to a robust
low-order scheme that maintains a connection at lower rate. The observable consequence is that
wireless throughput falls in steps rather than smoothly as a device moves away from an access
point, since each step is a modulation change.

Spread spectrum deliberately uses more bandwidth than the information requires, which sounds
wasteful and buys several properties. Direct sequence multiplies the data by a fast pseudorandom
code, spreading the energy across a wide band and allowing the receiver to recover it by
correlating with the same code, which provides processing gain against narrowband interference
and allows multiple users to share the band with different codes. Frequency hopping changes
carrier frequency rapidly according to a pattern, which averages over frequency-selective fading
and resists both interference and interception.

Modulation and coding:
  ASK, FSK, PSK        amplitude, frequency, phase modulation
  BPSK                 1 bit per symbol; the most robust common scheme
  QPSK                 2 bits per symbol
  16-QAM, 64-QAM, 256-QAM  4, 6, 8 bits per symbol; each step needs ~6 dB more
                       signal-to-noise ratio
  adaptive modulation  select by link quality; throughput falls in steps
  constellation        the symbol points; a diagram of received points diagnoses
                       phase noise, amplitude imbalance, and interference by the
                       shape of the scatter
  OFDM                 many narrow orthogonal subcarriers; converts a
                       frequency-selective channel into many flat ones and
                       simplifies equalization enormously
  cyclic prefix        a guard interval absorbing multipath delay spread; the
                       reason OFDM tolerates multipath
  PAPR                 OFDM's high peak-to-average ratio stresses the power
                       amplifier and forces backoff
  direct sequence      spreading by a fast code; processing gain and code-division
                       sharing
  frequency hopping    averages over fading and interference
  forward error correction  convolutional, Reed-Solomon, LDPC, turbo, polar;
                       trades rate for the required signal-to-noise ratio
  coding gain          the reduction in required SNR; several decibels for modest
                       overhead, and nearly always worth it

OFDM deserves emphasis because it is the technique underlying essentially every modern
high-rate wireless standard. A wideband signal in a multipath channel experiences frequency-
selective fading, where some frequencies are attenuated and others are not, and equalizing that
in a single-carrier system is computationally demanding. OFDM divides the band into many narrow
subcarriers, each of which is narrow enough that the channel is approximately flat across it, so
equalization becomes one complex multiplication per subcarrier. The cyclic prefix — a copy of
the symbol's end prepended to its start — absorbs the delay spread so that multipath copies do
not interfere between symbols, at the cost of a few percent of the throughput.

## Transmission Lines, Matching, and Amplifiers

Above the frequency where a conductor's length becomes comparable to a fraction of the
wavelength, it behaves as a transmission line rather than a wire. The characteristic impedance
depends on the geometry and dielectric, and it determines what happens at any discontinuity: a
mismatch reflects part of the incident wave back toward the source.

Impedance matching maximizes power transfer and minimizes reflection, and it is the central
practical task in RF design. The conventions are fifty ohms for most radio work and seventy-five
for video and broadcast reception, and the fifty ohm figure is a compromise — minimum attenuation
in coaxial cable occurs near seventy-seven ohms and maximum power handling near thirty, so fifty
is roughly the geometric middle.

The Smith chart is the classical tool for matching and remains useful as a way of thinking even
where software does the calculation. It maps the complex reflection coefficient onto a plane
where constant resistance and constant reactance are circles, so the effect of adding a series or
shunt component is a movement along a known arc. Matching becomes a geometric problem of
navigating from the load impedance to the centre, which is where the reflection is zero.

Transmission lines and amplifiers:
  characteristic impedance  set by geometry and dielectric; 50 ohm convention
  reflection coefficient  from the mismatch between load and line
  VSWR                 standing wave ratio; 1:1 perfect, 2:1 reflects ~11% of
                       power
  return loss          dB below incident reflected; higher is better
  Smith chart          reflection coefficient plane; matching as a geometric path
  L-network            two reactive components; matches any two resistances at one
                       frequency
  stub matching        a length of open or shorted line as a reactance
  quarter-wave transformer  matches two resistances with a line of intermediate
                       impedance
  balun                balanced to unbalanced transition; required when feeding a
                       dipole from coax, and its absence causes current on the
                       feedline outer conductor, which then radiates
  amplifier classes    A linear and inefficient, C efficient and nonlinear, E and
                       F switching for high efficiency
  1 dB compression     where gain falls by 1 dB; the practical linear limit
  third-order intercept  a figure of merit for intermodulation; higher is more
                       linear
  intermodulation      two signals producing sum and difference products; the
                       third-order products fall in band and cannot be filtered out
  noise figure         degradation of signal-to-noise; the first stage dominates
                       the whole chain

Third-order intermodulation deserves specific attention because it is the distortion mechanism
that filtering cannot remove. Two signals at nearby frequencies passing through a nonlinearity
produce products at frequencies that are close to the originals — specifically at twice one
frequency minus the other — so they land within the passband and are indistinguishable from
legitimate signals. This is why receiver linearity matters as much as sensitivity in a crowded
spectrum, and why a strong out-of-band signal can desensitize a receiver even though it is
nominally filtered.

[assistant] The position sensitivity is the diagnosis: you are in multipath fading, and your

At 915 MHz the wavelength is 32.8 cm, so multipath nulls occur roughly every 16 cm. In a
warehouse with metal racking there is no dominant line-of-sight path and many strong
reflections, which gives Rayleigh fading — the worst case, with deep fades occurring frequently.
Moving a few centimetres moves you out of a null, which is exactly what you observe.

Your 6 dB fade margin is adequate for open field and inadequate here. Rayleigh fading requires
roughly 20 dB of margin for 99% reliability, and 30 dB for 99.9%. That is the gap.

Three approaches, and the first is the most cost-effective by a wide margin.

Antenna diversity. Two antennas separated by a quarter wavelength or more — 8 cm at your
frequency — are unlikely to be in a fade simultaneously, since the fading is spatially
uncorrelated at that separation. Selecting whichever has the stronger signal typically recovers
10 dB of effective margin for the cost of a second antenna, a switch, and some firmware. Many
transceivers support this directly.

Frequency agility. Multipath fading is frequency selective, so a null at one frequency is
usually not a null at another. If you have channel-hopping available, hopping across the band
means a persistent null on one channel does not persist across all of them. This is what
frequency-hopping spread spectrum provides and it is why the technique is standard in
industrial environments.

Then increase the raw margin: a higher-gain antenna if the geometry permits, a lower data rate
with more robust modulation, or more transmit power within your regulatory limit. Each buys a
few decibels.

One thing to check first: your PCB trace antenna. Trace antennas are sensitive to the ground
plane, the enclosure, and nearby metal, and their real gain in situ is frequently several
decibels below the datasheet figure. Measuring the actual radiated performance in the enclosure,
or substituting an external antenna as a test, will tell you whether part of the shortfall is
there rather than in the channel.

## Antennas, Spectrum, and Practical RF

An antenna converts between guided and radiated waves, and reciprocity guarantees identical
transmit and receive characteristics. Its size is set by the wavelength: efficient radiation
requires dimensions comparable to a substantial fraction of a wavelength, which is why low
frequencies need large antennas and why electrically small antennas are inefficient and
narrowband — a fundamental limit rather than an engineering shortfall.

Gain is not amplification but redistribution: an antenna with gain concentrates the same
radiated power into a narrower beam, necessarily at the expense of other directions. Directivity
and beamwidth are inversely related, so higher gain means tighter pointing requirements. An
omnidirectional whip has near-unity gain in the horizontal plane with a null along its axis; a
dish concentrates enormously and must be aimed.

Spectrum is a regulated resource, and the regulatory framework shapes what is designable.
Licensed bands provide exclusive use and protection from interference at the cost of licensing.
Unlicensed bands — the industrial, scientific, and medical allocations — are free to use subject
to power and duty cycle limits, with no protection from interference, which means a design must
tolerate whatever else is present. Regional differences in allocations, power limits, and duty
cycle rules mean a product must be designed for the regions it will ship to.

Antennas, spectrum, and practice:
  half-wave dipole     the reference element; ~73 ohm radiation resistance, 2.15

  quarter-wave monopole  half a dipole over a ground plane; ~36 ohms, and its
                       performance depends critically on the ground plane
  patch antenna        planar, directional, easy to fabricate on a board
  PCB trace antenna    cheap and sensitive to layout, enclosure, and nearby metal
  chip antenna         small, requires a specified ground plane and keep-out
  gain and beamwidth   inversely related; gain is redistribution, not
                       amplification
  polarization         match transmit and receive, or lose several decibels;
                       cross-polarized is typically 20 dB down

  ISM bands            unlicensed, power and duty cycle limited, no interference
                       protection

  regulatory testing   emissions, spurious, and band edge compliance; plan and

Antenna keep-out violations are the most common practical RF error in embedded products and
are invisible until measured. A chip or trace antenna is designed assuming a specified ground
plane geometry and an area kept clear of copper, components, and enclosure metal. Routing a trace
beneath it, extending the ground plane into the keep-out, or placing a metal shield nearby shifts
the resonant frequency and destroys the match, typically costing ten decibels or more of
effective radiated power. The manufacturer's layout guidance exists for this reason and following
it is not optional.

The final practical observation is that RF design rewards measurement over calculation more
than most disciplines. Simulations and hand calculations establish the design, and the actual
performance depends on the enclosure, the ground plane, the nearby components, the cable
routing, and the environment in ways that are not captured. A vector network analyzer measuring
the antenna's match in its final assembly, and a spectrum analyzer measuring the actual radiated
output, are what distinguish a design that works from one that should. Budgeting time for that
measurement, and access to the instruments, is a project planning decision rather than a
technical one, and its absence is why so many embedded wireless products underperform their
specifications.
