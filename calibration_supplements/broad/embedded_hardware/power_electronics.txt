## Converter Topologies

Power electronics converts electrical energy between forms efficiently, and the central idea
is that switching is efficient while linear regulation is not. A linear regulator drops the
voltage difference across a pass element and dissipates it as heat, so efficiency is
approximately the output voltage divided by the input. A switching converter transfers energy in
packets using a switch that is either fully on, where the voltage across it is near zero, or
fully off, where the current through it is near zero — and in both states the dissipated power
is small.

The buck converter steps voltage down. A switch connects the input to an inductor during part
of the cycle, storing energy, and during the remainder the inductor's current continues through a
diode or a second switch, delivering energy to the output. In continuous conduction the output
voltage is the input times the duty cycle, which is the fraction of the period the main switch is
on — a relation that holds independent of load and is what makes the topology so tractable.

The boost converter steps voltage up. The switch shorts the inductor to ground, building
current, and when it opens the inductor's voltage adds to the input, forcing current into the
output at a higher voltage. Its output cannot be lower than its input, and it has a
right-half-plane zero in its control response that fundamentally limits the achievable loop
bandwidth — a control difficulty that the buck topology does not share.

Converter topologies:
  linear regulator     efficiency ~ Vout/Vin; simple, quiet, dissipative
  LDO                  a low-dropout linear regulator; used downstream of a
                       switcher to clean up noise
  buck                 step down; Vout = D times Vin in continuous conduction
  boost                step up; Vout = Vin/(1-D); has a right-half-plane zero
  buck-boost           either direction; inverting in the basic form
  SEPIC and Cuk        non-inverting step up or down; more components
  flyback              isolated, single magnetic component, low to medium power;
                       the workhorse for small isolated supplies
  forward              isolated, better at higher power than flyback
  half and full bridge  isolated, high power; four switches in the full bridge
  resonant / LLC       switches at zero voltage or current; much lower switching
                       loss, higher efficiency at high frequency
  charge pump          capacitors only; small, no inductor, limited current
  synchronous rectification  replace the diode with a transistor; eliminates the
                       diode drop and is essential at low output voltages
  continuous vs discontinuous conduction  whether inductor current reaches zero;
                       the transfer function and the control loop differ between
                       modes

Q: A buck converter is designed for a 5 V output from a 12 V input at 3 A. Replacing the
freewheeling diode with a MOSFET improves efficiency substantially. The reason is:
A. The MOSFET switches faster
B. The diode drops roughly 0.4-0.7 V while conducting most of the cycle, dissipating over a
   watt, whereas a low-resistance MOSFET drops far less
C. The MOSFET has lower capacitance
D. It reduces the inductor size
Reasoning: The diode conducts for the off-time, which here is about 58% of the period, carrying
3 A at a forward drop of half a volt or more — well over a watt of dissipation against a 15 W
output. A MOSFET with a few milliohms of on-resistance drops only tens of millivolts at the same
current. This is synchronous rectification, and its benefit grows as the output voltage falls.
Answer: B.

## Switching Devices and Losses

The switching device determines the achievable frequency, efficiency, and voltage rating. The
MOSFET dominates below a few hundred volts, switching fast with low conduction loss, and its
on-resistance rises substantially with temperature — roughly doubling from 25 to 125 degrees —
which must be accounted for in thermal design since the loss and the temperature reinforce each
other. The IGBT dominates at high voltage and high current with slower switching and a
voltage-drop characteristic rather than a resistance, making it better at high current and worse
at high frequency.

Wide bandgap devices have changed the design space. Silicon carbide and gallium nitride switch
faster with lower losses and tolerate higher temperatures and voltages, permitting higher
switching frequencies which in turn permit smaller magnetics and capacitors. The costs are price,
sensitivity to gate drive details, and the fact that very fast edges generate more
electromagnetic interference and demand far more careful layout.

Losses divide into conduction and switching, and the balance determines the optimal frequency.
Conduction loss is current squared times on-resistance and is independent of switching frequency.
Switching loss occurs during the transitions when voltage and current are simultaneously nonzero
and scales linearly with frequency. Raising the frequency therefore shrinks the passive
components and raises switching loss, and the optimum is where the total is minimized — which is
why wide bandgap devices, having much lower switching loss, move that optimum to a much higher
frequency.

Devices and losses:
  MOSFET               below a few hundred volts; fast, resistive conduction
  on-resistance         roughly doubles from 25 to 125 C; the thermal feedback
                       must be accounted for
  IGBT                 high voltage and current; slower, voltage-drop conduction
  silicon carbide      high voltage, fast, hot; permits higher frequency
  gallium nitride      very fast, low capacitance; extremely layout-sensitive
  body diode           intrinsic in a MOSFET; conducts during dead time, and its
                       slow reverse recovery is a loss and a noise source
  gate charge          the real figure of merit for switching speed; the driver
                       must supply it quickly
  gate driver          supplies peak current to charge the gate; an inadequate
                       driver slows the transition and raises switching loss
                       quadratically
  conduction loss      I squared R; frequency independent
  switching loss       overlap of voltage and current; linear in frequency
  gate drive loss      gate charge times voltage times frequency
  dead time            both switches off, preventing shoot-through; too long
                       forces body diode conduction, too short destroys the
                       devices
  soft switching       switch at zero voltage or current; nearly eliminates
                       switching loss and constrains the operating range
  shoot-through        both switches conducting simultaneously; a short across the
                       supply, and usually fatal

Dead time deserves emphasis because it is where a synchronous converter's reliability is
determined. Both switches in a half bridge must never conduct simultaneously, since that shorts
the input supply through them, so the controller inserts a dead time between turning one off and
the other on. Too little dead time risks shoot-through, which destroys the devices in
microseconds. Too much forces the body diode to conduct during the gap, which is lossy and whose
reverse recovery generates a current spike and substantial electromagnetic interference. The
correct value depends on the gate drive strength and the device capacitances, and it is
frequently the difference between a converter that meets its efficiency target and one that does
not.

## Magnetics and Control

The inductor is usually the largest, heaviest, and least standardized component in a converter,
and its design involves several interacting constraints. Inductance sets the current ripple for a
given voltage and frequency. The core must not saturate at peak current, since a saturated core
loses inductance abruptly and the current rises without limit. The winding must carry the current
without excessive loss, which at switching frequencies involves skin and proximity effects
concentrating current in a fraction of the conductor.

Core loss and copper loss trade against each other through the design. Copper loss falls with
more conductor area and rises with more turns; core loss rises with flux swing and frequency and
falls with a larger core. The balance is roughly equal loss in each at the optimum, and the
design proceeds by choosing a core size, computing the turns for the required inductance,
checking saturation, and iterating.

Control regulates the output against variations in input and load by adjusting the duty cycle,
and the loop must be stable. Voltage mode control compares the output to a reference and adjusts
the duty directly, which is simple and has a two-pole response from the output filter that
requires substantial compensation. Current mode control uses an inner loop regulating the
inductor current, which makes the outer loop see a single-pole response — far easier to
compensate — and provides inherent cycle-by-cycle current limiting.

Magnetics and control:
  inductance           sets ripple current for a given voltage-second product
  saturation           the core loses inductance abruptly; current then rises
                       uncontrolled
  core material        ferrite for high frequency, powdered iron for soft
                       saturation, amorphous and nanocrystalline for high
                       performance
  air gap              raises the saturation current and lowers the inductance;
                       most power inductors are gapped
  fringing             flux escaping around the gap; induces eddy currents in
                       nearby windings and conductors
  skin effect          current concentrates at the conductor surface at high
                       frequency
  proximity effect     adjacent conductors' fields redistribute current; often
                       larger than skin effect in multilayer windings
  litz wire            many insulated strands; mitigates both effects at
                       intermediate frequencies
  core loss            hysteresis and eddy currents; rises with flux swing and
                       frequency
  voltage mode         simple, two-pole response, more compensation
  current mode         inner current loop, single-pole outer response, inherent
                       current limit
  slope compensation   required in current mode above 50% duty cycle, or a
                       subharmonic oscillation appears
  loop bandwidth       typically a fifth to a tenth of the switching frequency
  phase margin         45 degrees or more; less produces ringing on load steps
  right-half-plane zero  in boost and flyback; fundamentally limits bandwidth and
                       cannot be compensated away

The right-half-plane zero deserves explanation because it is a genuine limit rather than a
tuning difficulty. In a boost converter, increasing the duty cycle to raise the output initially
lowers it, because more of the cycle is spent charging the inductor and less delivering to the
output. The output therefore moves the wrong way before moving the right way, which adds phase
lag without attenuation and cannot be cancelled by any compensator. The loop bandwidth must be
kept well below the zero's frequency, which limits transient response — and the zero moves lower
at higher duty cycle and higher load, so the worst case must be used.

[assistant] This is subharmonic oscillation in peak current mode control, and the trigger is
your duty cycle rather than the load directly.

The mechanism: in peak current mode, the switch turns off when the inductor current reaches a
threshold. A perturbation in the starting current propagates to the next cycle scaled by the
ratio of the down slope to the up slope, which equals the duty cycle divided by one minus the
duty cycle. Below 50% duty that ratio is less than one and perturbations decay; above 50% it
exceeds one and they grow, producing an alternating pattern at half the switching frequency. Your
duty is 0.42, close enough that load-dependent variation pushes it over.

The fix is slope compensation — add an artificial ramp to the current sense signal, which
changes the effective slope ratio and restores decay at any duty cycle. Most controllers have
either fixed internal slope compensation or a pin for setting it externally. The standard rule is
a compensating ramp of at least half the inductor's down slope, and a full down slope gives
deadbeat response to a perturbation.

Check whether your controller has internal compensation that is inadequate for your inductor
value. A larger inductance gives a shallower current slope, which makes a fixed internal ramp
relatively larger and helps; a smaller inductance makes it relatively smaller and hurts. If you
cannot adjust the compensation, increasing the inductor value is an alternative route to the same
result.

One thing to rule out first, since the symptom overlaps: verify that your output capacitor's
equivalent series resistance is within what the control loop was compensated for. Some
controllers rely on the ESR zero for phase margin, and replacing an electrolytic with a ceramic
capacitor removes it and destabilizes an otherwise fine design. If you changed the output
capacitor recently, that is the more likely cause and the fix is different — additional
compensation rather than slope compensation.

## Thermal, Battery Systems, and Protection

Thermal design determines whether a converter meets its rating, and the calculation is a chain
of thermal resistances from the junction to the ambient. Junction to case is a device property.
Case to heatsink depends on the interface material and mounting pressure. Heatsink to ambient
depends on the heatsink and the airflow. The temperature rise is the total dissipation times the
total resistance, and the junction temperature must remain below its rating with margin.

The thermal feedback in MOSFETs deserves specific attention. On-resistance rises with
temperature, so higher temperature means higher conduction loss, which means higher temperature.
For a well-designed system this converges to a stable operating point; for a marginal one it can
run away. The design must therefore use the on-resistance at the actual junction temperature
rather than the room temperature figure on the datasheet's front page, which is frequently
optimistic by a factor of two.

Battery systems add their own requirements. Lithium chemistries require charging within a
voltage and current envelope, with constant current until a target voltage and then constant
voltage until the current falls, and exceeding the voltage or charging outside a temperature
range degrades the cell and can cause thermal runaway. Cells in series must be balanced, since
capacity mismatch means one cell reaches its limit before the others and would be overcharged or
over-discharged without balancing.

Thermal, batteries, and protection:
  thermal resistance chain  junction to case to heatsink to ambient
  thermal runaway      on-resistance rises with temperature, raising dissipation
  derating             use the hot on-resistance and the actual ambient
  thermal vias         conduct heat through the board to more copper area
  lithium charging     constant current then constant voltage; temperature limits
  cell balancing       passive by bleeding the highest cells, active by
                       redistributing charge
  state of charge      estimated from voltage, coulomb counting, or a model; each
                       alone is inadequate and fusion is standard
  battery management   protection, balancing, state estimation, thermal management
  overcurrent          fuse, current limit, or foldback; foldback reduces current
                       under a fault and can latch up on a capacitive load
  overvoltage          crowbar or clamp; protects downstream circuitry
  undervoltage lockout  prevents operation below a safe input voltage, where the
                       gate drive would be insufficient and the switch would
                       operate in its linear region
  inrush limiting      NTC thermistor or an active limiter; prevents the initial
                       capacitor charging current from tripping protection or
                       welding contacts
  reverse polarity     a series MOSFET is far more efficient than a series diode
  isolation            required for safety at mains voltage; creepage and
                       clearance distances are set by standards

Undervoltage lockout is worth understanding because its absence produces a specific and
destructive failure. A MOSFET requires an adequate gate voltage to turn fully on; below that it
operates partially on with substantial resistance, dissipating heavily. During start-up or a
supply sag, a converter operating with insufficient gate drive can destroy its switches in
milliseconds. Lockout holds the converter off until the supply is adequate and adds hysteresis so
it does not chatter at the threshold.

## EMI and Layout

Switching converters generate electromagnetic interference by construction, because they
produce fast current and voltage transitions. The noise divides into conducted, travelling along
the input and output wiring, and radiated, emitted by loops and by long conductors acting as
antennas. Both are regulated and both must be addressed by design rather than by filtering
afterward, since a filter large enough to fix a badly laid out converter is expensive and
sometimes insufficient.

The critical loop is the single most important layout concept in the subject. In a buck
converter, the loop comprising the input capacitor, the high-side switch, and the low-side switch
carries a current that switches from zero to full load in nanoseconds. The loop's inductance
times that current slope produces a voltage spike, and the loop's area determines how much it
radiates. Minimizing this loop's physical area — placing the input capacitor immediately adjacent
to the switches with the shortest possible return path — is the highest-value layout action
available and frequently determines whether the design passes emissions testing.

The switch node is the corresponding voltage concern. It swings the full input voltage in
nanoseconds, so it is a strong capacitive noise source, and its area should be minimized —
large enough to conduct the current and carry heat, and no larger. Routing sensitive signals near
it, or placing a ground plane directly beneath a large switch node without considering the
coupling, injects noise into the control loop.

EMI and layout:
  critical loop        input capacitor and both switches; minimize its area above
                       all else
  switch node          minimize its area; it is a capacitive noise source
  input capacitor      place it immediately adjacent to the switches; bulk
                       capacitance further away is fine
  ground plane         continuous beneath the power stage; return current follows
                       the outgoing path
  feedback routing     away from the switch node and the inductor; route it as a
                       quiet trace referenced to the output ground
  current sense        Kelvin connections; sense across the element rather than
                       through the high-current path
  gate loop            the driver, gate, and source return; minimize it, or the
                       inductance slows switching and causes ringing
  snubber              an RC or RCD network damping ringing; costs efficiency and
                       is sometimes the pragmatic solution
  spread spectrum      dither the switching frequency; spreads the emission peaks
                       and reduces measured amplitude without reducing total
                       energy
  input filter         a pi or LC filter; check its interaction with the
                       converter's negative input impedance, since a filter with
                       inadequate damping can destabilize an otherwise stable
                       converter
  slew rate control    a gate resistor slows the edges; trades switching loss for

  shielding            a last resort; fixing the source is cheaper and more

The input filter interaction deserves the closing note because it produces a failure that is
baffling without knowing the mechanism. A regulated converter behaves as a negative incremental
input impedance — drawing more current when the input voltage falls, since it maintains constant
output power. An input filter with insufficient damping and an output impedance comparable to
that negative resistance forms an unstable system, and the converter oscillates at the filter's
resonance despite both the converter and the filter being individually correct. The remedy is
damping the filter, typically with a series resistor and capacitor across the filter capacitor,
and the design rule is that the filter's output impedance must remain well below the converter's
input impedance magnitude at all frequencies.
