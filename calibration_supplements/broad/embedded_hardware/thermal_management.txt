## Heat Transfer Fundamentals

Every watt a circuit consumes becomes heat, and thermal design is the discipline of moving that heat
to the ambient environment at an acceptable temperature rise. The three mechanisms are conduction
through solids, convection to a moving fluid, and radiation as electromagnetic emission, and a real
system uses all three in proportions that shift with geometry, temperature, and airflow.

Conduction is governed by the material's thermal conductivity, the cross-sectional area, and the
path length, and it is the mechanism that dominates within a device and its immediate mounting. The
enormous conductivity range across materials is what makes design possible: copper conducts roughly
two thousand times better than the epoxy in a circuit board and roughly ten thousand times better
than still air, so the design problem is usually one of arranging conductive paths from the source
to a surface where the other mechanisms can take over.

Convection to air is where most heat finally leaves, and its coefficient depends strongly on
whether the air moves. Natural convection, driven by buoyancy alone, gives coefficients in the
range of a few watts per square metre per kelvin. Forced convection with even modest airflow gives
an order of magnitude more, which is why a fan changes a thermal design categorically rather than
incrementally. Radiation is often dismissed and should not be: at typical electronics temperatures
it can carry a comparable share to natural convection from a dark surface, and it is free.

Q: A design that stays within temperature limits on the bench overheats when installed in its
enclosure. The primary cause is:
A. The components changed
B. The bench test had unrestricted natural convection and radiation to a cool room; the enclosure
   raises the local ambient temperature and restricts airflow, and both effects were absent from
   the test
C. Measurement error
D. Higher supply voltage
Reasoning: A board on a bench sits in an effectively infinite cool ambient with free airflow.
Inside an enclosure the internal air heats up, so the temperature difference driving convection
shrinks, and the enclosure walls block the airflow that was assumed. Thermal testing must be done
in the final mechanical configuration. Answer: B.

## Thermal Resistance Networks

The most useful analytical tool in practical thermal design is the resistance network analogy.
Temperature difference plays the role of voltage, heat flow the role of current, and thermal
resistance in kelvins per watt the role of electrical resistance. Series and parallel combination
rules apply directly, which means an entire thermal path can be analysed with the same intuition an
engineer already has for circuits.

A component's datasheet gives junction-to-case and junction-to-ambient resistances, and the
distinction matters greatly. Junction-to-ambient is measured on a specified test board under
specified conditions and rarely resembles the real installation; treating it as a property of the
part rather than of the part-plus-test-board is one of the most common thermal errors. Junction-to-
case is a property of the package and is the number to build a real network from.

The interface between a component and its heatsink is frequently the dominant resistance in the
chain, because two nominally flat surfaces touch at a small fraction of their apparent area and the
gaps are filled with air. Thermal interface material displaces the air with something conductive,
and the practical rules are that thinner is better, that excess material adds resistance rather than
removing it, and that mounting pressure matters as much as the material choice.

  the analogy          temperature as voltage, heat as current, resistance in
                       kelvins per watt
  series and parallel  combine as in circuits
  junction-to-case     a package property; the number to build a network from
  junction-to-ambient  a test-board property; not transferable to your design
  the common error     treating junction-to-ambient as intrinsic
  case-to-sink         dominated by the interface
  contact area         nominally flat surfaces touch at a small fraction of their
                       area
  thermal interface material  displaces the insulating air
  thinner is better    the material is a poorer conductor than the metals it
                       joins; it exists to fill gaps, not to conduct
  excess material      adds resistance; more is not better
  mounting pressure    as influential as material choice
  gap pads             for larger, less controlled gaps; higher resistance,
                       easier assembly
  phase change materials  conform under heat; good performance with simple
                       assembly
  spreading resistance  a small source into a large plate; the constriction near
                       the source can dominate and is missed by one-dimensional
                       analysis

## Board and Component Level Cooling

A printed circuit board is a thermal component, and treating it as one changes designs. Copper
planes conduct heat laterally very effectively; the board's epoxy conducts through its thickness
very poorly. The consequence is that heat spreads well within a layer and passes between layers
badly, which is why thermal vias exist — arrays of plated holes creating vertical copper paths
under a hot component.

Thermal via design follows a few rules that are frequently violated. Many small vias outperform few
large ones for a given copper area, because the plating thickness rather than the hole diameter
carries the heat. They should be placed directly under the thermal pad, not adjacent to it. They
should connect to a plane with substantial copper area, since a via array leading to an isolated
island accomplishes nothing. And filled or tented vias prevent solder from wicking away during
assembly, which is an assembly problem that manifests as a thermal one.

Component placement is the cheapest thermal lever and it is fixed early, which is why thermal
consideration must enter at the floorplanning stage rather than after routing. Hot components
spread out rather than clustered, thermally sensitive parts — voltage references, oscillators,
sensors — kept away from heat sources and from the airflow downstream of them, and orientation
chosen so that natural convection carries heat away from rather than across other components.

                       vertically

                       boundary layers merge and the inter-fin air stagnates

## Enclosures, Airflow, and System Level

At the system level the enclosure determines the thermal outcome more than any component choice. A
sealed enclosure must conduct all its heat through its walls to the outside surface, so its
external area and finish are the design variables, and internal fans in a sealed box move heat
around without removing any of it — though they do help by eliminating internal hot spots and
raising the effective internal convection coefficient.

Ventilated enclosures rely on air moving through, and the flow path must be designed rather than
assumed. Inlet low, outlet high, with the components arranged along the path in order of increasing
temperature tolerance, is the natural convection arrangement. Bypass — air finding a low-resistance
route that misses the components — is the standard failure, and blanking plates, baffles, and
sealed cable openings exist to prevent it.

Fan selection requires matching the fan curve to the system impedance curve. A fan rated for a
certain airflow delivers that only into free air, and the actual operating point is where its
pressure-flow curve intersects the system's resistance. A high-flow low-pressure fan performs badly
in a restricted enclosure and a smaller high-pressure fan may move more air through it, which is
counterintuitive from the specification numbers alone.

  sealed enclosure     all heat through the walls; external area and finish are
                       the variables
  internal fan in a sealed box  removes no heat and still helps by eliminating
                       hot spots

  baffles and blanking  force the air where it is needed
  fan curve            pressure against flow
  system impedance     the enclosure's resistance curve
  operating point      the intersection; free-air ratings are not what you get
  the counterintuitive result  a smaller high-pressure fan can outperform a

  acoustic constraint  fan noise scales steeply with speed; larger and slower is

  altitude derating    less dense air convects less; a design validated at sea
                       level can fail in a data centre at elevation

## Measurement, Transients, and Reliability

Thermal simulation is useful for comparing options and is not a substitute for measurement, because
its results depend on boundary conditions, material properties, and interface resistances that are
rarely known to better than tens of percent. The correct workflow uses simulation to choose among
designs and measurement to validate the chosen one.

Measurement technique determines whether the numbers mean anything. A thermocouple attached with a
gap reads the air rather than the surface. An infrared camera reports a temperature that depends on
the assumed emissivity, so a shiny metal surface reads far too cool unless it is coated or the
emissivity is corrected. On-die temperature sensors report the junction directly and are the most
trustworthy source where available. The most useful single practice is measuring the same point by
two different methods and investigating any disagreement.

Transient behaviour matters when the load is not steady. Thermal capacitance means temperature lags
power, so a short burst may not raise the junction significantly while a sustained load will. The
relevant time constants span from milliseconds for a die to minutes for a heatsink to tens of
minutes for an enclosure, and a design that survives a burst profile may fail on a duty cycle whose
average power is much lower but whose on-periods exceed the shortest relevant time constant.
