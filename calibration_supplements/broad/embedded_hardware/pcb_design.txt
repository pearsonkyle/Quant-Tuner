## Stackup and Board Construction

A printed circuit board is a laminated stack of conductive copper layers separated by
insulating substrate, and the stackup — how many layers, how thick, and in what arrangement —
is the first design decision and constrains everything after it. A two-layer board is cheapest
and provides no continuous reference plane, which limits it to low-speed work. A four-layer
board with signal, ground, power, and signal layers gives every signal an adjacent reference
plane and is the practical minimum for anything with fast edges or a microcontroller running
above a few tens of megahertz.

The reference plane is the reason layer count matters more than routing space. High-frequency
return current does not take the shortest resistive path back to the source; it takes the path
of least inductance, which is directly beneath the outgoing trace. A continuous plane under
every signal provides that path with a minimal loop area, which keeps emissions low, keeps
susceptibility low, and keeps the trace impedance controlled. Interrupting that plane with a
split, a slot, or a dense field of via antipads forces the return current to detour, creating a
large loop that both radiates and picks up interference.

Material choice affects loss and dimensional stability. Standard FR-4 is a glass-reinforced
epoxy that is inexpensive and adequate to a few gigahertz, with a dielectric constant around
4.3 that varies with frequency and with the glass weave. Low-loss laminates have lower and more
stable dielectric constants and lower dissipation factors, which matters above a few gigahertz
where FR-4's loss becomes significant. Flexible polyimide substrates permit bending, and
rigid-flex construction combines both in one assembly.

Stackup and materials:
  two layer            cheapest; no continuous reference plane; low speed only
  four layer           signal, ground, power, signal; the practical minimum for
                       fast edges
  six and above        more routing layers and more planes; better isolation
  reference plane      continuous, unbroken, directly adjacent to every signal
                       layer
  return current       follows the path of least inductance, directly beneath the
                       trace at high frequency
  plane splits         force return detours; never route a signal across one
  FR-4                 glass-epoxy; Dk ~4.3, adequate to a few GHz
  low-loss laminate    stable Dk, low loss tangent; above a few GHz
  polyimide            flexible; flex and rigid-flex
  copper weight        1 oz is ~35 um; heavier for high current
  controlled impedance  trace width and spacing to the reference set it; specify
                       it to the fabricator rather than calculating alone, since
                       it depends on their actual stackup
  symmetric stackup    balanced copper distribution prevents warping during
                       reflow

Impedance control is worth specifying explicitly rather than computing and hoping. The
characteristic impedance of a trace depends on its width, its height above the reference plane,
the dielectric constant, and the copper thickness, and the fabricator's actual layer thicknesses
differ from nominal. Providing the target impedance as a requirement lets them adjust the trace
width to their stackup, which is the only way to achieve the tolerance that high-speed
interfaces require.

Q: A high-speed signal trace crosses a gap in the ground plane beneath it. The most likely
consequence is:
A. No effect, since the signal path is unbroken
B. The return current must detour around the gap, creating a large loop that radiates and
   raises the impedance discontinuity
C. The trace resistance increases
D. The signal is shorted
Reasoning: At high frequency the return current flows directly beneath the trace. A gap forces
it around, and the resulting loop area produces electromagnetic emission, increased
susceptibility, and a local impedance discontinuity causing reflection. This is one of the most
common and most consequential layout errors, and it is invisible in a schematic. Answer: B.

## Schematic and Layout Practice

The schematic communicates circuit intent and should be organized for a human reader rather
than for the tool. Signals flowing left to right and power flowing top to bottom is the
convention that makes a schematic scannable. Functional blocks kept on separate sheets or in
separate regions with clear boundaries let a reader locate a subsystem. Net names that describe
function rather than tool-generated identifiers make the netlist readable and make debugging
possible.

Component selection at schematic time determines the layout's difficulty and the board's
manufacturability. Package choice affects assembly cost, rework feasibility, and thermal
behaviour. Availability determines whether the design can be built, and designing around a part
with a long lead time or a single source is a schedule risk that surfaces at the worst moment.
Checking availability and lifecycle status before committing is a step that is skipped often
enough to be worth stating.

Layout converts the schematic into physical geometry, and the sequence that works is placement
first, then power distribution, then critical signals, then everything else. Placement
determines most of the routing difficulty, and time spent iterating placement before routing
saves far more time than it costs. Components should be grouped by function, with the parts
associated with a given circuit kept together, and oriented consistently to simplify assembly
and inspection.

Layout sequence and practice:
  placement first      it determines routing difficulty more than anything else
  group by function    keep each subcircuit's parts together
  decoupling adjacent  place the smallest capacitor closest to the supply pin,
                       with the shortest possible loop to the planes
  crystal placement    close to the microcontroller, guard traces, no signals
                       routed underneath
  connector placement  at the board edge, oriented for the enclosure
  thermal placement    hot components spread out, away from temperature-sensitive
                       parts
  keep-out areas       antennas, mounting hardware, moving parts
  trace width          by current; roughly 1 mm per amp on 1 oz outer copper for
                       a 10 C rise, and check a calculator rather than guessing
  differential pairs   route together, matched length, consistent spacing
  length matching      for parallel buses; match to the timing budget, not
                       arbitrarily
  via stitching        connect planes together frequently; reduces plane
                       resonance and provides return paths near layer transitions
  return path vias     when a signal changes layers between different reference
                       planes, place a nearby via connecting those planes, or the
                       return current has no local path
  test points          plan them; probing a finished board without them is
                       difficult and often impossible

Return path vias are the detail most often omitted and most consequential in high-speed
designs. When a signal transitions from one layer to another, its return current must also
transition between reference planes, and it can only do so through a connection between them. If
the two planes are both ground, a stitching via near the signal via provides that path. If one
is ground and one is power, a decoupling capacitor near the transition provides an
alternating-current path. Without either, the return current travels to the nearest available
connection, which may be centimetres away, creating exactly the loop the stackup was designed to
avoid.

## Power Distribution and Signal Integrity

The power distribution network delivers current to every component with acceptable voltage
variation, and its behaviour is a frequency-domain problem rather than a direct-current one. A
digital circuit draws current in pulses synchronized to its clock, so the supply must present
low impedance across a wide frequency range — the bulk capacitors handle low frequencies, the
plane capacitance and small ceramics handle high frequencies, and the region between is where
resonances appear.

Decoupling capacitor selection is governed by inductance rather than by capacitance. Every
capacitor has a self-resonant frequency above which it behaves as an inductor, set by its
capacitance and its parasitic inductance, and that parasitic inductance is dominated by the
mounting geometry rather than by the part. A capacitor placed adjacent to the pin with short
wide traces and vias directly into the planes has perhaps a third the loop inductance of the
same part placed a centimetre away, which moves its useful frequency range up by a corresponding
factor. Placement matters more than value.

Signal integrity concerns whether a signal arrives recognizable, and the threshold for
concern is set by edge rate rather than by clock frequency. A signal whose propagation delay
along the trace exceeds roughly a sixth of its rise time behaves as a transmission line, and
modern logic families have edge rates of a nanosecond or less regardless of how slowly they are
clocked. A 1 MHz signal with a 500 picosecond edge requires the same transmission line treatment
as a 100 MHz one.

Power and signal integrity:
  PDN impedance        must be low across the frequency range the load demands
  bulk capacitors      low frequency; electrolytic or tantalum near the regulator
  ceramic decoupling   high frequency; adjacent to each supply pin
  self-resonance       every capacitor becomes inductive above it
  mounting inductance  dominated by loop geometry, not by the part
  anti-resonance       between capacitors of different values; a peak in
                       impedance where one is inductive and another capacitive
  plane capacitance    thin dielectric between power and ground planes provides
                       excellent high-frequency decoupling
  ferrite bead         isolates noisy sections; check its impedance at the
                       frequency of concern and its DC resistance at the current
  rise time threshold  treat as a transmission line when delay exceeds ~1/6 of the
                       rise time
  series termination   a resistor at the source; the common choice for
                       point-to-point
  reflection           from any impedance discontinuity; stubs, vias, connectors
  crosstalk            reduce by spacing, by a continuous reference, and by
                       avoiding long parallel runs
  ground bounce        from simultaneous switching outputs; spread them across
                       supply pins

[assistant] 240 MHz is the tenth harmonic of your 24 MHz clock, and the radiating structure is
almost certainly the 45 cm cable rather than the board itself. Boards are poor antennas at this
frequency; cables are excellent ones.

The mechanism is common-mode current. Something is developing a small voltage between the
board's ground reference and the cable's, and that drives common-mode current along the cable,
which radiates efficiently. Eight decibels over is a moderate failure and is usually fixable
without a respin.

Three things to address, in this order of root cause first.

Your three traces crossing plane splits are the most likely source of the ground voltage
gradient. Each forces return current to detour, developing a voltage across the plane
impedance, and that voltage appears between different points on your ground reference. Reroute
those three traces so they stay over continuous copper, or bridge the split with stitching
capacitors immediately adjacent to each crossing. This is the root cause fix.

Add a common-mode choke at the cable connector. It presents high impedance to common-mode
current while passing the differential signal, and it is the standard remedy for cable-driven
emissions. Choose one with adequate impedance at 240 MHz specifically — many are specified at
100 MHz and are much less effective an octave up.

Slow the clock edges if the driver permits it. Emission amplitude at the tenth harmonic falls
steeply with rise time, and a series resistor of a few tens of ohms at the clock source often
gives several decibels for one component. Verify the signal still meets timing and has adequate
edge quality at the receiver.

Two diagnostics worth running before committing. Use a near-field probe to find where on the
board the 240 MHz energy is strongest, which will confirm or refute the plane split hypothesis
directly. And repeat the emissions measurement with a ferrite clamp on the cable — if the level
drops substantially, the cable is confirmed as the radiator and the choke will work.

## Thermal, Manufacturability, and Assembly

Thermal design on a board is largely a matter of conduction into copper, since convection from
a small package is limited and the board itself is the heatsink for most components. Copper
pours connected to a device's thermal pad spread heat over a larger area from which it can be
convected, and thermal vias beneath the pad conduct it to the opposite side where more copper
area is available. The number and diameter of those vias, and whether they are filled, directly
determines the achievable thermal resistance.

Design for manufacturability means the board can be fabricated and assembled reliably at
reasonable cost, and the constraints come from the fabricator's process capability. Minimum
trace width and spacing, minimum drill size, annular ring, and aspect ratio all have standard
values that cost nothing and tighter values that cost more and reduce yield. Checking a design
against the intended fabricator's capabilities before release avoids the common experience of
receiving a quote with a surcharge for a feature that was unnecessary.

Assembly considerations shape footprint and placement decisions. Surface mount components are
placed by machine and require adequate spacing for the nozzle and for inspection. Component
orientation should be consistent to simplify programming and inspection. Polarity markings must
be present and unambiguous on the silkscreen, since an assembler with an ambiguous marking will
guess. Fiducial marks give the placement machine a reference and are required for fine-pitch
parts.

Thermal, manufacturing, and assembly:
  copper pour          the primary heat spreader; connect thermal pads to it
  thermal vias         conduct to the opposite side; more and larger is better,
                       and filled vias conduct better than open ones
  component derating   ratings fall with temperature; check at the actual
                       operating temperature
  thermal relief       spoke connections to planes for hand-solderable pads;
                       otherwise the plane sinks the heat and the joint does not
                       reflow
  minimum trace/space  standard values are cheap; tighter costs more and yields
                       worse
  annular ring         copper around a drilled hole; too little causes breakout
  aspect ratio         board thickness to drill diameter; limits plating
                       reliability
  solder mask dam      between fine-pitch pads; below a minimum width the
                       fabricator omits it
  paste stencil        aperture design controls solder volume; reduced apertures
                       for large thermal pads prevent floating
  fiducials            placement machine references; global and local for fine
                       pitch
  panelization         multiple boards per panel with breakaway tabs or v-scoring
  tombstoning          a small two-terminal part standing up during reflow from
                       asymmetric heating or pad design; symmetric pads and
                       thermal relief prevent it
  design rule check    run it; and confirm the rules match the actual fabricator

Tombstoning illustrates why footprint details matter. A small chip component with one pad
connected to a large copper pour and the other to a thin trace heats asymmetrically during
reflow, so one end's solder melts first and its surface tension lifts the part upright. The fix
is symmetric thermal connection to both pads, using thermal relief on the pour side, and it is
a footprint and layout decision rather than an assembly one — which is the general pattern for
assembly defects, most of which originate in design.

## Test, Bring-Up, and Documentation

Design for test means the board can be verified, and the effort is repaid the first time
something does not work. Test points on critical nets permit probing without soldering to a
component pin. A programming and debug connector must be accessible after assembly and ideally
after the board is in its enclosure. Boundary scan access permits testing interconnect without
physical probes. Separating power domains with a jumper or zero-ohm link allows each to be
brought up independently and current-limited.

Bring-up is the process of powering a new board for the first time, and doing it in stages
prevents a single fault from destroying multiple components. Inspect visually and check for
shorts between supplies and ground before applying power at all. Apply power through a
current-limited supply set just above the expected draw, so a short manifests as a current limit
rather than as smoke. Verify each supply rail in sequence before enabling the next. Only then
attempt to communicate with the processor.

Documentation is what makes a board maintainable by someone other than its designer, and it
outlives the designer's memory of the details. The schematic with readable net names is the
primary document. A bill of materials with manufacturer part numbers, not just values, is
required for reordering. Assembly drawings show component placement and orientation. A test
procedure states what to check and what constitutes a pass. And a revision history recording
what changed and why prevents the next person from reintroducing a problem that was already
solved.

Test and documentation:
  test points          on supplies, clocks, resets, and critical signals
  debug connector      accessible after assembly and in the enclosure
  boundary scan        interconnect testing without physical access
  power domain jumpers  bring up rails independently
  current-limited bring-up  a short becomes a current limit rather than damage
  staged power         verify each rail before enabling the next
  in-circuit test      bed of nails; production volume justifies the fixture
  functional test      exercise the actual function; catches what ICT cannot
  bill of materials    manufacturer part numbers, not just values
  assembly drawing     placement and orientation
  test procedure       what to check and what passes
  revision history     what changed and why
  errata               known issues and workarounds; write them down when found,
                       because they will be forgotten and rediscovered

The final practical point is to expect at least one revision. Boards rarely work perfectly on
the first attempt, and designs that leave room for correction — spare gates, unpopulated
component footprints at likely filter locations, extra test points, and generous space around
components that may need rework — make the second revision a modification rather than a
redesign. Adding these costs nearly nothing at design time and is consistently regretted when
omitted.
