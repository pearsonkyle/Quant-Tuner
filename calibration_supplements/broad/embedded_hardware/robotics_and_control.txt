## Feedback Control Fundamentals

A control system drives a plant toward a desired state. Open-loop control applies a
predetermined input with no measurement of the result — adequate when the plant is well
characterized and disturbances are negligible, as in a stepper motor commanded to a
position it can always reach. Closed-loop control measures the output, computes the error
against a reference, and adjusts the input accordingly, which buys robustness to
disturbances, to modelling error, and to parameter drift, at the cost of the possibility of
instability.

That tradeoff is the central fact of the subject. Feedback creates a loop, and a loop with
enough gain and enough phase lag oscillates. Everything in classical control theory is
machinery for determining how much gain can be applied before that happens and how to shape
the loop so that more can be applied safely.

System response is characterized by a handful of quantities that translate directly into
requirements. Rise time measures speed. Overshoot measures how far past the target the
response goes. Settling time measures how long until it stays within a tolerance band.
Steady-state error measures the residual offset after transients decay. These trade against
each other: a faster response generally means more overshoot, and eliminating steady-state
error generally slows the response.

Response characteristics of a second-order system:
  natural frequency      omega_n; sets the timescale
  damping ratio          zeta; sets the character
  zeta = 0               undamped, sustained oscillation
  0 < zeta < 1           underdamped, overshoot with decaying oscillation
  zeta = 1               critically damped, fastest without overshoot
  zeta > 1               overdamped, slow, no overshoot
  overshoot              exp(-pi zeta / sqrt(1 - zeta^2)); ~4.3% at zeta = 0.707
  settling time (2%)     ~4 / (zeta omega_n)
  rise time              ~1.8 / omega_n for zeta ≈ 0.7
  common design target   zeta ≈ 0.7, a good compromise of speed and overshoot

Stability in the classical framework is assessed from the loop's frequency response. Gain
margin is how much the loop gain can increase before instability; phase margin is how much
additional phase lag can be tolerated. Typical design targets are a gain margin above 6 dB
and a phase margin above 45 degrees, and these are not arbitrary — they provide room for the
modelling error, the sensor and computation delay, and the parameter variation that a real
system will exhibit and a model will not capture.

Q: A control loop is tuned to be very fast on the bench and oscillates when the mechanism
is loaded. The most likely explanation is:
A. The sensor failed
B. Loading changed the plant dynamics, reducing the phase margin the tuning assumed
C. The setpoint is too high
D. The sample rate is too high
Reasoning: Adding load changes inertia and often adds compliance, lowering the plant's
resonant frequency and adding phase lag. A tuning with marginal phase margin on the
unloaded system has none on the loaded one. This is why controllers should be tuned under
representative load and designed with margin rather than to the edge of stability.
Answer: B.

## PID Control

The proportional-integral-derivative controller remains the workhorse of industrial
control, comprising the overwhelming majority of deployed loops, because it requires no
model and its three terms have intuitive effects.

The proportional term produces an output proportional to the current error. Increasing it
speeds the response and reduces steady-state error, and past a point produces overshoot and
then oscillation. It cannot eliminate steady-state error in general, because a nonzero
output requires a nonzero error to generate it — a motor holding a load against gravity needs
continuous torque, which the proportional term can only supply by maintaining a position
error.

The integral term accumulates error over time, so any persistent error eventually produces
whatever output is needed to eliminate it. This removes steady-state error, which is its
entire purpose, at the cost of adding phase lag that reduces stability margin, and of
introducing windup.

The derivative term responds to the rate of change of error, providing anticipatory damping
that permits higher proportional gain. Its weakness is noise: differentiation amplifies
high-frequency content, so a noisy measurement produces a violently noisy derivative output.
It must be filtered, and it should be computed on the measurement rather than on the error,
so that a step change in setpoint does not produce a derivative spike — the derivative kick.

PID practice:
  parallel form         u = Kp e + Ki integral(e) + Kd de/dt
  derivative on meas.   differentiate -y rather than e; avoids setpoint kick
  derivative filtering  a first-order filter with N between 8 and 20 times the
                        derivative time constant; unfiltered D is unusable
  integral windup       when the actuator saturates, the integral keeps growing
                        and the loop overshoots badly on recovery
  anti-windup           clamp the integrator, or back-calculate from the saturated
                        output; mandatory on any loop that can saturate
  bumpless transfer     initialize the integrator on mode change so the output does
                        not jump
  feedforward           add a model-based term for known demands; the loop then
                        corrects only the residual, which is far easier
  sample rate           10-20x the closed-loop bandwidth; faster is not free
                        because it amplifies quantization and noise in D

Integral windup is the failure mode most likely to appear in a working system and surprise
its author. When the actuator saturates — a valve fully open, a motor at current limit — the
error persists because the plant cannot respond, so the integrator continues accumulating to
a very large value. When the plant finally reaches the setpoint, the integrator must unwind
before the output comes down, producing a large overshoot and a long recovery. The symptom
is a loop that behaves well for small setpoint changes and badly for large ones, and the
fix is anti-windup logic that stops or reverses accumulation while saturated.

Tuning methods range from systematic to empirical. Ziegler-Nichols provides a classical
starting point from the ultimate gain and period, and it is known to produce aggressive,
oscillatory tuning that usually needs detuning. Relay auto-tuning drives the loop into a
controlled oscillation to identify the same parameters automatically. Model-based tuning
such as lambda or internal model control derives gains from an identified plant model and a
desired closed-loop time constant, giving a single intuitive tuning knob. Manual tuning —
raise proportional gain until oscillation, back off, add integral for steady-state error,
add derivative only if needed and if the signal is clean enough — remains common and works.

## State Space and Modern Control

State-space representation describes a system by a first-order vector differential equation
in the state, with matrices mapping state and input to state derivative and output. Its
advantages over transfer functions are that it handles multiple inputs and outputs
naturally, exposes internal states rather than only the input-output relationship, extends
directly to time-varying and nonlinear systems, and is the natural form for numerical
computation.

Two structural properties determine what is achievable. Controllability asks whether the
input can drive the state anywhere in finite time, tested by the rank of the controllability
matrix. Observability asks whether the state can be reconstructed from the output history,
tested by the rank of the observability matrix. A system that is not controllable has modes
the input cannot influence; one that is not observable has modes the sensor cannot see. Both
are checked before designing anything, because no amount of control design fixes a structural
deficiency — the answer is a different actuator or a different sensor.

State feedback places the closed-loop poles wherever desired, provided the system is
controllable, by choosing a gain matrix. Pole placement gives complete freedom and no
guidance about where to place them; the linear quadratic regulator instead minimizes a
quadratic cost balancing state error against control effort, producing an optimal gain from
two weighting matrices that have direct engineering meaning. LQR is the standard approach
because tuning two cost weights is far more intuitive than choosing pole locations, and the
result comes with guaranteed stability margins.

State-space and estimation:
  state equation       x' = A x + B u;   y = C x + D u
  controllability      rank[B, AB, A^2 B, ...] = n
  observability        rank[C; CA; CA^2; ...] = n
  pole placement       u = -K x; place closed-loop eigenvalues of (A - BK)
  LQR                  minimize integral of (x'Qx + u'Ru); Q penalizes state error,
                       R penalizes effort
  observer             estimate the state from inputs and outputs; place observer
                       poles faster than controller poles
  separation principle controller and observer can be designed independently for
                       linear systems
  Kalman filter        the optimal observer under Gaussian noise; the observer gain
                       is computed from process and measurement noise covariances
  LQG                  LQR plus Kalman filter; optimal but with no guaranteed
                       robustness margins, unlike LQR alone
  integral action      augment the state with the integral of the error to remove
                       steady-state offset

The Kalman filter is worth understanding as a recursive Bayesian update rather than as a
formula. It maintains a state estimate and a covariance representing uncertainty. The
prediction step propagates both through the system model, increasing uncertainty by the
process noise. The update step incorporates a measurement, combining prediction and
measurement weighted by their relative uncertainties — so a precise measurement pulls the
estimate strongly and a noisy one barely moves it. The two tuning parameters are the process
noise covariance, expressing how much you trust the model, and the measurement noise
covariance, expressing how much you trust the sensor, and their ratio determines the
filter's responsiveness. Setting process noise too low produces a filter that ignores
reality; too high produces one that simply follows the noisy measurement.

## Kinematics and Actuation

Forward kinematics computes end-effector pose from joint angles and is straightforward: a
chain of transformations, one per joint, composed in order. Inverse kinematics computes the
joint angles achieving a desired pose and is the hard direction — it may have no solution
if the target is outside the workspace, multiple solutions when the arm can reach the same
point in different configurations, or infinitely many for a redundant manipulator with more
joints than task dimensions. Analytical solutions exist for common geometries; general cases
use iterative numerical methods based on the Jacobian.

The Jacobian relates joint velocities to end-effector velocity and is the central object of
manipulator analysis. It maps joint rates to Cartesian rates, its transpose maps
end-effector forces to joint torques, and its singular values describe the manipulator's
ability to move and exert force in each direction. Singularities occur where the Jacobian
loses rank — the arm fully extended, or two axes aligned — and at those configurations the
manipulator loses a degree of freedom and the inverse Jacobian blows up, commanding
enormous joint velocities for small Cartesian motions. Practical controllers detect
proximity to singularities and use a damped least-squares inverse that trades exactness for
bounded velocity.

Actuator selection drives the mechanical design more than any other choice.

Actuator characteristics:
  brushed DC          simple drive, brushes wear, torque proportional to current
  brushless DC        higher efficiency and life, needs commutation and rotor
                      position sensing or sensorless estimation
  stepper             open-loop position, no feedback needed, loses steps under
                      overload with no indication, poor torque at speed
  servo (BLDC+enc)    closed-loop position with an encoder; the standard for
                      precision motion
  hydraulic           very high force density, stiff, messy, expensive
  pneumatic           compliant, fast, hard to position precisely due to
                      compressibility
  series elastic      deliberate compliance with force sensing; safe for human
                      interaction, lower bandwidth
  direct drive        no gearbox, no backlash, backdrivable, needs a large motor
  gear reduction      multiplies torque by the ratio, divides speed, multiplies
                      reflected inertia by the ratio squared, adds backlash and
                      friction

The reflected inertia relationship is the one most often missed in mechanical design. A
gearbox multiplies the load inertia seen by the motor by the square of the reduction ratio,
so a 100:1 reduction makes the load appear 10,000 times heavier in inertial terms. This is
usually beneficial, since it means the motor's own inertia dominates and the load is easy to
control, and it is precisely why high-reduction systems are not backdrivable and cannot
sense external forces through motor current. A robot intended to interact safely with people
needs either low reduction, series elastic actuation, or explicit force sensing, because a
high-reduction arm cannot feel what it hits.

The accounting: 3.5 ms of sensor latency plus a 500 Hz control loop contributes about 6.5 ms
of total loop delay, which at 30 Hz is 70 degrees of phase lag. Your actuator's 45 Hz
bandwidth adds another 34 degrees. That is over 100 degrees of phase the simulation never
saw, and it puts your estimated phase margin slightly negative — which is exactly an
oscillation at 30 Hz.

Fixes in order of what I would try, starting with the one that costs nothing.

Reduce the gains, particularly Kd. A derivative gain of 4 against a proportional gain of 180
is providing damping at frequencies where the delay has already inverted its effect, so it is
destabilizing rather than damping above about 20 Hz. Cut Kd first and add a low-pass filter
on the velocity estimate with a corner around 30-50 Hz. Then reduce Kp until the oscillation
disappears with margin, and only then raise it back carefully.

Reduce the delay. Raising the control rate from 500 Hz to 1 kHz halves that component.
More importantly, find where the 3.5 ms of sensor latency comes from — encoder filtering,
bus transport, buffering in a driver — because that is usually reducible and it is pure loss.
If the encoder is on a serial bus polled once per cycle, moving to a synchronous read at the
top of the loop can eliminate a whole cycle of delay.

Then fix the simulation, because otherwise this recurs on the next controller. Add the
measured delay as an explicit transport lag and model the actuator as at least a first-order
lag at its measured bandwidth. A simulation without these systematically permits gains that
hardware cannot support, which is the single most common cause of sim-to-real failure in
legged robots.

If you need the high gains for stiffness, the durable solution is a model-based feedforward
term — computed torque or inverse dynamics — so the feedback loop only handles the residual
error and can run at much lower gain.

## Planning, Estimation, and Implementation

Motion planning finds a collision-free path from start to goal in configuration space,
where each dimension is a degree of freedom and obstacles map to forbidden regions. The
problem is high-dimensional for anything beyond a simple mechanism, which rules out exact
methods and motivates sampling. Rapidly-exploring random trees grow a tree by sampling
random configurations and extending toward them, finding feasible paths quickly in high
dimensions; probabilistic roadmaps build a reusable graph, which pays off when many queries
share the same environment. Both are probabilistically complete — they find a solution if
one exists, given enough time — and neither gives optimality without variants such as
RRT-star that rewire toward lower cost.

Trajectory optimization takes a different approach, formulating the problem as constrained
optimization over a parameterized trajectory with cost terms for effort, smoothness, and
obstacle proximity. It produces smooth, dynamically feasible motions directly rather than a
geometric path requiring post-processing, at the cost of local minima and sensitivity to
initialization. Model predictive control applies this idea in a receding horizon: optimize
over a short future window, execute the first step, and re-optimize — which handles
constraints and disturbances naturally and requires solving an optimization problem at every
control cycle, which is why its adoption tracked the availability of fast embedded solvers.

State estimation fuses multiple sensors, each with complementary weaknesses. An inertial
measurement unit gives high-rate acceleration and angular rate that drift when integrated.
Encoders give precise joint positions and cannot observe slip. Vision and lidar give
absolute position at low rate with occasional gross failures. Fusing them yields an estimate
better than any individually, and the standard tools are the extended Kalman filter for
mildly nonlinear systems, the unscented variant for stronger nonlinearity, and particle
filters for multimodal distributions where a single Gaussian is inadequate.

Real-time implementation constraints:
  determinism over speed  a controller that meets its deadline every cycle beats
                          one that is faster on average and occasionally late
  fixed control period    jitter in the sampling interval is equivalent to noise in
                          the derivative and to phase uncertainty in the loop
  measure the jitter      instrument the loop period; do not assume it
  no dynamic allocation   in the control path; unbounded latency
  bound every loop        no unbounded iteration inside a real-time task
  watchdog                a hung controller with an active actuator is dangerous
  safe state on fault     define what the actuator does when control is lost, and
                          make it the default rather than a commanded action
  saturate and rate-limit outputs, always; the plant has limits and the controller
                          should respect them explicitly
  units and frames        the largest source of real robot bugs; document the frame
                          and unit of every quantity at every interface

Frame and unit errors deserve the last word because they cause more robot failures than
control theory does. A quantity expressed in the wrong frame, an angle in degrees where
radians were expected, a sign convention differing between the encoder and the model, a
transform composed in the wrong order — each produces a system that moves confidently in
the wrong direction, and none of them is caught by any amount of control tuning. The
defenses are naming conventions that encode the frame in the variable name, unit types
enforced by the type system where the language permits, and a startup self-test that
commands a small known motion and verifies the measured response has the expected sign and
magnitude in every joint before anything else runs.
