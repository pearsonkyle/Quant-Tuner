## Design for Test

Testability is a design property, not a manufacturing activity, and a board designed without it can
be effectively untestable at any cost. The decisions that determine this are made during schematic
capture and layout, months before the first unit is built, which is why test engineering
participation early is worth far more than any amount of effort later.

Test points are the most basic requirement: accessible pads on every net that needs to be probed,
sized and spaced for the fixture technology, and on one side of the board where possible since
double-sided fixtures cost several times more. Nets without access can only be tested indirectly if
at all, and the common regret is a design where a fault can be detected but not localized, so a
failing board is scrapped rather than repaired.

Boundary scan provides access without physical probes by shifting test data through a chain of cells
at device pins, which is how dense boards with no physical access to interior nets are tested at
all. It requires the devices to support it and requires the chain to be routed and terminated
correctly, and a broken chain makes the whole facility unavailable — so its continuity is itself the
first thing a test programme checks.

Design for test:
  testability is designed  a board without it is untestable at any cost
  early involvement    test engineering during schematic and layout
  test points          accessible pads sized and spaced for the fixture
  single-sided access  double-sided fixtures cost several times more
  the localization requirement  detecting a fault is not enough if it cannot be
                       located
  boundary scan        test data shifted through cells at device pins
  chain integrity      a broken chain disables the whole facility
  in-circuit test access  isolating components electrically for individual
                       measurement
  built-in self test   the device testing itself and reporting; free coverage
                       where available
  test modes           firmware entry points exercising hardware directly
  the debug interface  kept accessible in production or fault analysis becomes
                       guesswork
  provisioning access  serial numbers, calibration data, and keys written during
                       test; the mechanism must exist before the first unit
  coverage analysis    which faults the test set would detect; a number worth
                       computing rather than assuming

Q: A production board fails functional test with a symptom that could be caused by any of six
components, and there is no way to distinguish among them. This indicates:
A. A manufacturing defect
B. A design-for-test deficiency — the test strategy can detect the fault but not localize it, so
   every failure becomes a scrapped board rather than a repaired one, and adding access at design
   time would have prevented it
C. An inadequate test program
D. Poor component quality
Reasoning: Fault localization is a property of the access the design provides and the structure of
the test set. Without measurement points between the candidate components, no test program can
distinguish them. This is the standard consequence of designing the test strategy after the layout
is complete. Answer: B.

## Assembly Processes

Surface mount assembly is a sequence of processes each with characteristic defects. Solder paste is
printed through a stencil onto the pads; components are placed by machine; the assembly passes
through a reflow oven whose thermal profile melts the solder and forms the joints. The great
majority of assembly defects originate in the printing step, which makes stencil design, paste
condition, and print inspection disproportionately important.

The reflow profile is a temperature-versus-time curve with distinct zones: preheat raising the
assembly gradually, soak allowing temperature to equalize and flux to activate, reflow above the
solder's melting point for a controlled duration, and cooling at a controlled rate. Deviations cause
specific defects — insufficient soak causes voiding and poor wetting, excessive time above melting
degrades the joint, and rapid cooling induces stress. The profile must be measured on an actual
assembly with attached thermocouples, since the oven's setpoints and the board's actual temperature
differ substantially and unpredictably with thermal mass.

Through-hole components are placed and soldered separately, by wave soldering or by selective
soldering or by hand, which is why a mixed-technology board costs more than its component count
suggests. Reducing the number of process steps is a real cost lever, and choosing surface-mount
alternatives for the few through-hole parts on an otherwise surface-mount board frequently saves
more than the component price difference.

Assembly processes:
  the sequence         print, place, reflow
  the defect origin    the majority originate in printing
  stencil design       aperture size and shape per pad; not simply the pad outline
  paste condition      temperature, age, and handling affect print quality
  print inspection     measuring deposited volume; catches defects before
                       components are placed
  placement accuracy   machine capability versus component pitch
  reflow profile       preheat, soak, reflow, cooling
  profile measurement  thermocouples on an actual assembly, not oven setpoints
  thermal mass effects  large components lag; the profile must satisfy both
                       extremes
  insufficient soak    voiding and poor wetting
  excessive reflow time  degraded joints
  tombstoning          a small component standing up when one end wets first
  the pad symmetry cause  asymmetric thermal mass at the two pads
  through-hole processes  wave, selective, or hand soldering
  the mixed-technology cost  extra process steps beyond the component price
  cleaning             required for some flux chemistries and not others; the
                       choice affects both process cost and long-term reliability

## Inspection and Functional Test

Inspection and test form a sequence, each catching what the previous stage cannot and each more
expensive per unit. Automated optical inspection compares the assembly against a reference image and
catches presence, orientation, and gross placement and solder defects quickly and cheaply. X-ray
inspection sees joints hidden beneath package bodies, which is the only way to inspect the
increasingly common packages with connections underneath. In-circuit test measures components
individually through the fixture. Functional test operates the board as a product.

The economic principle governing this sequence is that the cost of a defect rises by roughly an
order of magnitude at each stage it escapes — cheap at inspection, more at test, much more at system
assembly, and enormous in the field. This is what justifies inspection stages that seem redundant,
and it is the argument to make when inspection is proposed for removal on cost grounds.

Functional test verifies the board does what it is supposed to do, and its design is a real
engineering problem. It must be fast, since test time multiplies by production volume; it must be
deterministic, since intermittent test failures are indistinguishable from intermittent product
faults and destroy confidence in the whole line; and it must diagnose rather than merely pass or
fail, since a failure with no diagnostic information is a scrapped board. Test fixtures themselves
wear and drift and require their own calibration and periodic verification against known-good and
known-bad units.

Inspection and test:
  optical inspection   presence, orientation, gross defects; fast and cheap
  X-ray inspection     joints hidden beneath packages
  in-circuit test      components measured individually through a fixture
  functional test      the board operated as a product
  the escape cost      roughly an order of magnitude per stage
  the redundancy argument  apparently overlapping stages are economically
                       justified by that escalation
  test time            multiplied by volume; a first-order cost
  determinism          intermittent test failures destroy line confidence
  diagnostics          a pass-fail result with no information means scrap
  fixture wear         probes and contacts degrade; scheduled replacement
  fixture verification  against known-good and known-bad units
  false failure rate   the fraction of good units failing; costs yield directly
  false pass rate      the fraction of bad units passing; costs field returns
  the tuning tension   tightening limits reduces escapes and increases false
                       failures, and the correct setting depends on the relative
                       costs, which should be estimated rather than guessed
  guard banding        test limits tightened relative to specification limits by
                       the measurement uncertainty

## Calibration, Traceability, and Data

Any measurement in a production process is only as good as the calibration of the instrument making
it, and calibration must be traceable — linked through an unbroken chain of comparisons to a
recognized standard, each with stated uncertainty. An instrument with an expired calibration
invalidates every measurement made with it since it was last verified, which is why the calibration
schedule is enforced administratively rather than left to judgement.

Measurement system analysis quantifies whether a measurement process is adequate for the tolerance
it is checking. Repeatability is variation when the same operator measures the same unit
repeatedly; reproducibility is variation between operators or between instruments. Their combined
magnitude relative to the tolerance determines whether the measurement can distinguish good from
bad, and a measurement system consuming a large fraction of the tolerance produces false
acceptances and rejections regardless of how well the process itself performs.

Production data collection is what makes everything else improvable. Recording every measurement
against a unit serial number produces a dataset supporting yield analysis, trend detection, failure
correlation, and field return investigation. The discipline is to record measured values rather than
pass-fail results, because a value near the limit is information about a drifting process while a
pass is not, and a line that records only pass-fail cannot detect a problem until it is producing
failures.

Calibration and data:
  traceability         an unbroken chain to a recognized standard
  stated uncertainty   at every link in the chain
  the expiry consequence  measurements since the last verification are invalid
  administrative enforcement  schedules rather than judgement
  measurement system analysis  is the measurement adequate for the tolerance
  repeatability        same operator, same unit, repeated
  reproducibility      between operators and instruments
  the tolerance fraction  a measurement consuming much of the tolerance produces
                       false results regardless of process quality
  record values not verdicts  a value near the limit is information
  serial number association  every measurement tied to a unit
  yield analysis       what fails, where, and how often
  trend detection      drift caught before it produces failures
  statistical process control  control limits derived from the process, not from
                       the specification; the two are frequently confused and
                       they answer different questions
  the specification-control distinction  control limits describe what the process
                       does, specification limits describe what is acceptable, and
                       a process in control can still be producing out-of-
                       specification units

## Yield, Failure Analysis, and Ramp

Yield is the fraction of units passing at each stage, and the compound yield across a multi-stage
process is the product of the stage yields, which means that several stages at ninety-five percent
compound to something considerably worse than any of them individually suggests. Improving the worst
stage first is usually correct and requires knowing which it is, which requires the per-stage data.

Failure analysis determines why a unit failed, and its discipline is to establish the mechanism
rather than to find something that looks wrong. The sequence is non-destructive first — visual
inspection, electrical characterization, X-ray — then progressively destructive: decapsulation,
cross-sectioning, microscopy. Reversing this order destroys the evidence that would have identified
the cause, and the most common failure analysis error is opening the part before characterizing it
electrically.

Production ramp is the transition from prototype to volume, and its difficulties are consistently
underestimated. Processes that work at low volume with attentive engineers fail at volume with
operators following procedures. Components sourced in tens have different availability and variation
in tens of thousands. Yield at pilot volume is not yield at production volume. The practical
mitigations are building pilot runs on production tooling with production procedures, qualifying
alternative components before they are needed, and treating the first production run as a test of the
process rather than as a source of shippable product.

Yield, analysis, and ramp:
  stage yield          the fraction passing at each stage
  compound yield       the product; worse than any stage suggests
  the improvement target  the worst stage, which requires per-stage data
  first pass yield     passing without rework; the honest measure
  rework               costs more than it appears and introduces its own defects
  failure analysis     establish the mechanism, not merely find something wrong
  the sequence         non-destructive before destructive
  the common error     opening the part before characterizing it electrically
  cross-sectioning     the definitive view of a solder joint or a via
  root cause versus contributing factor  usually several factors, and stopping at
                       the first plausible one is how problems recur
  corrective action    addressing the mechanism; verification that it worked
  production ramp      prototype to volume
  the underestimation  processes working with engineers fail with operators
  pilot runs           production tooling, production procedures, production
                       operators
  second sourcing      qualified before it is needed, not during a shortage
  the first run framing  a test of the process rather than a source of shippable
                       product, which is the expectation-setting that determines
                       whether the ramp is managed or endured
