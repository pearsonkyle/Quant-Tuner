## Motor Types and Selection

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical motion through the interaction of
magnetic fields, and the types differ in how those fields are produced and switched. The choice
determines the drive electronics, the control complexity, the sensing requirement, and the
achievable performance, so it is the decision that constrains everything downstream.

Brushed DC motors use mechanical commutation: brushes contacting a rotating commutator switch the
current through the windings as the rotor turns, so the torque remains in one direction without any
electronics. This makes the drive trivially simple — apply voltage and it turns — and it makes the
brushes a wear item that limits life, generates electrical noise, and produces dust. Torque is
proportional to current and speed is proportional to voltage, which makes the control intuitive.

Brushless motors move the commutation into electronics, which eliminates the wear and permits far
higher efficiency, speed, and power density. The cost is that the drive must know the rotor
position to energize the correct windings, which requires either position sensors or an estimation
scheme. Two variants exist and are frequently confused: trapezoidal motors have concentrated
windings and are driven with block commutation, while sinusoidal motors have distributed windings
and are driven with sinusoidal currents, giving smoother torque at the cost of more complex
control.

Stepper motors move in discrete steps determined by the winding energization sequence, which
permits open-loop position control with no feedback at all. This is their entire appeal and their
central weakness: if the load exceeds the available torque, the motor loses steps silently and the
controller's position estimate is wrong with no indication. They also produce full holding current
regardless of load, so they run hot and consume power even when stationary.

Q: A stepper motor system positions correctly at low speed and accumulates error at high
acceleration. The most likely cause is:
A. Encoder failure
B. The motor is losing steps because the required torque exceeds what is available at that speed,
   and open-loop stepping has no way to detect it
C. Incorrect microstepping
D. Supply voltage too high
Reasoning: A stepper's available torque falls with speed because back EMF opposes the drive
voltage and the winding inductance limits current rise. If the acceleration demands more torque
than is available, the rotor falls behind and steps are lost, with no feedback to indicate it. The
remedies are a gentler acceleration profile, a higher supply voltage to force current faster, a
larger motor, or closed-loop operation. Answer: B.

## Drive Electronics

The power stage converts the supply into controlled current in the motor windings, and the
half-bridge is the fundamental building block: two switches in series across the supply with the
motor connected at their midpoint. Switching the upper device connects the winding to the supply
and the lower connects it to ground, so the average voltage is controlled by the duty ratio.

Three half-bridges form the three-phase inverter that drives brushless and induction motors, and
one or two form the arrangements driving brushed motors — a single half-bridge gives one direction
with braking, and a full bridge of two gives both directions.

The dead time between switching one device off and the other on is what prevents shoot-through,
where both conduct simultaneously and short the supply. It must be long enough for the first
device to turn off fully under all conditions and short enough that the resulting voltage
distortion is tolerable. Too little destroys the devices; too much distorts the output waveform and
degrades low-speed performance, which is why dead-time compensation is implemented in
performance-oriented drives.

Current measurement is required for any torque control and the placement determines what is
measurable. A shunt in each phase gives direct measurement of every phase at the cost of level
shifting from the switching node. A shunt in the low side of each leg is easier to measure and is
valid only while the low-side device conducts, which constrains the timing. A single shunt in the
supply return is cheapest and requires reconstruction of the phase currents from the switching
state, which fails at extreme duty ratios.

Drive electronics:
  half bridge          two switches; duty ratio sets the average voltage
  full bridge          two half bridges; bidirectional
  three-phase inverter  three half bridges; brushless and induction
  dead time            prevents shoot-through; too little destroys, too much
                       distorts
  dead-time compensation  correct the resulting distortion in software
  bootstrap            supplies the high-side gate drive; requires the low side to
                       conduct periodically to recharge, which constrains
                       hundred-percent duty operation
  gate driver          supplies peak current to switch quickly; an underpowered
                       driver raises switching loss quadratically
  shoot-through        both devices conducting; a short across the supply
  current sensing      phase shunts, low-side shunts, or a single supply shunt;
                       each trades cost against what is measurable and when
  regeneration         a decelerating motor returns energy; the supply must absorb
                       it or the bus voltage rises
  brake resistor       dissipates regenerated energy where the supply cannot
                       absorb it; required for high-inertia decelerating loads
  bus capacitance      absorbs switching ripple and regenerated energy; sized for
                       ripple current as much as for capacitance
  PWM frequency        above audibility if noise matters; higher costs switching
                       loss and permits lower current ripple

Regeneration deserves emphasis because it produces a failure that surprises people. A motor
decelerating a load acts as a generator and returns energy to the supply, raising the bus voltage.
A battery absorbs it, a supply with a diode input cannot, and the voltage rises until something
fails — typically the bus capacitors or the switches. Any system decelerating a substantial inertia
from a rectified supply needs either a brake resistor switched in when the bus rises or a control
strategy that limits the deceleration rate, and discovering this after the first hard stop is a
common experience.

## Commutation and Control

Commutation is the sequencing of winding currents to maintain torque as the rotor turns, and its
sophistication determines the torque quality. Six-step or trapezoidal commutation energizes two of
three phases at a time in a sequence determined by the rotor sector, which requires only coarse
position information — three Hall sensors give sixty-degree resolution, which is sufficient. Its
cost is torque ripple, since the torque varies within each sector, producing audible noise and
speed variation at low speed.

Field oriented control drives all three phases with sinusoidal currents whose phase is
continuously matched to the rotor position, which gives smooth torque and the best efficiency. It
works by transforming the three phase currents into a rotating reference frame where they become
two direct-current quantities — one producing torque and one producing flux — which can then be
controlled independently by ordinary regulators. This transformation is what makes the control
tractable, and it requires accurate continuous rotor position rather than sector information.

The control structure is a cascade of nested loops, and the ordering follows from their
bandwidths. The innermost current loop is fastest, running at the switching frequency or a
submultiple, and it regulates torque. The velocity loop is roughly a decade slower and commands
current. The position loop is slower again and commands velocity. Each loop must be substantially
faster than the one enclosing it, or they interact and the system oscillates.

Commutation and control:
  six-step commutation  two phases at a time; coarse position sufficient, torque
                       ripple
  Hall sensors         sixty-degree resolution; enough for six-step
  sinusoidal commutation  smooth torque; needs continuous position
  field oriented control  transform to a rotating frame; independent torque and
                       flux control
  Clarke and Park transforms  three phase to two axis, then to the rotor frame
  direct and quadrature currents  flux-producing and torque-producing
  field weakening      negative direct-axis current reduces the effective flux,
                       permitting speed above the back-EMF limit at reduced torque
  cascaded loops       current inside velocity inside position
  bandwidth separation  each loop roughly a decade faster than the one enclosing
                       it
  feedforward          command the expected current from the desired acceleration;
                       the feedback then corrects only the error, which
                       substantially improves tracking
  anti-windup          on every integrator that can saturate
  torque ripple        from commutation, cogging, and sensor error; audible and
                       visible at low speed
  cogging torque       from magnetic attraction between magnets and stator teeth;
                       present with no current, and reduced by skewing

Feedforward deserves emphasis because it is the single most effective addition to a motion
control loop and is frequently omitted. A feedback-only loop must first observe an error before
correcting it, so it necessarily lags the command. Computing the current required to produce the
commanded acceleration from the known inertia, and adding it directly, means the feedback loop
handles only the discrepancy between the model and reality. Tracking error typically falls by an
order of magnitude, and the addition requires only an inertia estimate.

## Sensing, Motion Profiles, and Transmission

Position sensing determines what control is possible. Hall sensors give sector information
adequate for six-step commutation and inadequate for smooth control. Incremental encoders give
high-resolution relative position and require a homing operation at startup to establish the
absolute reference. Absolute encoders give position immediately at power-on. Resolvers are rugged
analogue devices suited to harsh environments. Sensorless schemes estimate position from the back
EMF or from the winding inductance variation, which works well at speed and poorly or not at all
near zero, where the back EMF vanishes.

Motion profiles determine how a move is executed, and the choice affects the mechanical stress and
the settling behaviour. A trapezoidal velocity profile accelerates at a constant rate, cruises, and
decelerates, which is simple and produces a step change in acceleration at each transition —
infinite jerk, which excites structural resonances and produces audible knocking and vibration. An
S-curve profile limits the rate of change of acceleration, which lengthens the move slightly and
substantially reduces the excitation.

Mechanical transmission couples the motor to the load and its properties dominate the achievable
performance more than the motor's do. A gearbox multiplies torque by the ratio and divides speed,
and it multiplies the reflected inertia by the square of the ratio — which is usually beneficial,
since it makes the motor's own inertia dominant and the load easy to control, and it is why high
reduction systems are not backdrivable.

  Hall sensors         sector information; six-step only
  incremental encoder  high resolution, relative; requires homing
  absolute encoder     position at power-on; single or multi-turn
  resolver             rugged analogue; harsh environments
  sensorless           back EMF or inductance based; fails near zero speed
  quadrature           two channels ninety degrees apart; direction and four times
                       the resolution
  trapezoidal profile  constant acceleration; infinite jerk at the transitions
  S-curve profile      limited jerk; less excitation of resonances
  jerk                 the rate of change of acceleration; what excites structural
                       modes
  gear reduction       multiplies torque, divides speed, multiplies reflected
                       inertia by the ratio squared
  inertia matching     the classical rule of a load-to-motor inertia ratio near
                       one; a guideline rather than an optimum, and ratios far
                       from it work with appropriate control
  backlash             lost motion on reversal; the dominant limit on positioning
                       accuracy in geared systems, and it destabilizes a

Transmission compliance deserves emphasis because it sets a hard ceiling on control bandwidth.
Motor and load inertias connected by a flexible element form a two-mass resonant system, and a
control loop with bandwidth approaching that resonance excites it and becomes unstable. The
practical limit is roughly a third to a half of the resonant frequency, so a compliant belt drive
with a resonance at eighty hertz limits the loop to perhaps thirty, regardless of how good the
motor and drive are. Improving the response therefore requires stiffening the transmission rather
than tuning the controller, which is a mechanical fix to what presents as a control problem.

[assistant] You are exciting the mechanical resonance between the motor and the load through the
belt's compliance, and no amount of controller tuning removes it.

Your motor and load inertias, coupled through a compliant belt, form a two-mass system resonating
at about 45 Hz — exactly where you see the oscillation. A position loop with bandwidth approaching
that frequency feeds energy into the mode, and the standard limit is a third to a half of the
resonance, so 15 to 22 Hz is what this mechanical arrangement supports.

Your inertia ratio of 13 to 1 makes it worse, because the resonance is more pronounced when the
load inertia dominates. The motor barely feels the load through the compliance at that frequency,
so the two masses move independently.

Four options, in order of how much they buy.

Stiffen the transmission. This raises the resonant frequency directly and is the only change that
raises the ceiling rather than working beneath it. A wider or shorter belt, a stiffer belt
material, a higher preload, or replacing the belt with a direct coupling or a stiffer gearbox all
help. Doubling the stiffness raises the resonance by about forty percent.

Add a notch filter at 45 Hz in the velocity loop. This attenuates the controller's output at the
resonant frequency so it stops exciting the mode, and it typically permits a fifty to hundred
percent bandwidth increase. It is the standard remedy and it is a workaround rather than a fix —
the resonance is still there and a shift in its frequency, from load change or belt ageing, moves
it out of the notch.

Reduce the inertia ratio, by a different gear ratio or a larger motor. This reduces the
resonance's severity though not its frequency.

Add load-side feedback. You are measuring at the motor, so the loop does not see what the load is
actually doing and the compliance is entirely outside the loop. A second encoder on the load,
used in a dual-loop arrangement, closes the position loop around the actual output. This is the
most capable arrangement and it is more complex to tune, since the compliance is now inside the
loop.

One check first: verify the 45 Hz is mechanical rather than electrical by disconnecting the load
and repeating. If the oscillation disappears, it is the resonance as diagnosed. If it persists, the
velocity loop tuning or a sensor issue is involved and the fix is different.

## Sizing, Thermal, and Diagnostics

Sizing a motor requires the load's torque and speed requirements across the whole duty cycle
rather than at a single operating point. The peak torque requirement, typically during
acceleration, must be within the motor's peak capability. The root mean square torque over the
cycle must be within the continuous rating, since that is what determines the heating. And the
maximum speed must be achievable at the available supply voltage against the back EMF.

The distinction between peak and continuous rating is thermal. A motor's peak torque is limited by
magnetic saturation and by the drive's current capability, and it can be delivered briefly. The
continuous rating is limited by heat — the losses at that current, dissipated through the motor's
thermal path, must reach a steady temperature within the winding insulation's limit. A motor can
therefore deliver several times its continuous torque for seconds and would overheat within
minutes.

Diagnostics benefit from the motor being an accessible sensor of its own condition. Current
consumption reflects the mechanical load directly, so a rising trend at constant commanded motion
indicates increasing friction — a failing bearing, a contaminated guideway, a misalignment.
Following error reflects whether the axis is achieving its command. And the current signature's
frequency content reveals mechanical faults, since a defect in a bearing or a gear produces a
periodic load variation at a frequency determined by the geometry.

Safe torque off deserves the closing emphasis because the distinction it embodies is a safety
requirement rather than a design preference. Commanding a motor to zero torque relies on the
software, the communication, and the drive all functioning, and none of those can be relied upon in
a safety argument. Safe torque off is a hardware path that removes the gate drive supply
independently of the control system, so no software fault, no communication failure, and no
processor lockup can produce motion. Any machine where unexpected motion could injure someone
requires this rather than a software interlock, and it is the reason the function exists as a
certified capability in industrial drives rather than as something implemented in firmware.
