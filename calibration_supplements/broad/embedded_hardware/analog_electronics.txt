## Passive Components and Their Non-Idealities

Every real component is a network rather than an ideal element, and analog design is
largely the discipline of knowing which parasitic matters where. A resistor has series
inductance from its leads and body and parallel capacitance across it, so its impedance
rises then falls with frequency rather than staying flat. A capacitor has equivalent
series resistance and equivalent series inductance, so above its self-resonant frequency
it behaves as an inductor. An inductor has winding resistance, inter-winding capacitance,
and a core that saturates.

Capacitor dielectric choice matters far more than the capacitance value suggests. Class 1
ceramics such as C0G/NP0 are stable with temperature and voltage, have very low loss, and
are available only at small values — these are the right choice for filters and timing.
Class 2 ceramics such as X7R and Y5V achieve much higher capacitance in the same package
and lose a large fraction of it under DC bias: an X7R rated 10 microfarads at 16 volts may
deliver under 3 microfarads at 12 volts applied, and Y5V is worse still. This DC bias
derating is not on the front page of most datasheets and is a recurring cause of
supply-decoupling designs that measure far worse than they simulate.

Electrolytic capacitors provide bulk capacitance cheaply, with high ESR, limited
lifetime that halves for every ten degrees of temperature rise, and polarity that must be
respected. Film capacitors have excellent stability and low loss at larger sizes.
Tantalum offers high density with a failure mode that is a short circuit, sometimes
energetic, which is why they are derated heavily in voltage. Choosing a capacitor by
capacitance and voltage alone, ignoring dielectric and ESR, is the most common passive
component error.

Component non-idealities:
  resistor       series L, parallel C; tolerance, tempco, voltage coefficient,
                 thermal and excess noise, self-heating shifts value
  capacitor      ESR, ESL, DC bias derating (class 2 ceramics), dielectric
                 absorption, aging, temperature coefficient
  inductor       DCR, self-resonant frequency, core saturation, EMI radiation
  self-resonance every capacitor is inductive above it; parallel different values
                 to extend the low-impedance band, watching for anti-resonance
  ceramic piezo  class 2 ceramics are microphonic and generate voltage under
                 mechanical stress; a real problem in low-noise circuits
  tolerance stack combine tolerances in RSS for typical, worst-case for guaranteed

Decoupling capacitor placement is governed entirely by inductance. The impedance the chip
sees is dominated not by the capacitor's value but by the loop inductance of the path
through the capacitor, so a 100 nF part placed two centimetres away with a long via stub
is far worse than a 10 nF part placed adjacent with short vias to solid planes. The
practical rules follow: place the smallest, closest capacitor nearest the pin, minimize
the loop area formed by the pin, capacitor, and return path, and use multiple vias to
reduce via inductance. Values matter less than layout, which is a difficult idea for
people accustomed to schematic-level thinking.

Q: A 10 uF X7R ceramic rated for 16 V is used on a 12 V rail and the supply still shows
excessive ripple. The most likely cause is:
A. The capacitor is defective
B. DC bias derating has reduced the effective capacitance by more than half
C. The voltage rating is insufficient
D. The ripple frequency is too low
Reasoning: Class 2 ceramic dielectrics lose capacitance substantially under applied DC
bias, often 60-80% at three-quarters of rated voltage. The part is functioning as
specified; the specification simply does not describe the operating condition. Choosing a
higher voltage rating in a larger package, or a different dielectric, restores the
capacitance. Answer: B.

## Diodes, Transistors, and Discrete Design

A diode conducts in one direction, with an exponential current-voltage relation described
by the Shockley equation. The "0.7 volt drop" is a linearization convenient for hand
analysis and misleading when precision matters: the forward voltage depends
logarithmically on current and falls about 2 millivolts per degree Celsius of temperature
rise. That temperature coefficient is exploited deliberately in bandgap references and
causes trouble in bias circuits where it is ignored.

Diode variants serve distinct purposes. Schottky diodes have lower forward drop, around
0.3 volts, and much faster recovery, making them the choice for rectification in
switching supplies and for clamping — at the cost of higher reverse leakage. Zener diodes
conduct in reverse above a defined breakdown voltage and provide simple voltage
references, with a temperature coefficient that changes sign around 5.6 volts, which is
why references near that value are the most stable. Light-emitting diodes require a
current limit because their forward voltage is nearly fixed, so a small voltage change
produces a large current change, and driving them from a voltage source without a
resistor or current regulator destroys them.

Bipolar junction transistors are current-controlled: the collector current is
approximately beta times the base current, with beta varying widely between devices, with
temperature, and with current. Robust designs therefore avoid depending on beta,
typically by using emitter degeneration where a resistor in the emitter sets the current
by voltage division rather than by current gain. The base-emitter voltage has the same
-2 mV per degree coefficient as a diode, which is what causes thermal runaway in power
stages: rising temperature increases current at fixed drive, which raises dissipation,
which raises temperature further. Emitter resistors provide the negative feedback that
prevents this.

Field-effect transistors are voltage-controlled, with gate current essentially zero at DC.
MOSFETs dominate switching applications because they turn fully on with a low
drain-source resistance and require no continuous drive current. The gate is capacitive,
so switching speed is set by how fast the gate charge can be delivered — which is why gate
drivers exist and why a MOSFET driven directly from a microcontroller pin switches slowly
and dissipates heavily during the transition.

Discrete device essentials:
  diode equation       I = I_s (exp(V/(n V_T)) - 1);  V_T ≈ 25.85 mV at 300 K
  forward drop tempco  about -2 mV/K
  BJT regions          cutoff, active (amplification), saturation (switch on)
  BJT current          I_C ≈ beta I_B;  never design assuming a specific beta
  transconductance     g_m = I_C / V_T for a BJT; sets small-signal gain
  MOSFET threshold     V_GS must exceed V_th; check the datasheet at your drive
                       voltage, not the typical value
  MOSFET on-resistance R_DS(on) rises substantially with temperature — roughly
                       doubling from 25 C to 125 C for silicon
  gate charge          Q_g determines drive current needed for a given switching
                       speed; the real figure of merit, not gate capacitance
  body diode           intrinsic in a MOSFET; conducts in reverse, matters in
                       bridge circuits and synchronous rectification
  safe operating area  the combined voltage, current, and time limits; exceeding
                       it destroys the device even within individual ratings

Level shifting and switching topology deserve attention because they are where discrete
design most often goes wrong. A low-side N-channel MOSFET switching a load to ground is
simple, since the source is at ground and the gate drive is referenced to it. A high-side
switch requires either a P-channel device, whose gate must be pulled below the supply, or
an N-channel device with a gate driven above the supply rail — which requires a bootstrap
capacitor or an isolated supply. Attempting a high-side N-channel switch driven from a
logic-level signal referenced to ground produces a device that never fully turns on and
dissipates enormously.

## Operational Amplifiers

An operational amplifier is a differential amplifier with very high open-loop gain,
intended to be used with negative feedback. The two golden rules for analysis under
negative feedback are that no current flows into the inputs and that the amplifier drives
its output so as to make the input voltages equal. These are approximations that hold
extremely well within the amplifier's bandwidth and current limits, and nearly all
op-amp circuit analysis reduces to applying them.

The canonical configurations follow directly. An inverting amplifier has gain -R_f/R_in
with a virtual ground at the inverting input and an input impedance equal to R_in. A
non-inverting amplifier has gain 1 + R_f/R_g and very high input impedance. A voltage
follower is the unity-gain case, used as a buffer to drive a low impedance from a high one.
A difference amplifier subtracts two inputs, with common-mode rejection limited by
resistor matching — which is why an instrumentation amplifier, with matched resistors on
one die, achieves far better rejection than a discrete implementation can.

Real op-amps depart from ideal in ways that matter differently by application. Input
offset voltage produces an output error multiplied by the noise gain, and its drift with
temperature is usually more troublesome than its initial value, since the initial value
can be trimmed. Input bias current flows through source impedances and produces an error
voltage, which is why bipolar-input amplifiers need matched impedances at both inputs and
why FET-input amplifiers are preferred for high-impedance sources. Finite gain-bandwidth
product means closed-loop bandwidth equals gain-bandwidth divided by the closed-loop
gain, so a 1 MHz part configured for a gain of 100 has only 10 kHz of bandwidth. Slew rate
limits how fast the output can change regardless of bandwidth, and exceeding it produces
distortion that a small-signal bandwidth specification does not predict.

Op-amp parameters and what each governs:
  input offset voltage    DC accuracy;  multiplied by noise gain at the output
  offset drift            accuracy over temperature; usually the binding limit
  input bias current      error through source impedance; FET input for high-Z
  input offset current    the mismatch; what remains after impedance balancing
  gain-bandwidth product  closed-loop BW = GBW / closed-loop gain
  slew rate               large-signal speed;  full-power bandwidth = SR/(2 pi V_p)
  CMRR                    rejection of common-mode input; falls with frequency
  PSRR                    rejection of supply variation; also falls with frequency
  output swing            rail-to-rail or not; check the loaded specification
  input common-mode range not all parts include the rails; violating it can cause
                          phase inversion in older designs
  noise                   voltage noise density and current noise density; which
                          dominates depends on source impedance

Stability is the failure mode that surprises people because the schematic looks correct.
Negative feedback becomes positive if the loop accumulates 180 degrees of additional phase
shift while the loop gain still exceeds unity, and the circuit oscillates. The most common
cause in practice is capacitive loading on the output: the amplifier's output impedance
plus the load capacitance forms a pole inside the feedback loop. The standard remedies are
a small series resistor isolating the capacitive load, a feedback capacitor providing
phase lead, or selecting an amplifier specified as stable into the load. Long feedback
traces and stray capacitance at the inverting input cause the same problem, which is why
that node should be kept physically small.

[assistant] Classic transimpedance instability. Your phase margin is 12 degrees, which is
why it rings rather than settles.

The mechanism: the photodiode's 120 pF plus the amplifier's 8 pF of input capacitance
forms a zero in the noise gain with your 1 megohm feedback resistor, at about 1.2 kHz.
Above that frequency the noise gain rises at 20 dB per decade while the amplifier's open
loop gain falls at 20 dB per decade, so they close at 40 dB per decade — which
corresponds to two poles and hence 180 degrees of phase shift inside the loop. That is an
oscillator.

The fix is a feedback capacitor across the feedback resistor, which flattens the noise
gain above its corner and restores single-pole closure. The value for 45 degrees of phase
margin is about 0.57 pF, giving you 279 kHz of bandwidth.

That number is the practical problem: 0.57 pF is below the stray capacitance of a typical
feedback resistor footprint, which is often 0.1 to 0.3 pF and poorly controlled. Three
responses, in order of preference.

Reduce the source capacitance. This is the highest-leverage change. Reverse-bias the
photodiode — even a few volts typically cuts its capacitance by half or more — or select a
smaller-area diode if your optical design permits. Halving the capacitance directly
improves both stability and noise.

Use a faster amplifier. The required feedback capacitor scales inversely with the square
root of gain-bandwidth, so moving from 10 MHz to 100 MHz lets you use a larger, more
controllable capacitor while getting more bandwidth. Make sure the part is
low-input-capacitance and low-bias-current, since a fast general-purpose amplifier may
lose you more in offset than it gains in speed.

Split the gain. Use a lower feedback resistor — say 100 kilohms — followed by a voltage
gain stage of ten. The lower resistor moves the noise gain zero up by a decade and makes
the required feedback capacitor about ten times larger and easily realizable. The cost is
slightly worse noise, since the feedback resistor's thermal noise is referred to the input
with less gain ahead of it.

Whatever you choose, guard the inverting input node: keep it physically tiny, surround it
with a guard ring driven to the same potential, and do not run it over a plane. Stray
capacitance there is directly in the destabilizing path.

## Filters, Power, and Noise

Active filters use op-amps to realize responses that passive networks cannot achieve
without inductors. The Sallen-Key topology implements a second-order section with one
amplifier and is the common choice for low-pass and high-pass; the multiple-feedback
topology inverts and offers better high-frequency behaviour. Higher orders are built by
cascading second-order sections, and the section parameters are taken from tables for the
chosen response.

Response family selection is a real design decision rather than a default. Butterworth
gives maximally flat passband with moderate rolloff. Chebyshev accepts passband ripple in
exchange for a sharper transition. Elliptic accepts ripple in both bands for the sharpest
transition of all. Bessel sacrifices rolloff sharpness for maximally flat group delay,
preserving pulse shape — which makes it the correct choice whenever the time-domain
waveform matters, as in data links and instrumentation, and the wrong choice when
stopband rejection is what matters.

Power supply design divides into linear and switching approaches with a clear tradeoff.
A linear regulator drops the difference between input and output across a pass element,
dissipating the difference times the current as heat, so efficiency is roughly the ratio
of output to input voltage. It is quiet, simple, and fast-responding, which is why analog
and RF sections are often fed from a linear regulator downstream of a switcher. A
switching regulator stores energy in an inductor and transfers it in packets, achieving
efficiencies above ninety percent independent of the voltage ratio, at the cost of
switching noise, layout sensitivity, and design complexity.

Power and noise reference:
  linear regulator eff.   ~ V_out / V_in;  dissipation = (V_in - V_out) * I
  LDO dropout             the minimum headroom; check at your load current and
                          temperature, not typical
  buck / boost / buck-boost   step down / up / either
  switcher layout         the high di/dt loop — input capacitor, switch, diode or
                          synchronous FET — must be physically tiny; this loop is
                          the dominant EMI source and the most common design error
  thermal noise           v = sqrt(4 k T R B);  ~4 nV/sqrt(Hz) for 1 kohm at 300 K
  shot noise              i = sqrt(2 q I B);  in currents crossing a junction
  flicker (1/f) noise     dominant at low frequency; corner frequency varies by
                          device technology, low for JFET, higher for CMOS
  noise figure            degradation of SNR through a stage; the first stage
                          dominates the whole chain (Friis)
  noise bandwidth         for a single pole, 1.57 times the -3 dB bandwidth

The Friis noise formula has a consequence that governs all low-noise design: the noise of
the first amplifying stage dominates the entire chain, because subsequent noise is
referred to the input divided by the preceding gain. Spend the effort and the component
budget on the first stage; a mediocre second stage after a good first one costs almost
nothing. Correspondingly, any loss before the first amplifier — a lossy cable, a
resistive attenuator, a poorly matched connection — degrades the noise figure directly and
cannot be recovered afterward.

Analog PCB layout is where careful designs are commonly ruined. Ground is not a
node but a distributed conductor with impedance, and return currents flow in the path of
least impedance — which at high frequency means directly beneath the outgoing trace, not
by the shortest resistive route. A split in the ground plane under a signal trace forces
the return current to detour, creating a large loop that both radiates and receives.
Keeping a continuous reference plane beneath every signal is more important than any
grounding scheme.

Separating analog and digital returns is standard advice that is frequently applied
incorrectly. The goal is to prevent digital return currents from flowing through the
analog reference, which is achieved by controlling where currents flow — placing the
converter so its analog and digital sections straddle a single continuous plane, keeping
digital traces off the analog region — rather than by cutting the plane and joining the
halves at one point. A split plane with a trace crossing it is worse than no split at all.
Star grounding at a single point works at low frequency and fails at high frequency,
where the inductance of the star arms dominates.
