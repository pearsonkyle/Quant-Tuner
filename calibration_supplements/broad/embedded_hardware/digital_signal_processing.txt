## Sampling and Quantization

The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem states that a signal band-limited to B hertz is
perfectly reconstructible from samples taken at any rate above 2B. The theorem is exact
and its conditions are the part that matters in practice: the signal must be genuinely
band-limited, which no real signal is, and reconstruction requires an ideal sinc
interpolation of infinite extent, which no real system implements.

Aliasing is what happens when the condition fails. Frequency components above half the
sampling rate fold back into the band as lower frequencies, indistinguishable from
genuine content once sampled. A 3 kHz tone sampled at 5 kHz appears as a 2 kHz tone, and
no amount of subsequent processing can separate them, because the information
distinguishing them was destroyed at the moment of sampling. This is why an analog
anti-aliasing filter before the converter is mandatory rather than optional: it is the
only place the fold-back can be prevented.

Practical anti-alias filtering forces oversampling. A real analog filter has a finite
transition band, so a filter that passes 20 kHz and must reject 20 kHz-plus content
before folding cannot be built with a sharp enough skirt at a 44.1 kHz sample rate
without extreme order and terrible phase behaviour. The standard solution is to sample
well above Nyquist, use a gentle analog filter, and perform the sharp band-limiting
digitally where filters can be made arbitrarily steep with linear phase, then decimate.
This is why audio converters run at megahertz rates internally and output at 48 kHz.

Sampling and quantization reference:
  Nyquist rate            f_s > 2B for a signal band-limited to B
  aliased frequency       |f - round(f/f_s) * f_s|
  quantization SNR        SNR_dB ≈ 6.02 N + 1.76 for N bits, full-scale sine
  effective bits          ENOB = (SNR_dB - 1.76) / 6.02;  what you actually get
  dynamic range           ratio of largest to smallest representable signal
  oversampling gain       2x oversampling then decimation gains 3 dB (0.5 bit)
  dither                  adds ~1 LSB of noise before quantization; decorrelates
                          quantization error from the signal, trading distortion
                          for benign noise
  noise shaping           pushes quantization noise out of the band of interest;
                          the basis of sigma-delta conversion

The 6 dB per bit rule is the most useful number in the subject. Each additional bit
doubles the number of quantization levels, halving the error, which is a 6 dB
improvement in signal-to-noise ratio. A 12-bit converter has a theoretical 74 dB SNR, a
16-bit one 98 dB, a 24-bit one 146 dB — though the last is fictional, since no analog
front end has a noise floor that low, and real 24-bit converters deliver perhaps 20
effective bits. Always ask for ENOB rather than nominal resolution.

Dither is counterintuitive and correct: deliberately adding noise improves the result.
Without it, quantization error is deterministically related to the signal, producing
harmonic distortion — a quiet sine wave quantized coarsely becomes a stepped waveform
with audible harmonics. Adding about one LSB of random noise before quantization
decorrelates the error, converting structured distortion into a flat noise floor that is
far less objectionable and, crucially, allows signals below the quantization step to
remain audible through the noise.

Q: A signal containing energy at 7 kHz is sampled at 10 kHz with no anti-alias filter.
The 7 kHz component appears in the sampled data at:
A. 7 kHz   B. 3 kHz   C. 5 kHz   D. It is removed
Reasoning: Nyquist is 5 kHz. The component folds to |7 - 10| = 3 kHz. It is now
indistinguishable from genuine 3 kHz content and cannot be removed afterward, which is
precisely why the anti-alias filter must precede the sampler. Answer: B.

## The Fourier Transform and Spectral Analysis

The Fourier transform decomposes a signal into sinusoidal components, and the discrete
Fourier transform does so for a finite sampled sequence. The fast Fourier transform is
an algorithm computing the DFT in O(N log N) rather than O(N^2) by recursively
exploiting symmetry — a change that turned spectral analysis from a research computation
into a real-time operation and is one of the most consequential algorithms ever devised.

Interpreting a DFT requires knowing what its bins mean. An N-point DFT of a signal
sampled at f_s produces N bins spaced f_s/N apart, spanning zero to f_s. Bins above
N/2 correspond to negative frequencies and, for real input, are the complex conjugate
mirror of the lower half, carrying no additional information. Frequency resolution is
f_s/N and therefore depends only on the total observation duration, not on the sample
rate — observing for one second gives 1 Hz resolution whether sampled at 8 kHz or 8 MHz.
This is the fundamental time-frequency tradeoff, and it is why distinguishing two closely
spaced tones requires a long observation regardless of how fast you sample.

Spectral leakage arises because the DFT implicitly assumes the finite record repeats
periodically. If the signal does not complete an integer number of cycles within the
window, the assumed periodic extension has a discontinuity at the boundary, and that
discontinuity spreads energy across all bins. A pure sine wave at a bin-centre frequency
produces one nonzero bin; the same sine offset by half a bin spreads across the whole
spectrum with a slowly decaying skirt that can bury weaker nearby components entirely.

Windowing controls leakage by tapering the record to zero at both ends, removing the
discontinuity at the cost of widening the main lobe. Every window trades main-lobe width
against sidelobe level, and the choice depends on what the analysis must distinguish.

Window functions:
  rectangular (none)   narrowest main lobe (2 bins), worst sidelobes (-13 dB);
                       correct only when the signal is exactly periodic in the window
  Hann                 4-bin main lobe, -31 dB sidelobes, fast -18 dB/octave rolloff;
                       the sensible general-purpose default
  Hamming              4-bin, -43 dB first sidelobe, slower rolloff
  Blackman             6-bin, -58 dB; use when a weak tone sits near a strong one
  Blackman-Harris      8-bin, -92 dB; extreme dynamic range applications
  flat-top             very wide main lobe, near-exact amplitude accuracy; use for
                       calibration where amplitude matters more than resolution
  Kaiser               parameterized family trading the two continuously

Coherent gain and noise bandwidth corrections matter when the DFT is used for
measurement rather than display. A window attenuates the signal, so amplitude readings
must be scaled by the reciprocal of its coherent gain, and noise power estimates must
account for the window's equivalent noise bandwidth, which exceeds one bin. Omitting
these gives readings that are systematically low by several decibels, a common error in
homemade spectrum analyzers.

The spectrogram extends spectral analysis to non-stationary signals by computing DFTs
over successive overlapping windows. Its resolution is governed by the same uncertainty
tradeoff: a short window localizes events in time and blurs them in frequency, a long
window does the reverse, and no choice does both. Overlapping windows by 50% or 75%
recovers information lost to the window taper and is standard. Wavelet transforms address
the same problem with a basis whose time-frequency tiling varies with scale, giving fine
time resolution at high frequency and fine frequency resolution at low — a better match
to how most natural signals are structured.

## Digital Filters

A filter modifies a signal's frequency content. Digital filters divide into two families
with entirely different characteristics, and the choice between them is the first design
decision.

Finite impulse response filters compute each output as a weighted sum of a finite number
of past inputs. They are unconditionally stable, since there is no feedback, and they can
be made exactly linear phase by choosing symmetric coefficients — meaning all frequencies
are delayed equally so the waveform shape is preserved, which matters enormously for
data communications, for medical waveforms, and for any application where the shape
rather than the spectrum carries the information. The cost is order: achieving a sharp
transition requires many taps, and the computational load and latency scale with that
count.

Infinite impulse response filters include feedback, so the impulse response never fully
decays. They achieve a given frequency response with far fewer coefficients — often an
order of magnitude fewer than an equivalent FIR — which makes them attractive on
constrained hardware. The costs are that they can be unstable if poles move outside the
unit circle, that they have nonlinear phase which distorts waveform shape, and that they
are sensitive to coefficient quantization, especially at high order where a small
coefficient error can move a pole across the stability boundary.

Filter design and structure:
  FIR design methods    windowed sinc (simple, suboptimal), Parks-McClellan
                        (equiripple, optimal for a given order), least squares,
                        frequency sampling
  IIR design methods    analog prototype plus bilinear transform; Butterworth
                        (maximally flat), Chebyshev I (passband ripple, sharper),
                        Chebyshev II (stopband ripple), elliptic (both, sharpest),
                        Bessel (maximally flat group delay)
  FIR order estimate    N ≈ (attenuation_dB - 8) / (2.285 * transition_width_rad)
  IIR implementation    cascade of second-order sections, never direct-form high
                        order — coefficient sensitivity makes high-order direct
                        forms unusable in fixed point
  group delay           FIR linear phase gives constant (N-1)/2 samples;
                        IIR varies with frequency
  minimum phase         non-symmetric FIR with lower delay for the same magnitude
                        response, when linear phase is not required

The second-order-section rule is not a stylistic preference. A tenth-order IIR filter
implemented as a single direct-form structure has coefficients whose relationship to the
pole locations is extremely ill-conditioned; quantizing them to 16 bits can move poles
enough to destabilize the filter entirely. Factoring into five cascaded biquads makes
each section's coefficients directly related to a conjugate pole pair, so quantization
error moves each pole slightly rather than catastrophically. Every serious filter library
outputs second-order sections for this reason.

Multirate processing changes the sample rate within a system and is often the largest
available efficiency gain. Decimation reduces the rate, requiring a low-pass filter first
to prevent aliasing; interpolation raises it by inserting zeros and filtering to remove
the resulting spectral images. Polyphase decomposition exploits the fact that in
decimation most filter outputs are discarded and in interpolation most inputs are zero,
restructuring the computation to avoid the wasted multiplies — a saving equal to the rate
change factor. A cascaded integrator-comb filter performs large rate changes with no
multipliers at all, using only adders and delays, which is why it is ubiquitous in the
front end of sigma-delta converters and software radios.

[user] I need a 60 Hz notch on an ECG signal sampled at 500 Hz, but a simple notch is
distorting the QRS complexes.

[assistant] The distortion is phase, not magnitude — your IIR notch has 14 ms of group
delay variation across the QRS band, so different frequency components of the complex
arrive at different times and the shape smears. A high-Q IIR notch also rings for about
62 ms after a sharp transient, which is comparable to the 90 ms QRS duration itself.

Two good options, and I would take the first.

Your diagnostic band is 0.5 to 40 Hz and the interference is at 60 Hz — they do not
overlap. So you do not need a notch at all; you need a low-pass at 40 Hz. A linear-phase
FIR low-pass removes the 60 Hz entirely along with everything else above the diagnostic
band, and being linear phase it delays every component equally, preserving morphology
exactly. At 500 Hz sampling with a 40 Hz passband and 60 Hz stopband, an order of about
100 taps gives you 40 dB of rejection, which is 100 ms of constant delay — fine for
offline analysis, and correctable by compensating the known constant offset for
real-time display.

If you must keep content above 40 Hz, use a linear-phase FIR notch instead of the
biquad. Higher order, but constant group delay so the QRS shape survives.

A third technique worth knowing for mains interference specifically: adaptive
cancellation. Since 60 Hz is a known, nearly stationary frequency, you can estimate its
amplitude and phase over a window and subtract a synthesized sinusoid. This removes the
interference with essentially no effect on the signal band at all, and it tracks slow
drift in the mains frequency. It is more code than a filter but it is the cleanest
result.

One thing to check regardless: if the 60 Hz is large enough to need aggressive removal,
look at the source. Better electrode preparation, shorter leads, driven-right-leg
circuitry, and proper shielding usually reduce mains pickup by 20-40 dB, which is more
than any filter buys you and costs no signal fidelity.

## Fixed-Point Arithmetic

Embedded DSP frequently runs without a floating-point unit, so arithmetic uses integers
with an implied binary point. Q notation specifies the format: Q15 means one sign bit and
15 fractional bits in a 16-bit word, representing values in [-1, 1). Q31 is the 32-bit
equivalent. The representable range and the resolution are traded directly — every bit
moved from the fractional side to the integer side doubles the range and halves the
precision.

Multiplication in Q format requires a shift. Multiplying two Q15 numbers yields a Q30
result in a 32-bit accumulator, which must be shifted left by one and taken from the high
half to return to Q15. Getting this wrong by one bit is a factor-of-two error that is
easy to introduce and easy to miss when the algorithm still roughly works. Many DSP
cores provide a fractional multiply mode performing the shift in hardware, which is worth
using precisely because it removes this error class.

Overflow is the central hazard. Fixed-point addition of two full-scale values overflows,
and the behaviour on overflow determines whether the result is merely wrong or
catastrophic. Wraparound turns a large positive into a large negative, producing a
full-scale discontinuity — in audio, a loud click; in control, a violent transient.
Saturating arithmetic clamps to the maximum instead, which is a graceful degradation and
is why DSP instruction sets provide saturating add and multiply-accumulate instructions.
Use them.

Fixed-point practice:
  Q15 range           [-1, 0.999969];  resolution 2^-15 ≈ 3.05e-5
  Q31 range           [-1, ~1);  resolution 2^-31 ≈ 4.66e-10
  multiply Q15 x Q15  32-bit product in Q30; shift left 1, take high 16 for Q15
  accumulate          use a wide accumulator (40-bit on many DSPs) with guard bits
                      so intermediate sums cannot overflow
  headroom            scale inputs down by the filter's gain so the output cannot
                      exceed full scale; the sum of |coefficients| bounds it
  saturation          prefer saturating instructions over wraparound
  rounding            round-to-nearest rather than truncation; truncation biases
                      results toward zero and that bias accumulates in feedback
  coefficient scaling normalize filter coefficients and track the scale separately

Limit cycles are a fixed-point-specific pathology in IIR filters. Quantization inside the
feedback loop can sustain a small oscillation that never decays, so a filter given zero
input produces a persistent low-level output — audible as a tone in audio applications.
Increasing the internal word length, using round-to-nearest instead of truncation, and
adding a small dither to the feedback path all suppress it. It is a good example of a
problem that does not exist in the floating-point design and appears only in
implementation, which is why fixed-point implementations must be verified against the
reference rather than assumed equivalent.

## Adaptive Filtering and Applications

An adaptive filter adjusts its own coefficients to minimize an error signal, learning the
correct response rather than having it designed in. The least mean squares algorithm is
the workhorse: it updates each coefficient by a step size times the error times the
corresponding input sample, which is stochastic gradient descent on the mean squared
error. Its appeal is extreme simplicity — one multiply-accumulate per coefficient per
sample — and robustness.

Step size governs the fundamental tradeoff. Large steps adapt quickly and settle to a
noisier solution; small steps converge slowly to a more accurate one. Stability requires
the step size to be below a bound inversely proportional to the input power, which is why
normalized LMS divides by the current input energy — it makes the effective step size
independent of signal level, so a filter tuned on quiet input does not diverge when the
input gets loud. Recursive least squares converges far faster by maintaining an estimate
of the input correlation matrix inverse, at O(N^2) cost per sample instead of O(N) and
with numerical stability concerns that require care in fixed point.

The four canonical adaptive configurations differ only in what is connected where, and
recognizing which one a problem is helps enormously. System identification places the
adaptive filter in parallel with an unknown system and drives both with the same input,
so the filter converges to a model of the system. Inverse modelling places it in series
and drives it toward reproducing a delayed input, so it converges to the system's
inverse — this is channel equalization. Noise cancellation subtracts a filtered version
of a reference noise input from a corrupted signal, requiring only that the reference
correlate with the noise and not with the signal. Linear prediction drives the filter to
predict the next sample from previous ones, and the prediction error is the innovation —
the basis of speech coding and of many compression schemes.

Adaptive filter applications:
  echo cancellation      model the acoustic or hybrid echo path and subtract it;
                         the path changes when anyone moves, so adaptation must be
                         continuous
  active noise control   generate an anti-phase signal; limited by the acoustic
                         delay budget, which is why it works well for low-frequency
                         steady noise and poorly for transients
  channel equalization   invert the distortion of a communications channel
  adaptive beamforming   steer a null toward an interferer using a sensor array
  line enhancement       extract a narrowband signal from broadband noise
  biomedical             remove maternal ECG from fetal recordings, remove EOG
                         artifacts from EEG

The practical constraint that governs adaptive systems is the correlation requirement. A
noise canceller needs a reference input containing the noise and none of the desired
signal; if the reference picks up any of the signal, the adaptation cancels part of the
signal too, and the result is a filter that quietly removes what you wanted to keep.
This is the most common failure in deployed adaptive cancellation, and the diagnostic is
to check whether the output degrades when the desired signal is present but the
interference is not.

Embedded implementation brings its own constraints that shape algorithm choice. Memory
bandwidth often dominates over arithmetic, so a filter structure touching fewer
coefficients wins even at equal multiply count. Circular buffers avoid the cost of
shifting a delay line, and DSP cores provide modulo addressing to index them without
branches. Single-instruction multiple-data extensions on ARM Cortex-M4 and above process
several 16-bit samples per instruction, typically giving a two-to-fourfold speedup on
filter inner loops, and the CMSIS-DSP library provides tuned implementations that should
be preferred over hand-written loops. And the deepest optimization is usually algorithmic:
running the filter at a lower sample rate through decimation, or moving a convolution to
the frequency domain via FFT when the filter is long enough that N log N beats N^2 —
which for FIR filters happens somewhere around 64 taps, depending on the platform.
