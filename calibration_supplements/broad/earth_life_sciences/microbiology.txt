## Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure

Microorganisms constitute the overwhelming majority of biological diversity and biomass on Earth,
and the eukaryotes visible to us are a late and taxonomically narrow branch of the tree. The three
domains — bacteria, archaea, and eukarya — were established by comparing ribosomal RNA sequences,
which revealed that archaea, though superficially resembling bacteria, are as distinct from them as
either is from eukaryotes and are in some respects more closely related to us.

Bacterial cell structure is compact and highly organized despite lacking membrane-bound organelles.
The cell wall of peptidoglycan is the defining structure and it is the target of several antibiotic
classes precisely because animals have nothing like it. The Gram stain distinguishes two
architectures: a thick peptidoglycan layer that retains the stain, and a thin layer plus an outer
membrane that does not. That outer membrane is a permeability barrier which makes those organisms
intrinsically resistant to many compounds, and it carries lipopolysaccharide, which triggers a
strong inflammatory response and is responsible for much of the damage in severe infection.

Archaea differ fundamentally in membrane chemistry — ether-linked, branched lipids rather than
ester-linked straight chains — and this is part of why many of them tolerate conditions that
destroy other cells. They dominate in extreme environments and are also abundant in ordinary ones,
including the ocean and the gut, where their role is often overlooked because early methods
recovered them poorly.

Q: A disinfectant that kills bacteria in laboratory suspension fails to clear a persistent
contamination in a water system. The most likely reason is:
A. The bacteria are resistant to the chemical
B. The organisms are in a biofilm, whose polymer matrix limits penetration and whose slow-growing
   interior cells tolerate treatments that kill actively dividing free cells
C. The concentration is too low
D. The test was inaccurate
Reasoning: Biofilm tolerance is typically orders of magnitude greater than that of planktonic cells
of the same strain, through reduced penetration, altered physiology, and slow growth. It is
tolerance rather than genetic resistance, and it is why physical removal usually accompanies
chemical treatment. Answer: B.

## Metabolism, Growth, and Control

Microbial metabolic diversity vastly exceeds that of plants and animals. Energy can be obtained from
light or chemicals, carbon from carbon dioxide or organic compounds, and electrons from an
extraordinary range of donors including hydrogen, sulfur, iron, ammonia, and methane. Almost every
thermodynamically favourable reaction available in the environment is exploited by some organism,
which is why microbial activity drives the global cycling of nitrogen, sulfur, and carbon.

Respiration transfers electrons through a chain to a terminal acceptor, and oxygen is only the most
familiar one. Nitrate, sulfate, ferric iron, and carbon dioxide serve where oxygen is absent, each
yielding less energy in roughly the order that determines which process dominates as an environment
becomes progressively depleted of oxygen. Fermentation dispenses with an external acceptor entirely,
regenerating cofactors internally at much lower energy yield, and it is what makes bread, beer,
cheese, and yoghurt.

Growth in batch culture follows a characteristic curve: a lag phase of adaptation, exponential
growth at a rate set by conditions, a stationary phase when a resource is exhausted or waste
accumulates, and death. Exponential growth is the property that makes microbial problems escalate
suddenly — a population doubling every twenty minutes goes from imperceptible to overwhelming in
hours — and the practical consequence is that the response time available is far shorter than
linear intuition suggests.

## Genetics and Horizontal Gene Transfer

Bacterial genomes are compact, gene-dense, and organized into operons that transcribe functionally
related genes as a unit under one regulatory control. This organization makes regulation efficient
and is one reason bacterial gene expression responds so rapidly to environmental change — a shift
in nutrient availability can alter the transcriptional programme within minutes.

Horizontal gene transfer distinguishes microbial genetics fundamentally from the vertical
inheritance that dominates in animals. Genes move between organisms, including between distantly
related ones, through three mechanisms: transformation, the uptake of free DNA from the environment;
conjugation, direct transfer through a pilus, typically of a plasmid; and transduction, transfer by
a bacteriophage. The consequence is that a bacterial genome is a mosaic of ancestries and that a
strictly branching phylogenetic tree is the wrong model for this domain.

Antibiotic resistance is the most consequential application of this. Resistance genes carried on
plasmids move between strains and between species, and a single conjugative plasmid can carry
resistance to several drug classes simultaneously. The clinical implication is that exposure to one
antibiotic can select for resistance to others through co-selection on the same mobile element,
which is why resistance emerges and spreads faster than models of vertical inheritance predict.

## Microbiomes and Host Interaction

A microbiome is the community of microorganisms occupying an environment, and the human microbiome
is one of the most studied. The human body carries a microbial population comparable in cell count
to its own, dominated by the colon, and this community performs functions the host cannot: digesting
complex polysaccharides, synthesizing certain vitamins, occupying niches that would otherwise be
available to pathogens, and shaping immune development.

The field's methodological advance was sequencing rather than culture, because most environmental
and host-associated microorganisms do not grow under standard laboratory conditions. Amplicon
sequencing of a conserved marker gene identifies which taxa are present; shotgun metagenomics
sequences everything and identifies functional genes as well. Both describe composition, and the
persistent interpretive problem is that composition is correlational — a difference between healthy
and diseased communities does not establish which direction the causation runs, and disease
frequently alters the environment in ways that change the community.

Colonization resistance is the most practically important microbiome function. An established
community occupies niches and consumes resources, which excludes newcomers, and disrupting it with
broad-spectrum antibiotics permits opportunistic organisms to expand. The clinical demonstration is
that transplanting a whole community from a healthy donor resolves certain recurrent infections that
repeated antibiotics do not, which is strong evidence that the community rather than any single
organism is the functional unit.

## Pathogenesis, Methods, and Applications

Pathogenicity is a relationship rather than a property. Most microorganisms are harmless in their
usual context and some are harmful only in a compromised host or in the wrong body site, which is
why the distinction between pathogen and commensal is less sharp than the terms suggest. Virulence
factors — adhesins, toxins, invasion mechanisms, immune evasion strategies — determine what an
organism can do to a host, and their expression is typically regulated so they appear only in the
host environment.

Establishing that a specific organism causes a specific disease is harder than it appears, and the
classical criteria — presence in every case, isolation in pure culture, disease reproduced on
inoculation, re-isolation — fail for organisms that cannot be cultured, for asymptomatic carriage,
and for diseases with multiple contributing causes. Molecular versions of the criteria substitute
gene-level evidence, and the general lesson is that causal inference in infectious disease requires
the same care it requires anywhere else.

The applications of microbiology extend well beyond medicine. Industrial fermentation produces
antibiotics, enzymes, amino acids, solvents, and increasingly complex molecules through engineered
pathways. Bioremediation uses microbial metabolism to degrade contaminants. Wastewater treatment is
fundamentally a managed microbial process. Agriculture depends on nitrogen-fixing symbioses. Food
production across essentially every culture relies on controlled fermentation, and the same
organisms that spoil food are the ones that preserve it under different conditions.
