## Plant Structure and Organization

Plants are sessile autotrophs, and nearly every structural feature follows from those two facts. An
organism that cannot move must tolerate whatever conditions arrive, which favours plasticity in
development over the fixed body plan animals have. An organism producing its own food from light
must maximize light interception and gas exchange while limiting water loss, and those two goals
conflict directly.

The plant body has three organ systems. Roots anchor, absorb water and minerals, and store
reserves. Stems support, transport, and position leaves. Leaves are the photosynthetic organs,
built as flat surfaces to intercept light with an internal air space maximizing the surface area
available for gas exchange. The tissue systems cross these organs: dermal tissue covering,
vascular tissue transporting, and ground tissue filling and performing metabolism.

Growth in plants is indeterminate and localized to meristems — regions of dividing cells retained
throughout life. Apical meristems extend roots and shoots; lateral meristems add girth. This means a
plant continues producing new organs indefinitely, that its final form depends on conditions
throughout its life rather than being genetically fixed, and that damage to any part can be
compensated by new growth elsewhere. It is a fundamentally different developmental strategy from the
animal one and it is what makes plants so tolerant of damage.

  dermal tissue        the covering; cuticle limiting water loss

Q: A plant grown in shade develops longer stems and thinner leaves than a genetically identical
plant in full sun. This is:
A. A genetic difference
B. Developmental plasticity — plants adjust their form to conditions throughout life because they
   cannot relocate, and the shade response specifically trades support and leaf thickness for
   height to reach light
C. A disease
D. A measurement artifact
Reasoning: The shade avoidance response is a well-characterized developmental program triggered by
the light quality signal of neighbouring vegetation. Identical genotypes produce different forms in
different environments, which is the normal condition for plants rather than an exception.
Answer: B.

## Photosynthesis and Metabolism

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in two connected stages. The light
reactions in the thylakoid membranes use absorbed photons to split water, releasing oxygen, and to
generate the energy carriers that power the second stage. The carbon reactions in the stroma use
those carriers to fix carbon dioxide into sugar through a cycle whose central enzyme is the most
abundant protein on Earth.

That enzyme has a consequential flaw: it also reacts with oxygen instead of carbon dioxide,
initiating a pathway that consumes energy and releases previously fixed carbon. This
photorespiration wastes a substantial fraction of photosynthetic output, and it worsens at high
temperature and when stomata close to conserve water — precisely the conditions under which a plant
can least afford it. It is the sort of frozen accident that evolution cannot easily repair because
the enzyme is too central to modify.

Two carbon concentrating mechanisms evolved independently many times to work around it. The
four-carbon pathway separates initial carbon capture from the main cycle spatially, concentrating
carbon dioxide where the central enzyme operates and suppressing the oxygen reaction. Crassulacean
acid metabolism separates them temporally, opening stomata at night to capture carbon and closing
them during the day. The first suits hot bright environments and the second suits arid ones, and
both cost energy relative to the basic pathway, which is why the basic pathway persists in cool moist
conditions where it is more efficient.

## Water Transport and Stomatal Control

Water moves from soil through the plant to the atmosphere along a gradient of decreasing water
potential, and the mechanism is remarkable for requiring no energy input from the plant. Evaporation
from leaf surfaces creates tension; water's cohesion transmits that tension down continuous columns
in the xylem; and the columns are pulled up. The plant supplies the plumbing and the sun supplies
the energy.

The columns are under negative pressure, which is a physically precarious state — the water is
metastable and can cavitate, forming a bubble that breaks the column and blocks that conduit
permanently or until it can be refilled. Drought and freezing both promote cavitation, and a plant's
drought tolerance is substantially a matter of its xylem's resistance to it. There is a direct
tradeoff: wide conduits transport efficiently and cavitate readily, narrow ones the reverse, which
is why fast-growing species in wet environments and slow-growing species in dry ones differ
structurally in exactly this way.

Stomata are the control point for the central conflict. They open to admit carbon dioxide and lose
water while open, and the plant continuously trades photosynthetic gain against water cost. Guard
cells respond to light, internal carbon dioxide concentration, humidity, and a hormonal drought
signal, integrating these into an aperture. The efficiency of that tradeoff — carbon fixed per water
lost — is water use efficiency, and it is a primary breeding target and a primary axis of adaptation.

## Growth, Hormones, and Reproduction

Plant hormones coordinate development, and unlike animal hormones they are simple molecules acting
at multiple sites with effects depending on concentration, tissue, and the presence of other
hormones. The same compound promotes growth at one concentration and inhibits it at another, and in
one tissue and not in a neighbouring one, which is why plant hormone biology resists the clean
signal-and-receptor descriptions that animal endocrinology often permits.

Auxin is the central one. Produced at shoot tips and transported downward by a directional cellular
transport system, it establishes polarity, drives cell elongation, maintains apical dominance by
suppressing lateral buds, and directs vascular development. Removing the shoot tip releases the
lateral buds, which is why pruning produces bushier growth, and the directional transport is what
allows a plant to establish and re-establish an axis after damage.

Reproduction in flowering plants involves an alternation of generations that is compressed and
concealed relative to that of mosses and ferns. Flowers are the reproductive structures, and their
form is largely explained by pollination strategy: wind-pollinated flowers are inconspicuous and
produce enormous quantities of pollen, while animal-pollinated ones invest in attraction and reward
and produce far less. Double fertilization, unique to flowering plants, produces both an embryo and
a nutritive tissue, which is what makes cereal grains the calorie base of human diets.

## Plant-Environment Interaction and Crops

Plants interact constantly with other organisms and their responses are more sophisticated than
their immobility suggests. They detect herbivore damage and mount induced defences — synthesizing
toxins, strengthening tissues, and emitting volatile compounds that attract the herbivore's
predators. They distinguish mechanical damage from herbivory by chemical cues in insect saliva.
Neighbouring plants detect those volatiles and pre-emptively increase their own defences.

Symbioses are pervasive and consequential. Most land plants form associations with fungi that extend
their effective root system enormously in exchange for sugars, and this partnership is old enough
that it plausibly enabled the colonization of land. Legumes host nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root
nodules, obtaining nitrogen directly from the atmosphere at a substantial energy cost, which is why
they enrich soils and why they anchor crop rotations.

Crop improvement has proceeded through domestication, selective breeding, hybridization, and
increasingly through molecular methods. Domestication produced a consistent syndrome across
independently domesticated species — loss of seed shattering, reduced dormancy, larger seeds, less
branching, reduced defensive compounds — because the same selective pressures applied. Modern
breeding adds marker-assisted selection, which identifies desirable genotypes without waiting for the
phenotype, and gene editing, which introduces specific changes directly. The persistent constraint is
that yield is a complex trait influenced by many loci and by the environment, so gains are
incremental and depend on matching varieties to conditions.
