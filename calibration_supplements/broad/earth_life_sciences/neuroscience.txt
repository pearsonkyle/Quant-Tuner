## Neurons and Electrical Signalling

The neuron is the signalling unit of the nervous system, and its structure reflects its
function: dendrites receive inputs, the soma integrates them, the axon conducts the output, and
terminals transmit to targets. The human brain contains on the order of eighty-six billion
neurons and a comparable number of glial cells, with each neuron making thousands of synaptic
connections, giving a connection count in the hundreds of trillions.

The resting membrane potential, around minus seventy millivolts inside relative to outside, is
established by two mechanisms working together. The sodium-potassium pump moves three sodium
ions out for every two potassium ions in, consuming ATP and establishing concentration
gradients. The membrane is then far more permeable to potassium than to sodium at rest, so
potassium's tendency to flow down its concentration gradient dominates and pulls the potential
toward potassium's equilibrium value. The Nernst equation gives that equilibrium for a single
ion, and the Goldman equation combines several weighted by their permeabilities.

The action potential is a regenerative all-or-none event. Depolarization past a threshold
opens voltage-gated sodium channels, sodium enters and depolarizes further, opening more
channels — a positive feedback that drives the potential rapidly toward sodium's equilibrium.
Sodium channels then inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open, repolarizing the
membrane and often overshooting into a brief hyperpolarization. The refractory period during
which sodium channels remain inactivated is what makes propagation unidirectional, since the
region just traversed cannot fire again immediately.

Electrical signalling:
  resting potential      ~-70 mV; established by the pump plus selective
                         permeability
  Nernst equation        equilibrium potential for one ion from its concentration
                         ratio
  Goldman equation       combines ions weighted by permeability
  threshold              ~-55 mV; where the sodium feedback becomes regenerative
  action potential       all-or-none; amplitude carries no information, frequency
                         does
  depolarization         sodium influx
  repolarization         sodium inactivation plus potassium efflux
  refractory period      absolute then relative; enforces one-way propagation and
                         caps firing rate
  saltatory conduction   in myelinated axons, the signal jumps between nodes of
                         Ranvier; far faster and more energy-efficient
  conduction velocity    ~0.5-2 m/s unmyelinated, up to ~120 m/s myelinated
  demyelination          slows or blocks conduction; the mechanism of multiple
                         sclerosis
  graded potentials      dendritic and synaptic potentials; decay with distance
                         and sum, unlike action potentials

The all-or-none property has an important consequence for how information is encoded. Since
every action potential is essentially identical, the amplitude carries no information, and the
signal must be encoded in timing — firing rate, precise spike times, or the pattern across a
population. Rate coding is the classical picture and is demonstrably used, and precise timing
carries information in auditory localization and elsewhere, and population codes distribute
information across many neurons in a way that is robust to individual variability. Which coding
scheme predominates is area-dependent and remains an active question.

Q: Myelination increases conduction velocity primarily because:
A. It increases the membrane's ion channel density uniformly
B. It insulates the axon so that the signal regenerates only at the nodes, jumping between
   them rather than propagating continuously
C. It raises the resting potential
D. It increases axon diameter
Reasoning: Myelin is an insulating wrapping that reduces membrane capacitance and increases
resistance, so the depolarization spreads passively and rapidly along the internode and
regenerates only at the nodes of Ranvier where channels are concentrated. This saltatory
conduction is both much faster and much more metabolically efficient than continuous
propagation. Answer: B.

## Synapses and Neurotransmission

Chemical synapses transmit signals across a gap by releasing neurotransmitter. An arriving
action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels, calcium influx triggers vesicle fusion,
transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, and the
resulting conductance change produces a postsynaptic potential. Transmitter is then cleared by
reuptake, enzymatic degradation, or diffusion, which terminates the signal and permits the
synapse to respond again.

Receptor type determines the character of the response. Ionotropic receptors are themselves
ion channels, so binding opens them directly, giving fast responses on a millisecond timescale.
Metabotropic receptors are coupled to intracellular signalling cascades, giving slower
responses over hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, with amplification through the cascade and
the capacity to produce lasting changes including altered gene expression. The same transmitter
frequently acts on both types, so its effect depends on the receptor present rather than on the
transmitter alone — which is why a single neurotransmitter can be excitatory at one synapse and
inhibitory at another.

Synaptic integration is where computation happens. A neuron receives thousands of inputs, each
producing a small graded potential, and these sum both spatially across the dendritic tree and
temporally within a time window. Excitatory potentials depolarize toward threshold, inhibitory
ones hyperpolarize away from it, and whether the neuron fires depends on the net result at the
axon hillock. Inhibition positioned close to the soma can veto a large amount of distal
excitation, so the location of a synapse on the dendritic tree matters as much as its strength.

Synaptic transmission:
  chemical synapse       transmitter release, diffusion, receptor binding; slower
                         and highly modifiable
  electrical synapse     gap junctions; very fast, bidirectional, used where
                         synchrony matters
  ionotropic             the receptor is a channel; fast, milliseconds
  metabotropic           second messenger cascade; slow, amplifying, lasting
  glutamate              the principal excitatory transmitter
  GABA                   the principal inhibitory transmitter in the adult brain
  acetylcholine          neuromuscular junction, autonomic, and modulatory
  dopamine               reward prediction error, motor control, motivation
  serotonin              mood, sleep, appetite; widely modulatory
  norepinephrine         arousal, attention, the stress response
  neuropeptides          slow, diffuse, long-lasting modulation
  spatial summation      inputs at different locations combine
  temporal summation     inputs at different times combine within a window
  shunting inhibition    increases conductance, reducing the effect of excitation
                         without much hyperpolarization
  neuromodulation        changes the properties of a circuit rather than driving it
                         directly; how brain states are set

The distinction between transmission and modulation is worth keeping clear. Fast glutamate
and GABA transmission carries the moment-to-moment signal. Modulatory systems — dopamine,
serotonin, norepinephrine, acetylcholine — project diffusely from small nuclei and change how
the receiving circuits behave, adjusting gain, plasticity thresholds, and excitability. This is
why drugs acting on modulatory systems have such broad effects, and why understanding a
modulator's function in terms of a single behaviour is usually an oversimplification. Dopamine
is not the pleasure chemical; the best-supported account is that phasic dopamine encodes reward
prediction error, the difference between expected and received reward, which is the same
quantity that reinforcement learning algorithms use.

## Circuits, Sensory Systems, and Motor Control

Neural circuits combine neurons into functional units, and a few motifs recur across systems.
Feedforward excitation propagates signals. Feedforward inhibition, where an input excites both
a target and an interneuron that inhibits it, sharpens timing by limiting the window during
which the target can integrate. Recurrent excitation sustains activity after the input stops,
which is one mechanism of working memory. Recurrent inhibition implements competition, so that
the most strongly driven unit suppresses its neighbours — winner-take-all dynamics. Lateral
inhibition enhances contrast at boundaries.

Sensory systems share an organizational logic despite processing entirely different physical
signals. Receptors transduce the stimulus into electrical activity. Signals pass through
subcortical relays, in most modalities including the thalamus, to primary sensory cortex.
Processing proceeds hierarchically, with early stages responding to simple features and later
stages to progressively more complex and invariant ones. Topographic maps preserve the spatial
or featural organization of the receptor surface — retinotopic in vision, tonotopic in hearing,
somatotopic in touch — with cortical area allocated in proportion to receptor density rather
than to physical size, which is why the hands and lips occupy so much somatosensory cortex.

Motor control runs the hierarchy in the opposite direction. Cortical motor areas specify
intentions and movement parameters, the basal ganglia participate in action selection and the
initiation of movement, the cerebellum refines timing and coordination and adapts to errors,
and spinal circuits execute patterns and handle reflexes. The system is heavily predictive
rather than purely reactive: a forward model predicts the sensory consequences of a movement,
and comparing prediction to outcome both permits rapid correction faster than feedback allows
and cancels self-generated sensation, which is why self-tickling does not work.

Circuits and systems:
  feedforward inhibition  sharpens timing by limiting the integration window
  recurrent excitation    sustains activity; one basis of working memory
  lateral inhibition      enhances contrast at edges
  winner-take-all         competition through recurrent inhibition
  receptive field         the stimulus region and pattern driving a neuron
  hierarchical processing simple features to complex invariant representations
  topographic maps        retinotopic, tonotopic, somatotopic
  cortical magnification  area proportional to receptor density, not body size
  dorsal and ventral      where and how versus what; the two visual streams
  efference copy          a copy of the motor command used to predict consequences
  forward model           predicts sensory outcome; enables fast correction and
                          cancellation of self-generated sensation
  central pattern generator  spinal circuits producing rhythmic output without
                          descending command; walking, breathing, swimming
  reflex arc              the shortest loop; monosynaptic in the stretch reflex

The predictive framing has become central across neuroscience rather than being confined to
motor control. Perception is increasingly understood as inference — the brain maintaining
predictions about incoming sensation and processing primarily the discrepancy, which is
efficient because predicted input carries no new information. This accounts for a range of
phenomena including the strength of perceptual illusions, the effects of expectation on
detection, and the observation that much of the input to primary sensory cortex comes from
higher areas rather than from the sensory periphery.

## Learning, Memory, and Plasticity

Synaptic plasticity is the leading candidate mechanism for learning, and Hebb's principle
states its core idea: a synapse strengthens when presynaptic activity reliably contributes to
postsynaptic firing. The slogan that cells that fire together wire together captures it, with
the important qualification that the temporal order matters — spike-timing-dependent plasticity
shows strengthening when the presynaptic spike precedes the postsynaptic one by a few
milliseconds and weakening when the order reverses, which makes the rule causal rather than
merely correlational.

Long-term potentiation is the best-characterized cellular model. At glutamatergic synapses,
the NMDA receptor acts as a coincidence detector: it requires both glutamate binding, signalling
presynaptic activity, and postsynaptic depolarization to expel a magnesium block, so it opens
only when both are present. Calcium entering through it triggers cascades that insert additional
AMPA receptors, strengthening the synapse. Long-term depression weakens synapses under
different activity patterns, and both are necessary, since a system that only strengthens
saturates.

Memory systems are multiple and dissociable, which is established most clearly by patients
with selective damage. Declarative memory for facts and events depends on the hippocampus and
medial temporal lobe, and its impairment leaves procedural learning intact — a patient unable
to form new episodic memories can nonetheless improve at a motor skill across sessions while
denying having practised. Procedural memory depends on the basal ganglia and cerebellum.
Working memory depends on prefrontal circuits and is limited in capacity to a handful of items.

Learning and memory:
  Hebbian plasticity     coincident activity strengthens connections
  spike-timing dependent  order matters; pre-before-post strengthens
  long-term potentiation  NMDA receptor as coincidence detector; AMPA insertion
  long-term depression    weakening; necessary to prevent saturation
  homeostatic plasticity  scales all synapses to keep firing rates in range
  structural plasticity   new synapses and dendritic spines
  declarative memory      facts and events; hippocampus and medial temporal lobe
  procedural memory       skills; basal ganglia and cerebellum
  working memory          active maintenance; prefrontal; capacity ~4 items
  consolidation           stabilization over hours to years; sleep-dependent
  systems consolidation   gradual transfer from hippocampal to cortical dependence
  reconsolidation         retrieved memories become labile and are re-stored,
                          which means recall can alter the memory
  critical periods        windows of heightened plasticity during development
  adult neurogenesis      limited; the hippocampal dentate gyrus, with the extent
                          in humans genuinely contested

Reconsolidation deserves emphasis because it changes the intuitive model of memory as
storage. Retrieving a memory returns it to a labile state from which it must be re-stabilized,
and during that window it can be modified or disrupted. This explains why memories change with
repeated recall, why eyewitness testimony degrades through the very act of being rehearsed and
questioned, and why post-event information becomes incorporated into the recollection. It also
opens a therapeutic possibility, since disrupting reconsolidation of a traumatic memory during
the labile window may reduce its emotional force.

## Higher Function and Methods

Attention selects among competing inputs, and its neural signature is a modulation of
sensory responses: attended stimuli produce enhanced activity and suppressed responses to
unattended ones, effectively adjusting gain. It divides into a bottom-up component driven by
stimulus salience and a top-down component driven by goals, implemented by largely distinct
networks whose interaction determines what reaches awareness.

Executive function encompasses working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility,
and depends heavily on prefrontal cortex. Its late maturation — prefrontal development
continues into the mid-twenties — is the neurobiological correlate of the behavioural
observation that impulse control and long-horizon planning develop later than other cognitive
capacities.

Consciousness remains the hardest problem in the field, and the honest position is that no
theory commands consensus. Global workspace theory proposes that content becomes conscious when
it is broadcast widely across the brain, making it available to multiple systems. Integrated
information theory proposes a quantitative measure of the integration of information in a system
and identifies consciousness with it. Higher-order theories propose that a state is conscious
when it is the object of a suitable representation. Each has advocates, each faces objections,
and the empirical work has concentrated on identifying neural correlates of conscious access
rather than on adjudicating between them.

Methods and their tradeoffs:
  electrophysiology      single units; millisecond timing, single-cell resolution,
                         invasive, samples few cells
  multi-electrode arrays  hundreds to thousands of cells simultaneously
  EEG                    millisecond timing, poor spatial resolution, non-invasive,
                         cheap
  MEG                    similar timing, better spatial localization, expensive
  fMRI                   millimetre spatial resolution, seconds temporal, measures
                         blood oxygenation as an indirect proxy for activity
  PET                    metabolic and receptor imaging; radioactive tracers
  two-photon imaging     cellular resolution in living animals; calcium indicators
                         report activity indirectly and slowly
  optogenetics           light-gated channels permit causal manipulation of defined
                         cell types with millisecond precision
  chemogenetics          engineered receptors activated by an inert compound;
                         slower, less invasive
  lesion studies         classical causal evidence; confounded by reorganization
                         and by damage extending beyond the intended region
  connectomics           complete wiring diagrams; achieved for small organisms,
                         and being extended by volume electron microscopy

The distinction between correlation and causation is as pressing here as anywhere and is what
made optogenetics transformative. Imaging and recording establish that a population is active
during a behaviour, which is consistent with that population causing the behaviour, being
caused by it, or sharing a common cause. Optogenetic manipulation activates or silences defined
cell types on a millisecond timescale in behaving animals, so the causal contribution can be
tested directly. The caution that remains is that a manipulation sufficient to disrupt a
behaviour does not establish that the population's natural function is the one disrupted, since
perturbing any component of a tightly coupled system produces effects throughout it.

The interpretive caution that applies most broadly concerns functional imaging. A blood-flow
signal is an indirect and slow proxy for neural activity, spatial resolution encompasses
hundreds of thousands of neurons, and the statistical challenge of many voxels tested
simultaneously requires correction that is sometimes applied inadequately. Reverse inference —
concluding that a cognitive process occurred because an area associated with it was active — is
invalid unless that area is selective for that process, and most areas are not. The technique
is genuinely informative about where and when processing occurs and it does not license the
mind-reading claims that popular accounts frequently attach to it.
