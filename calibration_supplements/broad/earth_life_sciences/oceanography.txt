## Physical Properties and Structure

The ocean covers seventy-one percent of Earth's surface and contains ninety-seven percent of its
water, and its physical behaviour is governed by two properties that determine density:
temperature and salinity. Density controls whether water sinks or floats, which controls
stratification, which controls almost everything else — the vertical exchange of heat, of
nutrients, and of dissolved gases.

Seawater's density increases with salinity and, over most of the ocean's range, decreases with
temperature. The relationship is nonlinear, which produces effects that a linear intuition
misses. Cabbeling is the clearest: mixing two water masses of equal density but different
temperature and salinity produces water denser than either, so the mixture sinks — a mechanism
contributing to deep water formation.

The vertical structure divides into layers by density. A surface mixed layer is homogenized by
wind and waves, its depth varying from tens of metres in summer to hundreds in winter. Below it
the thermocline is a zone of rapid temperature decrease, and the corresponding density gradient —
the pycnocline — acts as a barrier to vertical exchange. Below that the deep ocean is cold,
uniform, and very slow to communicate with the surface.

The ocean's heat capacity deserves emphasis because it dominates the climate system's thermal
behaviour. Water's specific heat is over four times air's and the ocean's mass is vastly greater,
so the top few metres store as much heat as the entire atmosphere. This is why the ocean has
absorbed more than ninety percent of the excess heat from the planetary energy imbalance, why
ocean heat content is a far better measure of that imbalance than surface air temperature, and why
the climate system's response to a forcing is delayed by decades — the ocean must warm before
equilibrium is reached.

Q: Ocean heat content is a better indicator of the planetary energy imbalance than surface air
temperature because:

   capacity makes its temperature noisy
C. The ocean warms faster
D. Air temperature is not affected
Reasoning: The ocean's enormous heat capacity means it absorbs the overwhelming majority of the
accumulating energy, while the atmosphere stores a small fraction and its temperature fluctuates
substantially with ocean-atmosphere heat exchange such as El Niño. Ocean heat content therefore
integrates the imbalance directly with far less interannual noise. Answer: B.

## Circulation

Ocean circulation divides into a wind-driven surface component and a density-driven deep
component, coupled to each other and operating on entirely different timescales.

Surface circulation is driven by wind stress and shaped by Earth's rotation. The Coriolis effect
deflects moving water — to the right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern — and
because each layer of water drags the one beneath it with progressive deflection, the net
transport of the wind-driven layer is at right angles to the wind. This Ekman transport is why
wind blowing parallel to a coast drives upwelling or downwelling rather than moving water directly
onshore or offshore, and coastal upwelling brings nutrient-rich deep water to the surface,
supporting the most productive fisheries on Earth.

The great subtropical gyres result from the combination of wind patterns and rotation, and they
are asymmetric in a way that has large consequences. Western boundary currents — the Gulf Stream,
the Kuroshio — are narrow, fast, and deep, while the eastern return flow is broad and slow. This
intensification follows from the variation of the Coriolis parameter with latitude, and the
resulting concentrated poleward heat transport is why northwestern Europe is far milder than its
latitude would suggest.

Thermohaline circulation is driven by density differences arising from cooling and from salinity
changes. Deep water forms where surface water becomes dense enough to sink, which occurs in the
North Atlantic and around Antarctica, and it then spreads through the deep ocean and slowly
returns to the surface through mixing and upwelling. The full circuit takes on the order of a
thousand years, which sets the timescale over which the deep ocean communicates with the
atmosphere.

The Atlantic overturning circulation deserves specific attention because it is among the most
discussed tipping elements. Freshening of the North Atlantic surface — from ice melt and increased
precipitation — reduces the density of surface water and therefore inhibits the sinking that
drives the circulation. Palaeoclimate records show abrupt reorganizations in the past, and models
disagree substantially on the threshold and on whether current changes indicate approach to one.
The consequences of a substantial weakening would include cooling in the North Atlantic region
against a warming background, shifts in tropical rainfall belts, and accelerated sea level rise on
the North American east coast.

## Waves, Tides, and Coastal Processes

Surface waves are generated by wind, and their size depends on wind speed, duration, and fetch —
the distance over which the wind blows. Waves generated locally are steep and irregular; waves
that have travelled beyond their generating area sort themselves by speed, since longer waves
travel faster in deep water, arriving as regular swell. This dispersion is why swell arriving from
a distant storm becomes progressively shorter-period over the following days.

Wave behaviour changes fundamentally when the depth becomes comparable to the wavelength. In deep
water, water particles move in circles and the wave speed depends on wavelength. In shallow water
the circles flatten into ellipses and the speed depends only on depth, which makes waves refract
toward shallow water — bending to become parallel to the shore and concentrating energy on
headlands while spreading it in bays. This is why headlands erode and bays accumulate sediment.

Tides are forced by the gravitational gradient across the Earth from the Moon and Sun, and the
gradient rather than the force itself is what matters: the near side is pulled more than the
centre and the far side less, producing bulges on both sides. This is why there are two high tides
per day rather than one. Actual tides depart substantially from the equilibrium picture because
continents obstruct the tidal bulge, so tides propagate as waves in ocean basins with amplitudes
and timings set by basin resonance — which is why some locations have tides of a few centimetres
and others exceed fifteen metres.

Tsunamis behave differently from wind waves in a way that explains their destructiveness. Their
wavelength is hundreds of kilometres, so even the deep ocean is shallow relative to it, which
means they travel at the shallow water speed — several hundred kilometres per hour — while having
an amplitude of under a metre and being imperceptible to a ship. On approaching the coast the
speed falls with depth and the energy compresses into a shorter, taller wave. The long period is
also why the water continues rising for many minutes rather than breaking and retreating like an
ordinary wave, and why the flooding penetrates so far inland.

## Marine Chemistry and Ecosystems

Seawater's composition is remarkably constant in its major ions, whose ratios are the same
everywhere despite salinity varying, because the residence times of those ions are far longer than
the ocean's mixing time. This constancy is what permits salinity to be determined from a single
measurement of conductivity rather than requiring a full chemical analysis.

The carbonate system controls the ocean's pH and its capacity to absorb carbon dioxide. Dissolved
carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, which dissociates into bicarbonate and carbonate, and the
equilibrium among these buffers the pH against change. It is also why the ocean absorbs so much
atmospheric carbon dioxide — roughly a quarter of emissions — and why doing so lowers the pH.
Surface ocean pH has fallen about 0.1 units since preindustrial times, which is a thirty percent
increase in hydrogen ion concentration.

Ocean acidification's consequences fall on organisms building calcium carbonate structures.
Lowering the pH reduces the carbonate ion concentration, which reduces the saturation state with
respect to calcium carbonate, making shell and skeleton formation more energetically costly and
eventually causing dissolution. The saturation horizon — the depth below which calcium carbonate
dissolves — is shoaling, and cold water holds more carbon dioxide so polar regions are affected
first.

Hydrothermal vent communities deserve emphasis for what they established. Discovered in 1977,
they support dense ecosystems in complete darkness, based on chemosynthetic bacteria oxidizing
hydrogen sulfide rather than on photosynthesis. This demonstrated that an ecosystem need not
depend on sunlight, which expanded the conditions under which life is considered possible and
directly informs where life might exist elsewhere — the subsurface oceans of icy moons have
precisely this energy source available.

## The Ocean, Climate, and Observation

The ocean's role in climate operates through several coupled mechanisms. It stores heat, absorbing
over ninety percent of the excess energy from the planetary imbalance. It absorbs carbon, taking
up roughly a quarter of anthropogenic emissions at the cost of acidification. It transports heat
poleward, moderating temperature gradients. And it exchanges water with the atmosphere, driving
the hydrological cycle.

Sea level rise has two roughly comparable contributions currently. Thermal expansion accounts for
a substantial fraction, since warming water expands, and this component is committed for centuries
because the deep ocean warms slowly and will continue to expand long after surface warming stops.
Ice mass loss from glaciers and from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets accounts for the
remainder and is the component with the larger uncertainty and the larger potential.

El Niño and the Southern Oscillation is the largest source of interannual climate variability and
is fundamentally an ocean-atmosphere coupled phenomenon. Normally trade winds pile warm water in
the western Pacific and upwelling brings cold water to the surface in the east. During El Niño the
winds weaken, the warm pool spreads east, upwelling ceases, and the atmospheric circulation
reorganizes — producing drought in some regions and flooding in others and raising global mean
surface temperature by a few tenths of a degree for a year.

The Argo programme deserves the closing note because it changed what is knowable about the ocean.
Before it, ocean interior measurements came from research vessels along specific tracks, so the
global subsurface state was known from a sparse and irregular sample and the estimates of ocean
heat content carried enormous uncertainty. Argo deployed thousands of autonomous floats that drift
at depth, rise to the surface every ten days measuring temperature and salinity, transmit by
satellite, and sink again. The result is continuous global coverage of the upper two kilometres,
and it made ocean heat content — and therefore the planetary energy imbalance — measurable with a
precision that was previously unattainable. It is an instructive case of an observing system
changing a field's central questions from what is happening to why.
