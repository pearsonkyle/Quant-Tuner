## Homeostasis and Regulation

Physiology is the study of how living systems maintain function, and homeostasis is its
organizing principle: the maintenance of internal conditions within narrow ranges despite
external variation. Body temperature, blood pH, glucose concentration, osmolarity, and
electrolyte balance are all regulated within limits far tighter than the environment they exist
in, and the mechanisms are control systems in the engineering sense.

Negative feedback is the dominant mechanism and its structure is always the same: a sensor
detects a variable, a control centre compares it against a set point, and effectors act to
reduce the discrepancy. Thermoregulation is the clearest example — thermoreceptors report
temperature, the hypothalamus compares it against a set point, and shivering, vasoconstriction,
sweating, and vasodilation adjust heat production and loss. The set point is not fixed: fever
raises it, which is why a feverish patient feels cold and shivers while their temperature is
already elevated, since the body is defending a higher target.

Positive feedback amplifies rather than corrects, and it appears only where a rapid
irreversible transition is needed. Blood clotting, the action potential's upstroke, and the
uterine contractions of labour are the standard examples, each accelerating until a terminating
event occurs. Where positive feedback appears without a termination mechanism the result is
pathological — septic shock and some arrhythmias have this character, which is why they
deteriorate rapidly rather than stabilizing.

Regulatory concepts:
  homeostasis          maintenance within narrow ranges despite external variation
  negative feedback    sensor, comparator, effector; the dominant mechanism
  set point            the defended value; adjustable, as fever demonstrates
  positive feedback    amplifies; used where rapid irreversible transition is
                       needed, pathological where unterminated
  feedforward          anticipatory adjustment before the disturbance; the
                       cephalic phase of digestion, anticipatory heart rate rise
  gain                 how strongly the system corrects; higher gain holds the
                       variable tighter and risks oscillation
  reserve capacity     the margin above resting requirement; its loss is what
                       makes a stressor dangerous in a compromised patient
  compensation         a system offsetting another's failure; masks the underlying
                       problem until the compensating reserve is exhausted
  decompensation       the abrupt deterioration when compensation fails
  allostasis           adjusting set points to meet anticipated demand

Compensation deserves emphasis because it explains a clinically important pattern: a patient
can appear stable while a serious problem progresses, then deteriorate abruptly. A failing heart
is compensated by increased rate, increased contractility, and fluid retention, each of which
maintains output at the cost of reserve. Blood pressure is among the last things to fall in
shock, because vasoconstriction maintains it while perfusion is already inadequate. The
implication is that normal vital signs do not establish that a patient is well, and that markers
of the compensation — tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure, rising lactate — are earlier signals
than the variable being defended.

Q: A patient in early shock has a normal blood pressure. This most likely indicates:
A. The patient is not in shock
B. Compensatory vasoconstriction and tachycardia are maintaining pressure while perfusion is
   already inadequate
C. The measurement is incorrect
D. Blood volume is normal
Reasoning: Blood pressure is a defended variable, maintained by increasing heart rate and
systemic vascular resistance until those reserves are exhausted. It is therefore a late sign,
and shock is defined by inadequate tissue perfusion rather than by hypotension. Tachycardia,
narrowed pulse pressure, delayed capillary refill, and rising lactate precede the fall.
Answer: B.

## Cardiovascular System

The heart is two pumps in series. The right side receives systemic venous return and pumps to
the lungs at low pressure; the left receives oxygenated blood and pumps to the body at high
pressure. Because they are in series, their outputs must be equal over time — any sustained
mismatch would accumulate blood in one circuit — and the mechanism enforcing this is the
Frank-Starling relationship, whereby a greater filling volume stretches the myocardium and
produces a stronger contraction.

Cardiac output is the product of heart rate and stroke volume, and stroke volume depends on
three factors that are the framework for nearly all cardiovascular reasoning. Preload is the
filling volume, determined by venous return, and increases output through Frank-Starling.
Afterload is the pressure the ventricle must overcome to eject, and increases reduce output.
Contractility is the intrinsic force at a given preload, and is increased by sympathetic
stimulation and by inotropic drugs.

Blood pressure is the product of cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, and this
identity organizes both the physiology and the pharmacology. Every antihypertensive works by
reducing one term: diuretics reduce volume and therefore output, beta blockers reduce rate and
contractility, calcium channel blockers and vasodilators reduce resistance, and drugs acting on
the renin-angiotensin system reduce both.

Cardiovascular relationships:
  cardiac output       heart rate times stroke volume; ~5 L/min at rest
  preload              filling volume; Frank-Starling increases output
  afterload            ejection resistance; increases reduce output
  contractility        intrinsic force at a given preload
  ejection fraction    the fraction of ventricular volume ejected; ~55-70% normal
  blood pressure       cardiac output times systemic vascular resistance
  mean arterial pressure  approximately diastolic plus a third of the pulse
                       pressure; the perfusion-relevant figure
  autoregulation       organs maintain their own blood flow across a pressure
                       range; the brain and kidney do this strongly
  baroreceptor reflex  rapid pressure correction via rate and resistance
  conduction system    SA node, AV node, bundle branches, Purkinje fibres
  the AV delay         allows atrial contribution to filling before ventricular
                       contraction
  coronary perfusion   occurs mainly in diastole, since systolic compression
                       obstructes flow; this is why tachycardia, which shortens
                       diastole disproportionately, precipitates ischaemia
  Starling forces      hydrostatic and oncotic pressure across the capillary;
                       determine filtration and reabsorption, and their imbalance
                       causes oedema

Coronary perfusion occurring in diastole is a detail with large clinical consequences. The
myocardium is compressed during systole so its own blood supply flows mainly when it relaxes,
and increasing heart rate shortens diastole proportionally more than systole. A patient with
narrowed coronary arteries therefore develops ischaemia when the heart rate rises, both because
demand increases and because supply time decreases — which is why rate control is central to
managing angina and why tachycardia in a patient with coronary disease is treated rather than
tolerated.

## Respiratory and Renal Systems

Respiration exchanges oxygen and carbon dioxide, and the two gases behave differently in ways
that determine clinical reasoning. Carbon dioxide is far more soluble and diffuses readily, so
its elimination depends almost entirely on ventilation — the volume of air moved per minute. It
is the variable that respiratory drive is tuned to, with central chemoreceptors responding to
the pH change that carbon dioxide produces in cerebrospinal fluid.

Oxygen uptake depends on ventilation, on diffusion across the alveolar membrane, and on the
matching of ventilation to perfusion. That last is the usual limiting factor: a region ventilated
and not perfused contributes dead space, and a region perfused and not ventilated contributes
shunt. Shunt is the more problematic because blood passing unventilated alveoli is not
oxygenated at all, and increasing the inspired oxygen concentration does not help — which is why
hypoxaemia that fails to correct with supplemental oxygen indicates shunt physiology.

The oxygen-haemoglobin dissociation curve is sigmoid, and its shape is functional. The flat
upper portion means that substantial falls in alveolar oxygen tension produce little change in
saturation, which provides a reserve at altitude and in mild lung disease. The steep middle
portion means that at tissue oxygen tensions, small further falls release large amounts of
oxygen. And the curve shifts: acidosis, hypercapnia, elevated temperature, and elevated
2,3-DPG shift it rightward, reducing affinity and releasing more oxygen — exactly the conditions
present in metabolically active tissue.

Respiratory and renal:
  minute ventilation   rate times tidal volume; determines CO2 elimination
  dead space           ventilated, not perfused; wasted ventilation
  shunt                perfused, not ventilated; hypoxaemia unresponsive to
                       supplemental oxygen
  V/Q mismatch         the usual cause of hypoxaemia in lung disease
  dissociation curve   sigmoid; flat above, steep in the tissue range
  right shift          acidosis, CO2, heat, 2,3-DPG; releases more oxygen where
                       metabolism is high
  compliance           lung distensibility; reduced in fibrosis and oedema
  resistance           airway calibre; increased in asthma and COPD
  nephron              glomerulus, proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule,
                       collecting duct
  glomerular filtration  ~180 L/day filtered, ~1.5 L excreted; the reabsorption is
                       the work
  countercurrent multiplier  the loop of Henle establishes a medullary osmotic
                       gradient enabling concentrated urine
  ADH                  increases collecting duct water permeability; the main
                       osmolarity control
  aldosterone          sodium retention and potassium excretion; volume control
  RAAS                 renin, angiotensin, aldosterone; blood pressure and volume
  acid-base            respiratory adjusts CO2 in minutes, renal adjusts
                       bicarbonate over days; the two compensate for each other

The acid-base system's two-speed structure is the key to interpreting a blood gas. A primary
respiratory disturbance changes carbon dioxide immediately and the kidney compensates by
adjusting bicarbonate over one to three days. A primary metabolic disturbance changes
bicarbonate and the lungs compensate within minutes by changing ventilation. The pattern of which
is deranged, which direction the other has moved, and whether the compensation is complete
identifies the primary problem, its duration, and whether more than one process is present.

## Endocrine, Gastrointestinal, and Musculoskeletal

The endocrine system regulates through hormones travelling in the bloodstream, which makes it
slower and more sustained than neural control. Most axes have the same architecture: the
hypothalamus releases a releasing hormone, the pituitary releases a stimulating hormone, the
target gland releases the effector hormone, and the effector inhibits the two levels above it.
That negative feedback structure means the level of the stimulating hormone diagnoses where a
problem lies — a low effector hormone with a high stimulating hormone indicates target gland
failure, while both being low indicates a pituitary or hypothalamic problem.

Glucose regulation illustrates opposing hormone pairs. Insulin, released when glucose rises,
promotes uptake and storage and is the only hormone that lowers glucose. Glucagon, cortisol,
growth hormone, and adrenaline all raise it, which is why hypoglycaemia has redundant defences
and hyperglycaemia has only one. The consequence is that loss of insulin action produces
sustained hyperglycaemia with no compensating mechanism, which is the pathophysiology of
diabetes.

The gastrointestinal tract digests and absorbs, and its regional specialization is functional.
The stomach's acid denatures protein and kills organisms and begins protein digestion; the
duodenum receives pancreatic enzymes and bile and is where most digestion occurs; the jejunum
and ileum absorb the products, with specific sites for specific nutrients — iron and calcium
proximally, bile salts and vitamin B12 in the terminal ileum. That regional specificity means
resection or disease of a segment produces predictable deficiencies.

Endocrine, GI, and musculoskeletal:
  hypothalamic-pituitary  releasing, stimulating, effector, with feedback
                       inhibition
  primary failure      low effector, high stimulating hormone
  secondary failure    low effector, low stimulating hormone
  insulin              the only glucose-lowering hormone
  counter-regulatory   glucagon, cortisol, growth hormone, adrenaline; redundant
                       defence against hypoglycaemia
  thyroid              sets metabolic rate; the axis is the classic feedback
                       teaching example
  cortisol             stress response, gluconeogenesis, immune suppression;
                       diurnal rhythm
  calcium regulation   parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, calcitonin; bone, gut, and
                       kidney
  gastric acid         denatures protein, kills organisms, begins pepsin
                       activation
  terminal ileum       bile salt and vitamin B12 absorption; resection causes
                       specific deficiencies
  enteric nervous system  the gut's own extensive neural network; largely
                       autonomous
  microbiome           metabolic, immune, and barrier functions; an active area
                       with more claims than established mechanisms
  sarcomere            the contractile unit; sliding filament mechanism
  motor unit           one motor neuron and the fibres it innervates; recruitment

  bone remodelling     osteoblasts build, osteoclasts resorb; continuous, and
                       responsive to mechanical load

## Clinical Reasoning and Evidence

Clinical reasoning combines pattern recognition with analytical inference, and the balance
depends on the presentation's familiarity. Experienced clinicians recognize common
presentations rapidly and correctly, and that same pattern recognition produces characteristic
errors when a presentation resembles a common condition and is not it. The analytical mode —
generating a differential, weighing each possibility, seeking discriminating evidence — is
slower and is what catches those cases.

Diagnostic testing is best understood probabilistically. A test does not establish a diagnosis;
it moves the probability from before the test to after it, and how far it moves depends on the
test's likelihood ratios and on where the probability started. This is why the same result means
different things in different patients: a positive result in a low-prevalence population is
frequently a false positive, and a negative result in a high-probability patient does not
exclude the diagnosis.

The consequence for practice is that pre-test probability must be estimated before ordering,
and that tests should be ordered when the result would change management. A test whose positive
and negative results lead to the same next step provides information without value, and in
aggregate such testing produces incidental findings that generate further investigation, cost,
and occasionally harm — the cascade effect that overtesting reliably produces.

Clinical reasoning and evidence:
  pre-test probability  from prevalence, history, and examination; determines what
                       the result means
  sensitivity          proportion of diseased correctly identified; a highly
                       sensitive test's negative result is good at ruling out
  specificity          proportion of well correctly identified; a highly specific
                       test's positive result is good at ruling in
  predictive values    depend on prevalence; the same test performs differently in
                       different populations
  likelihood ratio     how much a result shifts the odds; independent of
                       prevalence, which is why it is the more portable measure
  test threshold       order a test when the result would change management
  incidentalomas       findings unrelated to the question; the cost of imaging
                       broadly
  number needed to treat  patients treated for one to benefit; converts relative
                       effects into interpretable terms
  absolute vs relative risk  a 50% relative reduction of a 0.2% risk is a 0.1%
                       absolute reduction; reporting only the relative figure
                       systematically overstates benefit
  surrogate endpoints  a biomarker standing in for an outcome; several drugs have
                       improved a surrogate and worsened mortality
  evidence hierarchy   systematic review, randomized trial, cohort, case-control,
                       case series; with the caveat that a well-conducted
                       observational study can outrank a poor trial
  clinical significance  distinct from statistical significance; a detectable
                       effect may be too small to matter

The absolute versus relative risk distinction deserves the strongest emphasis because it is the
most consequential communication issue in medicine. A treatment halving the risk of an event
sounds compelling and means something entirely different depending on the baseline: halving a
40% risk prevents twenty events per hundred treated, and halving a 0.2% risk prevents one per
thousand. Both are correctly described as a fifty percent reduction. Presenting only the
relative figure, which is standard in press coverage and common in abstracts, systematically
overstates benefit and is a substantial contributor to unrealistic expectations of treatment.

Surrogate endpoints deserve a closing caution because the failures have been severe. A
surrogate is a measurable intermediate assumed to predict the outcome of interest — bone density
for fracture, blood pressure for stroke, tumour response for survival, arrhythmia suppression
for cardiac death. Some surrogates are well validated and some are not, and the history includes
drugs that improved the surrogate impressively and increased mortality: antiarrhythmics that
suppressed the arrhythmia and raised death rates, and hormone therapies that improved the
biomarker and worsened the outcome. The lesson is that a mechanism connecting the surrogate to
the outcome is not sufficient evidence that treating the surrogate improves the outcome, and
only trials measuring the outcome itself can establish it.
