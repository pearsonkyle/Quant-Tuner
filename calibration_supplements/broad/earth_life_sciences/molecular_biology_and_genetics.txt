## Nucleic Acids and Molecular Structure

DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel strands, each a polymer of nucleotides
joined by phosphodiester bonds between the 3' hydroxyl of one sugar and the 5'
phosphate of the next. The antiparallel arrangement — one strand running 5' to 3'
while its partner runs 3' to 5' — is not an incidental detail. It is why replication
is continuous on one strand and discontinuous on the other, why every polymerase
synthesizes only in the 5' to 3' direction, and why sequence conventions must always
specify a strand and a direction.

Base pairing is specific and asymmetric in strength: adenine pairs with thymine through
two hydrogen bonds, guanine with cytosine through three. That difference in bond count
has direct practical consequences. GC-rich sequences have higher melting temperatures,
so PCR primer design must balance GC content to get consistent annealing across a
multiplex reaction, and GC-rich regions are systematically harder to amplify and
sequence. The complementarity itself is what makes the molecule an information carrier
that can be copied: each strand specifies the other, so a template plus a polymerase
suffices to reconstruct the whole.

RNA differs from DNA in three ways that together determine its role. It has ribose
rather than deoxyribose, with an extra 2' hydroxyl that makes the backbone chemically
reactive and the molecule far less stable — which is appropriate for a transient
message and disastrous for archival storage. It uses uracil in place of thymine.
And it is typically single-stranded, which lets it fold into complex secondary and
tertiary structures with catalytic activity. That last property is the basis of the RNA
world hypothesis: a molecule that both stores information and catalyzes reactions could
bootstrap life without requiring proteins and DNA to arise simultaneously, and the
ribosome's catalytic core being made of RNA rather than protein is strong evidence
that this is a relic rather than a speculation.

Nucleic acid quick reference:
  DNA bases          A, T, G, C; A-T two H-bonds, G-C three
  RNA bases          A, U, G, C
  backbone           5' phosphate to 3' hydroxyl, phosphodiester bonds
  helix geometry     B-form, ~10.5 bp per turn, 3.4 A rise per base, 2 nm diameter
  melting temp       rises with GC content and with length and salt concentration
  Chargaff's rules   in duplex DNA, A = T and G = C
  human genome       ~3.1 Gb haploid, ~20,000 protein-coding genes, <2% coding
  codon              3 bases; 64 codons encode 20 amino acids plus stop
  genetic code       redundant (multiple codons per amino acid), near-universal

The genetic code's redundancy is structured rather than arbitrary, and the structure is
protective. The third base of a codon is the most variable — wobble position — so many
substitutions there are synonymous and change no amino acid. Codons for chemically
similar amino acids also tend to be similar, so a nonsynonymous change at the first or
second position often substitutes a residue with comparable properties. The code
therefore buffers against both mutation and translational error, which is very likely
why this particular assignment among the astronomically many possible ones was fixed
so early and has barely changed since.

Q: A single base substitution changes a codon from GCU to GCC, both encoding alanine.
This mutation is:
A. Nonsense   B. Missense   C. Synonymous   D. Frameshift
Reasoning: The protein sequence is unchanged, so the mutation is synonymous (silent).
Note that synonymous does not mean consequence-free — codon usage affects translation
speed and can alter co-translational folding, and the change may disrupt a splice site
or a regulatory element. Answer: C.

Q: An insertion of two nucleotides in a coding region is most likely to:
A. Change one amino acid
B. Shift the reading frame, altering all downstream residues and often introducing a
   premature stop
C. Have no effect because the code is redundant
D. Only affect the promoter
Reasoning: Insertions or deletions of lengths not divisible by three shift the reading
frame from that point onward, so every downstream codon is misread and a premature stop
usually appears within a few dozen codons. This is why indels are typically far more
damaging than substitutions, and why three-nucleotide indels are comparatively benign.
Answer: B.

## The Central Dogma

Information flows from DNA to RNA to protein. Replication copies DNA, transcription
produces RNA from a DNA template, and translation builds protein from an mRNA
sequence. Reverse transcription, from RNA back to DNA, is the well-known exception used
by retroviruses and by retrotransposons, and it is the basis of cDNA synthesis, RNA-seq
library preparation, and the reverse-transcriptase step in RT-PCR diagnostics. What the
dogma actually asserts is narrower than it is often stated: information does not flow
back out of protein into nucleic acid sequence.

Replication is semiconservative — each daughter duplex has one parental and one new
strand — and it is remarkably accurate. DNA polymerase makes roughly one error per
100,000 bases, its 3' to 5' proofreading exonuclease improves that to one in ten
million, and mismatch repair brings the final rate to about one error per billion bases
per division. The directionality constraint forces the leading strand to be synthesized
continuously toward the fork while the lagging strand is built backward in Okazaki
fragments that are later joined by ligase. Because polymerases cannot initiate de novo,
primase lays down RNA primers, and because the terminal primer on a linear chromosome
leaves a gap, ends shorten each division — the end-replication problem, solved in germ
line and stem cells by telomerase and unsolved in somatic cells, which is one component
of cellular senescence.

Transcription copies one strand into RNA. RNA polymerase binds a promoter, unwinds the
duplex, and synthesizes 5' to 3' using the template strand, so the resulting RNA
matches the coding strand with U for T. In eukaryotes the primary transcript is
extensively processed: a 7-methylguanosine cap is added at the 5' end for stability and
ribosome recruitment, a poly-A tail at the 3' end for stability and export, and introns
are excised by the spliceosome. Alternative splicing lets one gene produce many protein
isoforms, which is the main reason humans manage vast proteomic complexity with only
about 20,000 genes — a number that was a genuine surprise when the genome was
sequenced, having been widely expected to be closer to 100,000.

Translation reads the mRNA in triplets at the ribosome. Transfer RNAs bearing
anticodons deliver the matching amino acids, and peptide bonds form between them,
catalyzed by ribosomal RNA rather than by protein. Initiation begins at a start codon,
AUG encoding methionine, in an appropriate sequence context; elongation proceeds codon
by codon; termination occurs at one of three stop codons, recognized by release factors
rather than by tRNAs. The process is fast — roughly 20 amino acids per second in
bacteria — and energetically expensive, consuming several ATP equivalents per residue,
which is why translation is so heavily regulated.

Central dogma summary:
  replication      DNA -> DNA;   semiconservative, ~1e-9 error rate after repair
  transcription    DNA -> RNA;   RNA polymerase, promoter-driven, 5'->3'
  RNA processing   capping, polyadenylation, splicing (eukaryotes)
  translation      RNA -> protein; ribosome, tRNA adaptors, 5'->3' along mRNA
  reverse transcr. RNA -> DNA;   retroviruses, retrotransposons, cDNA synthesis
  start codon      AUG (methionine)
  stop codons      UAA, UAG, UGA

## Gene Regulation

Every cell in an organism carries the same genome, so differentiation is a matter of
which genes are expressed rather than which are present. Regulation occurs at every
step from chromatin accessibility to protein degradation, and the transcriptional layer
is the most studied because it is the most economical — not making a transcript is
cheaper than making and then degrading it.

In bacteria the operon is the canonical unit: functionally related genes transcribed
together from one promoter under shared control. The lac operon is the textbook
example and remains instructive because it combines both logics. A repressor blocks
transcription in the absence of lactose (negative control, inducible), while catabolite
activator protein bound to cyclic AMP is required for strong transcription and is
present only when glucose is scarce (positive control). The result is a logical AND:
the operon expresses substantially only when lactose is present and glucose is absent,
which is exactly the condition under which metabolizing lactose is worthwhile.

Eukaryotic regulation adds chromatin as a layer bacteria lack. DNA is wrapped around
histone octamers into nucleosomes and packed into higher-order structure, and
accessibility determines whether transcription factors can bind at all. Histone tails
carry covalent modifications — acetylation generally loosens chromatin and associates
with active transcription, while specific methylation marks can indicate either
activation or repression depending on the residue — and DNA itself can be methylated at
CpG dinucleotides, typically silencing the associated promoter. These marks are
heritable through cell division without changing the sequence, which is the molecular
substance of epigenetics.

Regulatory mechanisms by level:
  chromatin        nucleosome positioning, histone modification, DNA methylation
  transcriptional  promoters, enhancers (which can act over great distances via
                   looping), silencers, insulators, transcription factor binding
  post-transcript. alternative splicing, RNA editing, nuclear export control
  RNA stability    microRNAs and siRNAs targeting transcripts for degradation or
                   translational repression; 3' UTR elements
  translational    initiation factor availability, upstream open reading frames,
                   ribosome stalling
  post-translat.   phosphorylation, ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation,
                   glycosylation, proteolytic activation

Enhancers acting at a distance is the feature that most distinguishes eukaryotic
regulation and the one with the greatest clinical relevance. An enhancer may sit
hundreds of kilobases from the gene it controls, contacting the promoter through a
chromatin loop, and topologically associating domains constrain which enhancers can
reach which promoters. Disrupting a domain boundary can therefore cause a gene to fall
under the control of an enhancer it should never see, producing disease without any
alteration to the gene's own sequence — a mechanism that explains a growing fraction of
the noncoding variants implicated by genome-wide association studies.

Gene regulatory networks built from these components implement genuine computation.
Feedback loops produce switches and oscillators; a positive feedback loop with
cooperative binding gives bistability, so a transient signal produces a permanent
change of state, which is how cell fate decisions are made irreversible. Negative
feedback with delay gives oscillation, which is the architecture of circadian clocks
and of the segmentation clock in developing embryos. Feed-forward loops filter
transient signals, responding only to inputs sustained beyond a threshold duration.
These motifs recur across organisms because they solve recurring problems, and they are
the direct inspiration for synthetic biology's engineered circuits.

## Inheritance and Population Genetics

Mendelian inheritance follows from meiosis. Alleles segregate because homologous
chromosomes separate in the first division, and genes on different chromosomes assort
independently because those separations are independent. Genes on the same chromosome
do not assort independently — they are linked, and the frequency with which they
recombine is proportional to the distance between them, which is what made genetic
mapping possible long before sequencing existed.

Dominance describes the phenotype of the heterozygote, not the frequency or importance
of an allele, and this is the most persistent misconception in the subject. A dominant
allele is not more common, not stronger, and not favoured by selection; it simply means
one copy suffices to produce the phenotype. Incomplete dominance yields an intermediate
phenotype, codominance expresses both alleles fully as in the AB blood group, and many
traits are polygenic, with continuous variation arising from many loci of small effect
plus environment — which is why most traits of interest do not show clean Mendelian
ratios at all.

Population genetics quantifies how allele frequencies change. Hardy-Weinberg
equilibrium is the null model: in an infinite, randomly mating population with no
selection, mutation, or migration, allele frequencies p and q remain constant and
genotype frequencies settle at p^2, 2pq, and q^2 after a single generation. Its value is
as a baseline — a locus deviating from Hardy-Weinberg proportions signals that
something is happening, whether selection, population structure, assortative mating, or,
most often in practice, a genotyping error. Deviation testing is a standard quality
control step in every association study for exactly this reason.

Forces changing allele frequencies:
  natural selection   non-random differential reproduction; the only force that
                      systematically builds adaptation
  genetic drift       random sampling; magnitude scales as 1/(2N), so it dominates
                      in small populations and is negligible in large ones
  gene flow           migration; homogenizes populations, opposes divergence
  mutation            the ultimate source of variation; rate per site per generation
                      is small (~1e-8 in humans) but genome-wide yields ~50-100 new
                      variants per individual
  non-random mating   changes genotype frequencies without changing allele
                      frequencies; inbreeding raises homozygosity
  Hardy-Weinberg      p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1; the no-evolution null

Effective population size, not census size, governs drift, and it is typically far
smaller — reduced by unequal sex ratios, variance in reproductive success, and
historical bottlenecks. This is why conservation genetics worries about populations
that look numerically healthy, and why human genetic diversity is lower than the
current population would suggest: the species passed through bottlenecks whose signature
persists in linkage disequilibrium patterns and in the excess of rare variants that
recent explosive growth has produced.

Q: A recessive disease affects 1 in 10,000 people in a population at Hardy-Weinberg
equilibrium. What fraction of the population are carriers?
A. 1 in 100   B. 1 in 50   C. 1 in 10,000   D. 1 in 200
Reasoning: Affected individuals are q^2 = 1/10,000, so q = 1/100. Carrier frequency is
2pq = 2 * 0.99 * 0.01 ≈ 0.0198, about 1 in 50. The general lesson is that carriers vastly
outnumber affected individuals for rare recessives — roughly 2/q times as many — which
is why carrier screening detects far more than disease incidence suggests. Answer: B.

Q: Genetic drift has the largest effect in:
A. Large populations with strong selection
B. Small populations, regardless of selection
C. Populations with high migration
D. Populations at Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
Reasoning: Drift is sampling error in allele transmission and its magnitude scales as
1/(2N_e). In small populations it can fix or eliminate alleles regardless of their
fitness effects, overwhelming weak selection; in large populations it is negligible.
The relevant comparison is between the selection coefficient s and 1/(2N_e): when s is
smaller, the allele behaves as effectively neutral. Answer: B.

## Sequencing, Bioinformatics, and Genome Editing

Sanger sequencing, using chain-terminating dideoxynucleotides, produces reads up to
about 1,000 bases at very high per-base accuracy and remains the standard for verifying
a single construct. Next-generation sequencing changed the economics entirely by
massively parallelizing short reads: Illumina sequencing-by-synthesis produces hundreds
of millions of reads of 100-300 bases with error rates near 0.1%, at a cost per base
that has fallen faster than Moore's law for two decades. Long-read platforms — PacBio's
single-molecule real-time sequencing and Oxford Nanopore's measurement of ionic current
as DNA passes through a pore — produce reads of tens to hundreds of kilobases at higher
error rates, and their length is what resolves repetitive regions, structural variants,
and haplotype phasing that short reads cannot.

Coverage depth is the parameter governing what an experiment can detect, and the
requirement varies enormously by application. Germline variant calling in a diploid
genome is reliable at around 30x, since a heterozygous site should appear in about half
the reads and 30x gives adequate statistical separation from sequencing error. Somatic
variant calling in a tumour with low purity and subclonal populations may need 500x to
1000x, because the variant allele fraction can be a few percent. RNA-seq for
differential expression is measured in reads per sample rather than coverage, with
20-30 million reads typical, and the binding constraint is usually the number of
biological replicates rather than depth — three replicates at 20 million reads is far
more informative than one at 60 million, a tradeoff that is routinely gotten wrong.

Bioinformatics workflow and formats:
  FASTQ         raw reads plus per-base Phred quality scores; Q30 = 1 error in 1000
  quality control  adapter trimming, quality filtering, contamination screening
  alignment     BWA-MEM or minimap2 to a reference; output is SAM/BAM/CRAM
  de novo assembly  when no reference exists; long reads dramatically improve contiguity
  variant calling  GATK, DeepVariant, bcftools; output is VCF
  annotation    predict functional consequence of each variant
  RNA-seq       pseudo-alignment (salmon, kallisto) or spliced alignment (STAR),
                then differential expression (DESeq2, edgeR) with multiple-testing
                correction across ~20,000 genes
  key statistic  Benjamini-Hochberg FDR, not Bonferroni; genomics expects many
                true positives

CRISPR-Cas9 turned genome editing from a specialist undertaking into a routine
technique. A guide RNA of about 20 nucleotides directs the Cas9 nuclease to a matching
genomic sequence adjacent to a protospacer adjacent motif, where it creates a
double-strand break. The cell then repairs the break by one of two pathways, and which
one operates determines the outcome. Non-homologous end joining is error-prone and
introduces small insertions or deletions, which is how genes are knocked out.
Homology-directed repair uses a supplied template to introduce a precise edit but
operates efficiently only in dividing cells and is generally much less frequent.

The refinements address the limitations of that basic scheme. Base editors fuse a
catalytically impaired Cas9 to a deaminase, converting one base to another without
creating a double-strand break, which avoids the indel-generating repair pathway
entirely. Prime editors use a reverse transcriptase and an extended guide carrying the
desired sequence, enabling small insertions, deletions, and all base conversions with
greater precision. Off-target editing at sites with partial guide complementarity
remains the central safety concern, addressed by careful guide design, high-fidelity
Cas9 variants, and empirical genome-wide off-target assays rather than computational
prediction alone.

[assistant] Use guides 1 and 2. Here is what the numbers are telling you.

Guide 3 has the best predicted on-target efficiency but seven sites with three or fewer

Guide 4 targets exon 5 of 9, which is late enough to be a real risk. A frameshift there
can still permit a truncated protein with residual function, and it also sits far
enough into the transcript that nonsense-mediated decay is less reliable. Target early
exons, ideally within the first third of the coding sequence and in an exon common to
every annotated isoform — otherwise you knock out some isoforms and not others.

Three things beyond guide selection that determine whether the experiment is
interpretable. First, use at least two independent guides targeting different sites; a
phenotype reproduced by both is evidence about the gene, while a phenotype from one
guide is evidence about that guide. Second, validate at the protein level by western
blot, not only by sequencing the edit — an indel confirmed by sequencing can still
yield a functional protein through exon skipping or an alternative start codon, and
this is a common and embarrassing failure. Third, include a non-targeting control guide
so you can separate the gene's effect from the general stress of Cas9 activity and
double-strand break repair.

If the gene is essential you will not recover knockout clones at all, and the absence of
clones is itself a result. Plan for an inducible or conditional system if that is a
realistic possibility.

## Evolution

Evolution is change in allele frequencies over generations, and natural selection is
the only mechanism among the several that produces adaptation. The argument requires
just three conditions: heritable variation exists, that variation affects reproductive
success, and more offspring are produced than survive. Given those, differential
reproduction follows necessarily, and it is this deductive character that makes
selection so robust a principle.

Selection takes several forms distinguished by their effect on the trait distribution.
Directional selection favours one extreme and shifts the mean. Stabilizing selection
favours intermediates and reduces variance, which is why human birth weight clusters
tightly — too small and survival falls, too large and delivery becomes dangerous.
Disruptive selection favours both extremes against the middle and can initiate
divergence. Balancing selection actively maintains multiple alleles, through
heterozygote advantage as in the sickle-cell allele's protection against malaria,
through frequency-dependent advantage where rarity itself is beneficial, or through
variation in selection pressure across environments.

Molecular evolution provides the quantitative test. Under neutral theory most
substitutions are selectively neutral, fixed by drift rather than selection, and this
gives a null expectation against which selection can be detected. The ratio of
nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rates, dN/dS, is the standard measure: a ratio
near one indicates neutrality, below one indicates purifying selection removing
deleterious changes (the overwhelmingly common case for functional genes), and above
one indicates positive selection driving change — found in immune genes, in
reproductive proteins, and in host-pathogen arms races.

Evidence for common descent, from independent lines:
  fossil record        transitional forms in the predicted stratigraphic order
  comparative anatomy  homologous structures with shared developmental origin;
                       vestigial organs; suboptimal designs explicable only by history
  biogeography         species distributions tracking geological history rather
                       than present-day habitat suitability
  molecular            phylogenies from independent gene families converging on the
                       same tree; the near-universal genetic code
  pseudogenes          shared broken copies of the same gene, with the same lesions,
                       in related lineages — the strongest single line of evidence
  observed evolution   antibiotic and pesticide resistance, Lenski's long-term E.
                       coli lines, seasonal influenza drift

Common misconceptions are worth stating explicitly because they persist. Evolution is
not goal-directed; there is no progress toward complexity, and parasites frequently
evolve toward radical simplification. Individuals do not evolve — populations do.
Traits do not arise because they are needed; variation arises without regard to utility
and selection filters afterward. Fitness is reproductive success in a specific
environment, not strength or longevity, and a trait that reduces individual survival can
spread if it raises reproductive output. And "just a theory" misreads the word: in
science a theory is an explanatory framework supported by evidence, not a guess, and
evolution is simultaneously an observed fact (allele frequencies demonstrably change)
and a theory explaining it.
