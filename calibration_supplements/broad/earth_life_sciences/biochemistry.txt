## Biomolecules and Water

Biochemistry is the chemistry of living systems, and nearly all of it occurs in water, which
makes water's properties the starting point rather than background. Water is a small bent
molecule with a substantial dipole, hydrogen bonds extensively, and has anomalously high melting
and boiling points, high heat capacity, high surface tension, and lower density as a solid than
as a liquid — all consequences of that hydrogen bonding network.

The hydrophobic effect follows from water's structure and is the dominant organizing force in
biology. A nonpolar molecule placed in water cannot hydrogen bond with it, so the surrounding
water molecules adopt more ordered arrangements to preserve their own bonding, which lowers
their entropy. Aggregating the nonpolar molecules reduces the total surface requiring this
ordering and releases the constrained water, raising entropy. The effect is therefore
entropy-driven rather than an attraction between nonpolar groups, and it is what folds proteins,
assembles membranes, and drives most molecular recognition.

The four major classes of biomolecule are built by polymerizing a modest set of monomers.
Proteins are polymers of twenty amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Nucleic acids are polymers
of four nucleotides joined by phosphodiester bonds. Carbohydrates are polymers of sugars joined
by glycosidic bonds, and their branching capacity gives them far more structural diversity per
unit length than the linear polymers. Lipids are not polymers at all but are grouped by
solubility, and their defining property is amphipathicity — a polar head and nonpolar tails —
which is what makes them assemble into bilayers.

Biomolecules and water:
  hydrogen bonding     water's dominant property; underlies nearly all anomalies
  hydrophobic effect   entropy-driven aggregation of nonpolar groups; the primary
                       folding and assembly force
  amphipathic          both polar and nonpolar regions; lipids and detergents
  pH and pKa           the ionization state of a group depends on the pH relative
                       to its pKa; charge determines interaction
  buffering            weak acid plus conjugate base; blood bicarbonate coupled to
                       respiration
  amino acids          twenty; grouped by side chain as nonpolar, polar, acidic,
                       basic
  peptide bond         planar and rigid from resonance; restricts the backbone to
                       two rotatable angles per residue
  nucleotides          base, sugar, phosphate; four in DNA and four in RNA
  carbohydrates        branching gives high information density per unit length
  lipids               classed by solubility rather than structure; fatty acids,
                       phospholipids, sterols

  hydrolysis           polymer breakdown consumes water

The peptide bond's rigidity is worth stating because it determines protein structure. Resonance
delocalizes electrons across the carbonyl and the nitrogen, giving the bond partial double
character, so the six atoms of the peptide unit are coplanar and rotation about it is
restricted. Each residue therefore contributes only two rotatable backbone angles, which reduces
the conformational space enormously and is what makes the folding problem finite rather than
hopeless.

Q: A nonpolar solute aggregates in water. The thermodynamic driver is:
A. Attraction between the nonpolar molecules
B. Entropy gain from releasing water molecules that were constrained around the nonpolar
   surfaces
C. Enthalpy of the new bonds formed
D. Reduced pressure
Reasoning: Nonpolar molecules interact only weakly through dispersion forces, which does not
account for the effect's magnitude. Water surrounding a nonpolar surface adopts more ordered
arrangements to maintain its hydrogen bonding, and aggregation reduces the total constrained
surface, releasing those water molecules and increasing entropy. The hydrophobic effect is
entropic. Answer: B.

## Protein Structure and Function

Protein structure is described at four levels, and the central claim is that the sequence
determines the structure, which determines the function. Primary structure is the amino acid
sequence. Secondary structure comprises local regular patterns stabilized by backbone hydrogen
bonds — the alpha helix and the beta sheet, both proposed on structural grounds before being
observed. Tertiary structure is the three-dimensional fold of a single chain, stabilized
predominantly by the hydrophobic effect burying nonpolar residues in the interior. Quaternary
structure is the assembly of several chains.

The folding problem — predicting structure from sequence — was among biology's longest-standing
open questions and has recently been substantially solved computationally for many proteins by
deep learning methods trained on the accumulated structural database. This is a prediction
capability rather than a mechanistic understanding: the models predict structures accurately and
do not explain the folding process, and cases with few homologous sequences, intrinsically
disordered regions, and conformational change on binding remain difficult.

Levinthal's paradox frames why folding must be directed. A chain of a hundred residues with even
a few conformations per residue has astronomically many possible structures, so random search
would take longer than the universe's age, and yet proteins fold in milliseconds to seconds. The
resolution is that the energy landscape is funnelled: partially correct structures are
progressively lower in energy, so the search is guided downhill rather than random, and folding
proceeds through intermediates rather than by enumeration.

Protein structure:
  primary              the sequence
  secondary            alpha helix, beta sheet; backbone hydrogen bonds
  tertiary             the three-dimensional fold; hydrophobic core
  quaternary           multiple chains assembled
  domain               an independently folding unit; the module of protein
                       evolution
  motif                a recurring small structural pattern
  disulfide bond       covalent cross-link; stabilizes extracellular proteins
                       where the environment is oxidizing
  chaperone            assists folding and prevents aggregation; the crowded
                       cellular interior makes misfolding likely without them
  denaturation         loss of structure from heat, pH, or chaotropes; frequently

  Levinthal's paradox  random search is impossible; the landscape is funnelled
  intrinsically disordered  no fixed structure; common in regulatory proteins and
                       overlooked for decades because it defied the
                       structure-function paradigm
  allostery            binding at one site changes conformation and activity at
                       another; the fundamental mechanism of biological regulation
  induced fit          the binding site adjusts on binding; more accurate than the
                       rigid lock-and-key picture

Allostery deserves emphasis because it is how biological regulation works at the molecular
level. A molecule binding at one site alters the protein's conformation and thereby its activity
at a distant site, which permits activity to be controlled by signals unrelated to the substrate.
Haemoglobin is the classical example, where oxygen binding at one subunit increases the affinity
of the others, producing the sigmoid binding curve that makes oxygen delivery efficient. Nearly
every regulated enzyme and receptor operates by the same principle, and allosteric sites are
increasingly the target of drug development because they permit modulation rather than the
blunt inhibition that active-site binding produces.

## Enzymes

Enzymes are catalysts, accelerating reactions by factors of a million to a trillion without
being consumed and without altering the equilibrium. That last point is worth stating precisely:
a catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward and reverse reactions equally, so it
accelerates the approach to equilibrium and leaves the equilibrium position unchanged. An enzyme
cannot make a thermodynamically unfavourable reaction favourable; it can only make a favourable
one fast.

The catalytic mechanism is stabilization of the transition state. The active site binds the
transition state more tightly than the substrate, which lowers the activation barrier, and this
framing explains several observations: transition state analogues are extremely potent
inhibitors, catalytic antibodies can be raised against transition state analogues, and enzymes
that bind their substrate too tightly are poor catalysts because they stabilize the ground state
rather than the transition state.

Michaelis-Menten kinetics describes the standard behaviour. Rate rises with substrate
concentration and saturates as the enzyme becomes fully occupied, giving a hyperbolic curve. The
maximum rate reflects the enzyme's turnover and concentration, and the Michaelis constant is the
substrate concentration giving half the maximum rate, which serves as an inverse measure of
apparent affinity. The ratio of turnover number to Michaelis constant is the specificity
constant, which measures catalytic efficiency and is bounded above by the diffusion limit —
several enzymes operate at that limit, meaning they convert essentially every substrate molecule
that encounters them.

  catalysis            lowers activation energy; does not change the equilibrium
  transition state     the enzyme stabilizes it, which is the mechanism
  active site          the binding and catalytic region; a small part of the
                       protein
  specificity          from complementary shape, charge, and hydrogen bonding
  Michaelis constant   substrate concentration at half maximal rate; an inverse
                       affinity measure
  turnover number      reactions per enzyme per second
  specificity constant  catalytic efficiency; bounded by diffusion
  cofactor             a non-protein component required for activity; metals and

  competitive inhibition  competes for the active site; raises the apparent
                       Michaelis constant, and can be overcome by more substrate
  non-competitive      binds elsewhere; lowers the maximum rate, and is not
                       overcome by more substrate
  irreversible         covalent modification; aspirin acetylating cyclooxygenase
  allosteric regulation  effectors binding remote sites; produces sigmoid kinetics

Feedback inhibition is the control architecture worth recognizing because it recurs throughout
metabolism. The end product of a pathway inhibits the enzyme catalyzing the first committed step,
so production is throttled when the product accumulates and resumes when it is consumed. Placing
the control at the first committed step avoids accumulating intermediates that have no other use,
and using the end product as the signal means the pathway responds to demand rather than to
supply. This is negative feedback with a well-chosen sensor and actuator, and it is why metabolic
concentrations are far more stable than the fluxes through them.

## Metabolism and Bioenergetics

Metabolism is the totality of chemical reactions in a cell, divided into catabolism, which
breaks molecules down and releases energy, and anabolism, which builds them up and consumes it.
The two are coupled through shared energy carriers, of which ATP is the central one — catabolism
regenerates it and anabolism spends it, and the cell maintains it far from equilibrium so that
its hydrolysis can drive unfavourable reactions.

Coupling is the mechanism making thermodynamically unfavourable reactions proceed. An
unfavourable reaction is coupled to a favourable one through a shared intermediate, so the
combined process has a negative free energy change. This is why ATP hydrolysis appears throughout
biosynthesis, and why the relevant quantity is not ATP's standard free energy but the actual
free energy under cellular conditions, which is substantially larger because the cell maintains
the ratio of ATP to its products far above equilibrium.

Glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation form the central catabolic
pathway. Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate in the cytoplasm without oxygen, yielding a small
amount of ATP directly and reduced electron carriers. The citric acid cycle oxidizes the pyruvate
derivative completely to carbon dioxide, yielding more reduced carriers. Oxidative
phosphorylation transfers electrons from those carriers through a membrane-embedded chain to
oxygen, pumping protons across the membrane, and the resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis.

Chemiosmosis deserves emphasis both for its mechanism and for its history. The proposal that
energy from electron transport is stored as a proton gradient across a membrane, rather than as a
chemical intermediate, was resisted for years because the field expected a conventional
high-energy compound. It is now recognized as one of biology's central mechanisms, operating in
mitochondria, chloroplasts, and bacteria alike, and ATP synthase is a rotary motor — protons
flowing down the gradient turn a rotor, whose rotation drives conformational changes that
synthesize ATP. That a molecular machine works by rotation was itself unexpected and has been
directly visualized.

## Membranes, Signalling, and Methods

Membranes are lipid bilayers with embedded proteins, and their structure follows directly from
the amphipathicity of phospholipids: in water they spontaneously arrange with polar heads outward
and nonpolar tails inward, forming a bilayer that is self-sealing because an edge would expose
tails to water. The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid in which lipids and proteins diffuse
laterally, and its permeability is selective — small nonpolar molecules cross readily, while ions
and polar molecules require transport proteins.

Transport divides by whether it requires energy. Passive transport moves substances down their
electrochemical gradient, either directly through the bilayer or through channels and carriers
that provide a path. Active transport moves them against the gradient and requires energy, either
directly from ATP or indirectly by coupling to another substance's favourable gradient. The
sodium-potassium pump is the archetype and consumes a substantial fraction of a cell's energy
budget, maintaining the gradients that then power secondary transport and electrical signalling.

Signal transduction converts an extracellular signal into an intracellular response, and the
architecture is consistent across systems. A receptor binds the signal, a transduction cascade
relays and amplifies it, and effectors produce the response. Amplification is the reason for the
cascade: one receptor activates many transducers, each of which activates many enzymes, so a few
signal molecules produce a large response. The cost is that the pathway has many points at which
regulation and dysfunction can occur, which is why signalling proteins are so heavily represented
among oncogenes.

Cryo-electron microscopy deserves the closing note because it changed what is structurally
accessible. X-ray crystallography requires the protein to form a well-ordered crystal, which many
proteins — particularly membrane proteins and large flexible complexes — do not do, so a large
fraction of the proteome was structurally inaccessible for decades. Cryo-EM images individual
particles in vitrified solution and computationally averages many orientations into a
three-dimensional reconstruction, requiring no crystal. Improvements in detectors and image
processing brought its resolution to near-atomic, and the consequence is that structures of
membrane receptors, ribosomes, viruses, and large machines that had resisted crystallography for
decades became routine within a few years.
