## Selection, Drift, and the Mechanisms of Change

Evolution is change in the heritable characteristics of populations across generations, and it
requires only three conditions: variation among individuals, heritability of that variation, and
differential reproduction associated with it. Where those three hold, the composition of the
population changes, and the argument is close to a logical necessity rather than an empirical
claim about biology specifically.

Natural selection is the process arising when the differential reproduction is caused by the
variation. It is not the only mechanism of evolutionary change and it is frequently not the
dominant one. Genetic drift — the random sampling of which individuals happen to reproduce — changes
allele frequencies in any finite population, and its magnitude is inversely proportional to
population size. In a small population drift overwhelms weak selection entirely, which means that a
mildly advantageous variant can be lost and a mildly deleterious one can fix, purely by chance.

The relationship between the two is quantified by comparing the selection coefficient with the
reciprocal of the effective population size. When selection is much stronger, the outcome is
deterministic; when it is much weaker, the variant behaves as though neutral. This threshold is why
effective population size is one of the most consequential quantities in evolutionary biology, and
why species with small populations accumulate slightly deleterious mutations that large populations
purge.

  the three conditions  variation, heritability, differential reproduction
  natural selection    differential reproduction caused by the variation
  genetic drift        random sampling; magnitude scales inversely with
                       population size
  the threshold        selection dominates when its coefficient exceeds one over
                       the effective population size
  effective population size  the size of an idealized population with the same
                       drift; usually far smaller than the census count
  mutation             the ultimate source of variation; mostly neutral or

  recombination        reshuffles combinations; decouples linked sites
  gene flow            migration between populations; homogenizes them

Q: A slightly deleterious mutation becomes fixed in a population. The most likely explanation is:
A. The mutation was actually beneficial
B. The population was small enough that drift dominated weak selection, so the allele's fate was
   effectively random despite its mild disadvantage
C. Selection does not act on mutations
D. The measurement is wrong
Reasoning: Selection is effective only when its strength exceeds the magnitude of drift, which
scales inversely with effective population size. In a small population, mutations with small
fitness effects behave as though neutral and can fix by chance. This is a routine outcome, not an
anomaly. Answer: B.

## Population Genetics

Population genetics is the quantitative core of evolutionary theory, and it began by establishing
what happens in the absence of evolution. The Hardy-Weinberg principle states that under random
mating, no selection, no drift, no mutation, and no migration, allele and genotype frequencies
remain constant. Its value is as a null model: a population departing from Hardy-Weinberg
proportions is telling you that one of those assumptions fails, and which direction the departure
runs narrows down which.

The dynamics of a single locus under selection are tractable and instructive. A beneficial dominant
allele rises quickly at first and then slowly, because when it is common the recessive homozygotes
that selection acts against are rare. A beneficial recessive allele does the opposite, lingering at
low frequency for a long time because it is almost always masked in heterozygotes. This asymmetry
means that the rate of adaptation depends on dominance as much as on the strength of selection.

Linkage complicates the single-locus picture in ways that matter for interpretation. Sites near a
selected variant are dragged along with it, a process called genetic hitchhiking, which reduces
diversity in the surrounding region and produces a signature called a selective sweep. Detecting
these signatures in genome data is how selection is inferred in species that cannot be
experimented on, and distinguishing them from the diversity reductions produced by demographic
history — a bottleneck reduces diversity genome-wide — is the central inferential difficulty.

## Speciation and Phylogenetics

A species is a population or set of populations that is reproductively isolated from others, under
the most widely used definition, and speciation is the process by which that isolation arises. The
definition has well-known difficulties — it does not apply to asexual organisms, it is untestable
for fossils, and hybridization between recognized species is common — which is why several
alternative species concepts coexist and why the boundary is often genuinely fuzzy rather than
merely uncertain.

Geographic separation is the most straightforward route. Populations divided by a barrier accumulate
differences independently through drift and divergent selection, and if they remain separated long
enough, reproductive incompatibilities arise as a byproduct. When secondary contact occurs,
selection against unfit hybrids can then strengthen prezygotic isolation directly, a process called
reinforcement. Speciation without geographic separation is harder because gene flow opposes
divergence, but it occurs — through host shifts, polyploidy in plants, and strong disruptive
selection with assortative mating.

Phylogenetics reconstructs the branching history relating organisms. Characters shared through
common ancestry are informative; characters shared through convergent evolution are misleading, and
distinguishing them is the core methodological problem. Molecular data made the field quantitative
because sequences supply many independent characters and because models of substitution allow
likelihood-based inference rather than counting.

## Molecular Evolution and Evolutionary Development

The neutral theory of molecular evolution asserts that most substitutions between species are
neither advantageous nor deleterious but effectively neutral, fixed by drift. It was controversial
when proposed and is now the standard null model, which is a substantial shift: the question asked
of any observed sequence difference is no longer whether it is adaptive but whether it departs
detectably from neutral expectation.

The tests for that departure are elegant. Comparing the rate of substitutions that change an amino
acid with the rate of those that do not gives a ratio whose expected value under neutrality is one;
values below indicate purifying selection removing changes, and values above indicate positive
selection driving them. Comparing polymorphism within a species with divergence between species
gives an independent test, because selection affects the two differently. Both are applied
routinely to genome data and both are sensitive to demography in ways that require care.

Evolutionary developmental biology addresses how genetic change produces morphological change, and
its central finding is that the same small set of regulatory genes patterns the body plans of
animals as different as flies and vertebrates. Morphological diversity arises far more from changes
in when and where those genes are expressed than from changes in the genes themselves. This
resolves an old puzzle — how such varied forms arise from such conserved genomes — and it relocates
the action from protein-coding sequence to regulatory sequence.

## Evidence and Inference

The evidence for common descent is convergent from independent lines, and the convergence is what
makes it robust rather than any single line being conclusive. The fossil record documents forms
intermediate between major groups. Comparative anatomy shows shared structures repurposed across
lineages, including structures that are functionally poor and make sense only as inherited
constraints. Biogeography shows distributions that track geological history. Molecular sequences
produce a phylogeny that matches the one derived from morphology, from entirely independent data.

Vestigial structures and molecular pseudogenes are particularly informative because they are
evidence of history rather than of function. A non-functional copy of a gene that works in related
species, disabled by an identifiable mutation shared with the nearest relatives, records both the
ancestry and the loss. The shared inactivating mutation is the key detail — an independently
disabled gene would carry a different lesion.

Evolution is observable on human timescales where generation times are short and selection is
strong. Antibiotic resistance, pesticide resistance, herbicide resistance, and viral immune escape
are all evolution occurring in real time under selection pressures we apply, and they are also
the practical reason the theory matters outside biology: resistance management is applied
evolutionary biology, and strategies that ignore it fail predictably.
