## The Water Cycle and Water Budgets

Hydrology follows water through its reservoirs and pathways, and the organizing tool is the water
balance: over any defined area and period, inflow equals outflow plus the change in storage. Applied
to a catchment, the inflows are precipitation and any imports, the outflows are evapotranspiration,
streamflow, and groundwater exiting the boundary, and the storage terms are soil moisture,
groundwater, snowpack, and surface water bodies.

The balance is simple to state and difficult to close in practice, because the terms are measured
with very different accuracy. Precipitation is measured at points and interpolated over areas with
substantial error, particularly for snow and in complex terrain. Streamflow is measured relatively
well at gauges through a stage-discharge relationship that must be recalibrated as the channel
changes. Evapotranspiration is rarely measured directly and is usually estimated from meteorological
variables. Groundwater exchange is the least constrained term and frequently absorbs the residual
error of everything else.

Evapotranspiration deserves particular attention because it is the largest outflow in most
catchments and the least intuitive. It combines evaporation from surfaces and transpiration through
plants, and it is limited either by available energy or by available water depending on conditions.
In humid regions energy is limiting, so it tracks radiation; in arid regions water is limiting, so
it tracks supply. Vegetation change therefore alters catchment yield substantially, which is why
afforestation reduces streamflow and why land use change is a hydrological intervention whether or
not it is intended as one.

Q: A catchment is reforested and downstream streamflow decreases. Why?
A. The measurement changed
B. Trees transpire substantially more than the previous cover and intercept more precipitation
   before it reaches the ground, so a larger fraction of the water balance leaves as
   evapotranspiration and less as streamflow
C. The rainfall decreased
D. Water is stored in the wood
Reasoning: Evapotranspiration is the dominant outflow, and it depends strongly on vegetation type
through rooting depth, leaf area, and canopy interception. Deeper-rooted, higher-leaf-area cover
increases it. This is a well-documented result from paired catchment studies and it is a real
tradeoff against the other benefits of reforestation. Answer: B.

## Precipitation, Runoff, and Catchment Response

When precipitation falls on a catchment, it partitions into interception by vegetation, infiltration
into the soil, storage in depressions, and overland flow. The partition depends on rainfall
intensity relative to the soil's infiltration capacity, on antecedent moisture, on slope, and on
land cover, and it determines how much of a storm becomes streamflow and how quickly.

The hydrograph — discharge plotted against time following a storm — has a characteristic shape whose
features are diagnostic. A rapid rise and high peak indicates fast pathways: impervious surfaces,
saturated soils, steep slopes, or efficient drainage. A slow broad response indicates infiltration
and subsurface pathways. The recession after the peak reflects drainage of storage and its shape
describes the catchment's storage-discharge relationship, which is what sustains flow between
storms.

Urbanization changes this dramatically and predictably. Impervious surfaces prevent infiltration and
storm drains deliver water to channels far faster than overland and subsurface routes, so peaks
become higher and earlier and baseflow between storms declines because the groundwater is no longer
recharged. Both effects are damaging, and the design response has shifted from conveying water away
as fast as possible toward retaining and infiltrating it near where it falls, which addresses the
peak and the recharge simultaneously.

## Groundwater

Groundwater occupies the pore spaces and fractures of subsurface materials, and it holds far more
fresh water than all rivers and lakes combined. An aquifer is a formation transmitting useful
quantities, and its behaviour depends on two properties that are frequently confused: porosity, the
fraction of void space, which determines how much water is stored, and hydraulic conductivity, the
ease of flow, which determines how fast it moves. Clay has high porosity and very low conductivity —
it holds much water and yields little.

Flow is driven by hydraulic head gradients and is described by a linear relationship between flow
rate and gradient. The velocities are slow — typically metres per year to metres per day — which has
two important consequences. Contamination moves slowly and persists for decades, so an aquifer
contaminated today remains so long after the source is removed. And groundwater systems respond to
changes with long lags, so the effect of a pumping regime or a recharge change may not appear for
years and continues after the cause stops.

Unconfined aquifers have a water table as their upper surface and are recharged directly from above,
which makes them accessible and vulnerable to surface contamination. Confined aquifers lie beneath a
low-conductivity layer, are recharged where that layer is absent, and are under pressure — a well
penetrating one has water rising above the aquifer top and occasionally to the surface. Sustained
extraction exceeding recharge depletes storage, and in some settings causes irreversible compaction
of the formation, permanently destroying the storage capacity and producing land subsidence at the
surface.

## Floods, Droughts, and Extremes

Flood frequency analysis fits a distribution to historical peak flows and estimates the magnitude
associated with a given exceedance probability. The common terminology invites a specific
misunderstanding: a hundred-year flood is one with a one percent annual exceedance probability, not
one that occurs once per century, and two such events in consecutive years are unremarkable rather
than evidence of a broken model.

The analysis rests on assumptions that are increasingly questionable. Stationarity — that the
statistical properties of the record apply to the future — is violated by land use change, by
reservoir operation, and by climate trends. Independence between annual peaks is generally
reasonable. Sufficient record length is frequently not satisfied, since estimating a one percent
event from thirty years of data extrapolates well beyond the data and the resulting confidence
intervals are wide enough that the point estimate should never be quoted alone.

Drought is harder to define than flood because it is a deficit relative to expectation rather than an
absolute condition, and the relevant deficit depends on the use. Meteorological drought is a
precipitation shortfall; agricultural drought is soil moisture insufficient for crops; hydrological
drought is low streamflow and storage; and socioeconomic drought is demand exceeding supply. They
develop and end at different times, which is why a region can be out of meteorological drought while
its reservoirs remain depleted, and why declaring drought over requires specifying which one.

## Water Quality, Management, and Infrastructure

Water quality is characterized by physical, chemical, and biological parameters, and the relevant set
depends on the use. Drinking water is concerned with pathogens first, then with chemical
contaminants and disinfection byproducts. Aquatic ecosystems are concerned with dissolved oxygen,
temperature, nutrients, and toxicity. Irrigation is concerned with salinity and specific ions.
Assessing quality without specifying the use produces a list of numbers rather than a judgement.

Point source pollution enters from an identifiable discharge and is comparatively straightforward to
regulate and treat. Non-point source pollution — agricultural runoff, urban stormwater, atmospheric
deposition — enters diffusely across a landscape and is the dominant remaining problem in most
developed watersheds, precisely because there is no pipe to permit and no facility to upgrade.
Addressing it requires changing practices across many landowners, which is a governance problem more
than a technical one.

Water management allocates a variable supply among competing uses, and the institutional arrangements
matter as much as the physical infrastructure. Allocation systems that grant fixed quantities perform
badly under variability; systems that grant proportional shares of an assessed availability adapt
better. Environmental flows — water allocated to the ecosystem rather than to extraction — are
increasingly recognized as a legitimate allocation rather than as water left over, and integrating
them requires deciding what the river is for, which is a value question that hydrology can inform and
cannot answer.
