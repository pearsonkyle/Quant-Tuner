## Population Ecology

Population ecology studies how numbers of organisms change, and the two baseline models
frame everything that follows. Exponential growth occurs when per-capita birth and death rates
are constant, producing growth proportional to current size and therefore unbounded increase.
It describes real populations only briefly — during colonization of new habitat, or recovery
from a crash — because resources are finite.

Logistic growth introduces a carrying capacity at which growth stops, with the per-capita
rate declining linearly as the population approaches it. This produces the sigmoid curve
familiar from textbooks and is a substantial idealization: carrying capacity is not a fixed
property of an environment but varies with conditions, resource availability, and the
population's own effects on its habitat. Its value is as a null model against which real
dynamics are compared, not as a description.

Real populations exhibit dynamics the simple models do not predict. Time lags between
population size and its regulatory effect produce oscillations and overshoot, because the
population continues growing past carrying capacity before the feedback takes effect. Discrete
generations with strong density dependence produce cycles and, at high growth rates, chaos —
the logistic map's period-doubling route to chaos was discovered in exactly this ecological
context. Stochasticity matters disproportionately at small population size, where random
variation in births and deaths can drive extinction regardless of average conditions, which
is the demographic basis for minimum viable population sizes.

Population concepts:
  exponential growth     dN/dt = rN;  unbounded, brief in practice
  logistic growth        dN/dt = rN(1 - N/K);  sigmoid, carrying capacity K
  intrinsic rate r       maximum per-capita growth under ideal conditions
  carrying capacity K    variable, not a fixed environmental constant
  density dependence     regulation through competition, disease, predation
  density independence   weather, disturbance; affects rate regardless of density
  r-selected             high fecundity, low survival, early reproduction; favoured
                         in variable or disturbed environments
  K-selected             low fecundity, high investment, late reproduction;
                         favoured in stable saturated environments
  life table             age-specific survival and fecundity; the demographic core
  metapopulation         subpopulations linked by dispersal; local extinction and
                         recolonization
  source-sink            productive patches exporting individuals to patches where
                         mortality exceeds reproduction
  Allee effect           reduced per-capita growth at low density; mate-finding,
                         group defence, cooperative foraging
  minimum viable pop.    the size below which stochastic extinction becomes likely

The Allee effect deserves emphasis because it inverts the usual expectation and has direct
conservation consequences. Standard density dependence means small populations grow fastest,
which would make recovery from low numbers easy. Where individuals depend on each other — for
finding mates, for cooperative hunting, for predator dilution, for maintaining a modified
habitat — per-capita growth falls at low density instead, creating a threshold below which the
population declines to extinction regardless of protection. The passenger pigeon's collapse
from billions to zero is the standard example, and it explains why some species fail to
recover after harvesting stops.

Q: A conservation programme protects a species whose population has fallen to a few dozen
individuals, and the population continues to decline despite complete protection from its
former threat. The most likely ecological explanation is:
A. The carrying capacity fell
B. An Allee effect, in which per-capita growth is reduced at very low density
C. Exponential decay
D. Density dependence
Reasoning: Removing the original threat should permit growth under ordinary density
dependence, since small populations face least competition. Continued decline suggests
per-capita growth is negative at low density, which is the Allee effect — from difficulty
finding mates, loss of cooperative behaviours, or inbreeding depression. Answer: B.

## Species Interactions

Interactions between species are classified by their effect on each participant, and the
classification is useful because the dynamics differ systematically. Competition harms both.
Predation, parasitism, and herbivory benefit one at the other's expense. Mutualism benefits
both. Commensalism benefits one and is neutral for the other, and genuine examples are rarer
than casual classification suggests, since most apparently neutral relationships turn out to
have small costs or benefits when measured.

The competitive exclusion principle states that two species competing for exactly the same
limiting resource cannot coexist indefinitely — the better competitor excludes the other. Its
apparent contradiction by the enormous diversity of coexisting species is resolved by niche
differentiation: species partition resources by type, by space, by time, or by size, so they
are not in fact competing for an identical resource. Character displacement, where
coexisting species diverge in traits more than allopatric populations do, is the evolutionary
signature of this partitioning.

Predator-prey dynamics generate cycles under simple assumptions, with the predator population
lagging the prey by a quarter cycle. The classic lynx and hare data from fur trading records
show this pattern, though the interpretation is complicated by the fact that the hare cycle
appears in areas without lynx, indicating that vegetation and other factors contribute. This
is a general caution about the field: the mechanism that a simple model attributes a pattern
to is frequently one among several, and demonstrating which operates requires manipulation
rather than correlation.

Interaction types and dynamics:
  competition            -/-;  interference or exploitation
  competitive exclusion  identical niches cannot coexist stably
  niche partitioning     resource, spatial, temporal differentiation
  character displacement evolutionary divergence where species co-occur
  predation              +/-;  can generate cycles with a lag
  functional response    predation rate versus prey density; saturating, and the
                         sigmoid type can stabilize prey at low density
  parasitism             +/-;  the parasite generally does not kill the host
  parasitoid             kills the host; important in biological control
  mutualism              +/+;  pollination, mycorrhizae, gut microbiota
  obligate vs facultative  whether the partner is required
  cheating               mutualisms are exploitable and require enforcement or
                         partner choice to persist
  keystone species       an effect on the community disproportionate to abundance
  ecosystem engineer     modifies the physical habitat; beavers, corals, trees
  trophic cascade        predator effects propagating down through multiple levels

Keystone species and trophic cascades are the ideas with the widest reach outside ecology.
Removing a top predator releases its prey, which then over-exploits the next level down,
altering the community structure far beyond the direct interaction. Sea otter removal allowed
sea urchin populations to explode and eliminate kelp forests; wolf reintroduction in
Yellowstone altered elk browsing behaviour and, through that, riparian vegetation and stream
morphology. The size of these effects has sometimes been overstated in popular accounts,
which attribute to a single reintroduction changes that had multiple causes, and the general
phenomenon of top-down regulation is nonetheless well established.

## Communities and Ecosystems

A community is the set of interacting species in an area, and its structure is described by
species richness — the count — and evenness — how equally abundance is distributed. Diversity
indices combine both, and the choice among them matters because they weight rarity
differently: richness counts every species equally regardless of abundance, Shannon diversity
weights by log abundance, and Simpson diversity is dominated by the common species.
Conclusions about whether diversity increased or decreased can depend on which index is used.

Species-area relationships hold with remarkable generality: the number of species increases
with area following a power law with an exponent typically between 0.15 and 0.35. The
practical consequence is the basis of much conservation planning — reducing habitat area to
ten percent of its original extent is expected to retain roughly half of the species, not ten
percent. Island biogeography theory explains the pattern as a balance between immigration,
which decreases with isolation, and extinction, which decreases with area, and the theory
applies to habitat fragments as well as literal islands.

Ecosystems add the abiotic environment and focus on energy flow and material cycling. The
foundational asymmetry is that energy flows through while matter cycles: energy enters as
sunlight, is captured by primary producers, passes up trophic levels with large losses, and
leaves as heat, never to be reused. Matter, by contrast, is repeatedly recycled through
organisms, soil, water, and atmosphere.

Community and ecosystem structure:
  species richness       the count of species
  evenness               how equally abundance is distributed
  Shannon / Simpson      diversity indices weighting rarity differently
  species-area curve     S = cA^z, z typically 0.15-0.35
  island biogeography    immigration versus extinction; area and isolation
  trophic level          producer, primary consumer, secondary, tertiary
  ecological efficiency  ~10% of energy transferred per level, with wide variation
  food chain length      limited by energy loss; rarely exceeds four or five levels
  food web               the realistic network; most consumers feed at several levels
  primary production     GPP is total photosynthesis, NPP is GPP minus plant
                         respiration; NPP is what supports everything above
  biomass pyramid        can be inverted in aquatic systems, where fast-turnover
                         phytoplankton support a larger standing crop of consumers
  decomposers            return nutrients; without them, nutrients would be locked
                         in dead biomass and productivity would cease

The roughly ten percent transfer efficiency between trophic levels is the quantity with the
broadest consequences. Most energy at each level is lost to respiration, to unconsumed
biomass, and to indigestible material, so the energy available to support each successive
level falls by an order of magnitude. This limits food chain length, explains why top
predators are rare and require large territories, and underlies the arithmetic of human diet:
producing a given quantity of animal protein requires roughly an order of magnitude more
primary production than producing the equivalent plant protein, which is why land use and
agricultural emissions depend so strongly on dietary composition.

## Biogeochemical Cycles

Nutrients cycle between organisms and the environment, and the cycles differ in whether they
have a substantial atmospheric phase, which determines how quickly they equilibrate globally
and how susceptible they are to human perturbation.

The carbon cycle exchanges enormous fluxes between atmosphere, ocean, land biosphere, and
soils on annual timescales, and much smaller fluxes with rocks and fossil deposits over
geological time. Human extraction and combustion of fossil carbon transfers material from the
slow reservoir into the fast one at a rate the fast cycle cannot absorb, which is why
atmospheric concentration rises despite the ocean and land taking up roughly half the
emissions.

The nitrogen cycle is limited by a chemical barrier: atmospheric nitrogen is abundant and
inert, held by a triple bond that most organisms cannot break. Nitrogen fixation by bacteria,
some free-living and some symbiotic with legumes, converts it to biologically usable forms,
and this was the rate-limiting step for terrestrial productivity through most of Earth's
history. Industrial fixation by the Haber-Bosch process now roughly doubles the natural rate,
which supports a large fraction of the human population and produces the excess nitrogen that
causes eutrophication and nitrous oxide emissions.

Cycle characteristics:
  carbon           fast biological exchange, slow geological; fossil combustion
                   moves the slow reservoir into the fast one
  nitrogen         atmospheric N2 is inert; fixation is the bottleneck
  Haber-Bosch      industrial fixation roughly doubles the natural rate
  nitrification    ammonia to nitrite to nitrate, by bacteria
  denitrification  nitrate back to N2; returns nitrogen to the atmosphere
  phosphorus       no atmospheric phase; weathering to soil to organism to
                   sediment; effectively one-way on human timescales
  eutrophication   nutrient enrichment causing algal blooms, then decomposition
                   consuming oxygen, then hypoxic dead zones
  limiting nutrient  usually phosphorus in freshwater, nitrogen in marine systems
  water cycle      evaporation, transpiration, precipitation, runoff
  sulfur           volcanic and biological; acid deposition when combined with
                   fossil combustion
  residence time   how long an atom stays in a reservoir; determines how fast a
                   perturbation propagates

Phosphorus deserves particular attention because its cycle has no atmospheric phase, which
makes it fundamentally different from carbon and nitrogen. It enters ecosystems only by rock
weathering, cycles through organisms, and is eventually lost to ocean sediments where it is
unavailable until tectonic uplift returns it over millions of years. There is no biological or
industrial process that returns phosphorus from the ocean to land at scale. Phosphate rock is
therefore a genuinely finite resource on human timescales, and the fraction of mined phosphorus
that reaches the ocean through agricultural runoff is permanently lost — a resource question
distinct from the pollution question that the same runoff creates.

## Disturbance, Succession, and Conservation

Disturbance is any event that removes biomass and creates opportunity, from fire and flood to
windthrow and grazing. Succession is the sequence of community change that follows. Primary
succession begins on newly exposed substrate with no soil and proceeds slowly, since soil
formation is the rate-limiting step. Secondary succession follows a disturbance that left soil
intact and proceeds much faster, which is why an abandoned field reforests in decades while a
new lava flow takes centuries.

The intermediate disturbance hypothesis proposes that diversity peaks at intermediate
frequency and intensity of disturbance: too little allows competitive dominants to exclude
others, and too much permits only rapid colonizers. The hypothesis is intuitive, has empirical
support in some systems, and has been substantially criticized as not general — a useful case
study in the difference between a memorable idea and an established one.

The recognition that disturbance is a normal component of ecosystems rather than a departure
from a stable state changed management practice fundamentally. Fire suppression in
fire-adapted systems allows fuel accumulation that eventually produces catastrophic fires
far outside the historical range, and it eliminates the species that depend on periodic
burning. Managing for a fixed target state is generally less successful than managing for the
processes that maintain the system.

Disturbance, threats, and response:
  primary succession     on new substrate; soil formation limits the rate
  secondary succession   after disturbance with soil intact; much faster
  pioneer species        fast-colonizing, short-lived, high dispersal
  climax community       the historical framing; now understood as one of several
                         possible states rather than a fixed endpoint
  alternative stable states  a system can occupy more than one configuration under
                         identical conditions; shifts can be abrupt and hard to
                         reverse
  hysteresis             reversing the driver does not reverse the state
  resilience             capacity to absorb disturbance and retain function
  habitat loss           the leading cause of extinction, by a wide margin
  fragmentation          reduces effective area, increases edge, blocks dispersal
  invasive species       released from natural enemies; the second leading cause
  overexploitation       harvesting exceeding replacement
  climate change         range shifts, phenological mismatch, ocean acidification
  extinction debt        species committed to extinction by past habitat loss but
                         not yet gone; current diversity overstates future

Extinction debt is the concept most likely to mislead assessment of conservation status.
After habitat is reduced, the species that the remaining area cannot support do not disappear
immediately — long-lived individuals persist, populations decline gradually, and the new
equilibrium is reached over decades to centuries. A fragmented landscape therefore contains
species that are functionally already lost, and a survey showing they are present overstates
the conservation outcome. The corresponding hopeful case is that intervention during that
window can prevent the debt from being paid, which is a stronger argument for acting early
than the current species list would suggest.

Ecosystem services frame the practical case for conservation in terms that decision-makers
weigh: provisioning services such as food and timber, regulating services such as pollination,
water purification, flood control, and carbon storage, and cultural services. The framing is
useful for making otherwise invisible values explicit and it carries a genuine risk, which is
that it implies anything without a quantified service value is expendable. Both the
instrumental argument and the case that species have value independent of use are available,
and treating the economic framing as the only legitimate one concedes more than the evidence
requires.
