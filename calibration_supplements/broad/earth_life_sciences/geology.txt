## Earth Structure and Interior

Earth's internal structure is known almost entirely from seismology, because no borehole has
penetrated even the full thickness of the crust. Earthquake waves refract and reflect at
boundaries where physical properties change, and mapping those boundaries from arrival times
at stations worldwide produced the layered model.

Two classification schemes describe the same planet and are often confused. The compositional
division — crust, mantle, core — is based on chemistry. The mechanical division —
lithosphere, asthenosphere, mesosphere, outer core, inner core — is based on how the material
behaves. The lithosphere comprises the crust plus the uppermost rigid mantle and is what
breaks into tectonic plates; the asthenosphere beneath it is mantle rock close enough to
melting to flow slowly. The distinction matters because plate tectonics operates on the
mechanical layering, not the compositional one.

Seismic waves come in two types with different diagnostic value. Primary waves are
compressional, travel fastest, and pass through both solids and liquids. Secondary waves are
shear, travel slower, and cannot propagate through liquid because a fluid has no shear
strength. The existence of a shadow zone where secondary waves do not arrive is the direct
evidence that the outer core is liquid, and the refraction pattern of primary waves revealed
the solid inner core within it. This is a good example of a global structural conclusion drawn
from a simple physical property of wave propagation.

Earth's layers:
  crust               oceanic 5-10 km, basaltic, dense, young (< 200 Myr);
                      continental 30-70 km, granitic, less dense, up to 4 Gyr old
  Moho                the crust-mantle boundary, marked by a velocity jump
  upper mantle        peridotite; the asthenosphere lies within it
  transition zone     410 and 660 km discontinuities from mineral phase changes
  lower mantle        to 2890 km; high pressure phases
  outer core          liquid iron-nickel; generates the magnetic field by dynamo
  inner core          solid iron despite higher temperature, because pressure
                      raises the melting point
  geothermal gradient ~25 C/km in the crust, far lower in the mantle
  heat sources        radioactive decay of U, Th, K plus primordial heat
  magnetic field      requires a liquid conducting layer in convective motion

The geodynamo deserves note because it links the deep interior to the surface environment.
Convection in the liquid outer core, organized by Earth's rotation, sustains a self-exciting
magnetic field that deflects the solar wind and protects the atmosphere from stripping. The
field reverses polarity at irregular intervals averaging a few hundred thousand years, and the
record of those reversals frozen into cooling oceanic crust produced the symmetric magnetic
stripes on either side of mid-ocean ridges — which was the decisive evidence for seafloor
spreading and hence for plate tectonics.

Q: Secondary seismic waves do not arrive in a shadow zone on the far side of Earth from an
earthquake. This establishes that:
A. The core is denser than the mantle
B. Some layer is liquid, since shear waves cannot propagate through fluid
C. The Earth is layered
D. The inner core is solid
Reasoning: Shear waves require a medium with shear strength, which liquids lack. Their
complete absence beyond a certain angular distance indicates a liquid layer blocking them —
the outer core. The solidity of the inner core was inferred separately from refracted primary
wave arrivals. Answer: B.

## Plate Tectonics

Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of geology, explaining the distribution of
earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges, and the shapes and geological correspondences of
continents. The lithosphere is divided into rigid plates that move relative to one another at
rates of centimetres per year, driven primarily by the negative buoyancy of cold subducting
slabs pulling the trailing plate — slab pull — with ridge push from the elevated
mid-ocean ridge contributing less, and mantle convection providing the underlying circulation.

Continental drift was proposed by Wegener in 1912 on the basis of fitting coastlines, matching
fossil assemblages across oceans, and correlated rock formations, and it was rejected for
decades because no plausible mechanism existed for moving continents through oceanic crust.
The resolution came from seafloor spreading: the continents do not plough through the ocean
floor, they ride on plates that include it, and new oceanic crust is created at ridges and
destroyed at trenches. The history of the idea is a useful case of correct observation with an
unacceptable mechanism, resolved only when the mechanism was found.

Boundary types determine the geology:
  divergent           plates separate; mid-ocean ridges and continental rifts;
                      shallow earthquakes, basaltic volcanism, new crust created
  convergent, ocean-continent  oceanic subducts beneath continental; deep trench,
                      volcanic arc, deep earthquakes along the Benioff zone
  convergent, ocean-ocean      island arc; the older, denser plate subducts
  convergent, continent-continent  neither subducts easily; crustal thickening
                      and mountain building, as in the Himalaya
  transform           plates slide past; shallow earthquakes, little volcanism,
                      as along the San Andreas
  hotspot             a mantle plume independent of plate boundaries; produces
                      island chains as the plate moves over it
  triple junction     where three plates meet; complex and often unstable

Subduction zones are where the most consequential geology happens. The descending slab
carries water in hydrated minerals; as it heats, that water is released into the overlying
mantle wedge, lowering the melting point and generating magma that rises to form volcanic
arcs. The magma interacts with continental crust on its way up, differentiating toward more
silica-rich compositions, which is why arc volcanoes erupt explosively — silica-rich magma is
viscous and traps gas — while ridge and hotspot basalts flow effusively. The largest
earthquakes on record are all subduction megathrust events, because the fault plane is
shallow-dipping, enormous in area, and locked over long intervals.

## Minerals, Rocks, and the Rock Cycle

A mineral is a naturally occurring inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and
an ordered internal crystal structure. Rocks are aggregates of minerals. The silicate minerals
dominate the crust because silicon and oxygen are the most abundant elements available, and
their structural family is organized by how the silica tetrahedra link — isolated,
in chains, in sheets, or in three-dimensional frameworks — which determines cleavage,
hardness, and weathering behaviour.

Igneous rocks crystallize from melt. Their texture records cooling rate: slow cooling at
depth allows large crystals to grow, giving coarse-grained intrusive rocks such as granite,
while rapid cooling at the surface gives fine-grained extrusive rocks such as basalt, and
quenching gives volcanic glass. Their composition records the source and the degree of
differentiation, running from mafic, iron and magnesium rich and silica poor, to felsic,
silica and alkali rich. Bowen's reaction series describes the order in which minerals
crystallize from a cooling melt and explains why fractional crystallization drives residual
melts toward felsic compositions.

Sedimentary rocks form from accumulated particles or precipitates and are the ones that
record surface history. Clastic rocks are made of fragments, classified by grain size from
conglomerate through sandstone to shale, with the grain size and sorting recording the
transport energy and distance. Chemical and biochemical rocks precipitate from solution or
accumulate from organisms, limestone being by far the most important. Sedimentary rocks cover
most of the continental surface, contain nearly all fossils, and host most fossil fuel and
groundwater resources.

Metamorphic rocks form when existing rock is altered by heat, pressure, or chemically active
fluids without melting. Regional metamorphism at convergent boundaries produces foliated
rocks whose platy minerals align perpendicular to the maximum stress, giving the sequence
slate, phyllite, schist, gneiss with increasing grade. Contact metamorphism near an intrusion
produces non-foliated rocks because the stress is not directional. Metamorphic mineral
assemblages are used as geothermobarometers, recording the pressure and temperature the rock
experienced, which is how the burial and exhumation history of a mountain belt is
reconstructed.

Rock classification essentials:
  igneous, intrusive     granite, diorite, gabbro; coarse grained, slow cooling
  igneous, extrusive     rhyolite, andesite, basalt; fine grained, fast cooling
  composition series     felsic (granite/rhyolite) to mafic (gabbro/basalt)
  Bowen's series         olivine crystallizes first, quartz last
  clastic sedimentary    conglomerate, sandstone, siltstone, shale by grain size
  chemical sedimentary   limestone, rock salt, chert
  foliated metamorphic   slate, phyllite, schist, gneiss with increasing grade
  non-foliated           marble from limestone, quartzite from sandstone
  index minerals         indicate metamorphic grade
  rock cycle             any rock type can become any other through the
                         appropriate process; there is no fixed sequence

## Deformation, Earthquakes, and Volcanoes

Rock deforms elastically under small stress and permanently under larger stress, and whether
that permanent deformation is brittle or ductile depends on temperature, pressure, strain
rate, and rock type. Cold, shallow, rapidly stressed rock fractures; hot, deep, slowly
stressed rock flows. This is why folds and faults often occur in the same rock unit — the
folding happened at depth and the faulting after uplift, or the strain rate differed.

Faults are classified by relative motion, and each type corresponds to a stress regime and a
tectonic setting. Normal faults accommodate extension, with the hanging wall moving down, and
characterize rifts and mid-ocean ridges. Reverse and thrust faults accommodate compression
and characterize convergent margins. Strike-slip faults accommodate horizontal shear and
characterize transform boundaries. Reading the fault type from field evidence directly
constrains the stress field that produced it.

Earthquakes result from the sudden release of accumulated elastic strain when friction on a
fault is overcome. Elastic rebound theory describes the cycle: stress accumulates as plates
move while the fault remains locked, the rock deforms elastically, and when the shear stress
exceeds the frictional strength, slip occurs and the rock springs back. This explains why
large earthquakes recur at intervals on a given fault segment and why the accumulated slip
deficit is a meaningful measure of hazard.

Magnitude and intensity are distinct and routinely conflated. Magnitude is a single number
describing the energy released, computed from seismogram amplitudes, and it is logarithmic —
each unit corresponds to about 32 times more energy and ten times more ground motion
amplitude. Intensity describes shaking and damage at a particular location and varies across
the affected area with distance, depth, and local ground conditions. A moderate earthquake on
soft sediment can produce more damage than a larger one on bedrock, which is why site
response matters so much for building codes.

Earthquake and volcano quantities:
  moment magnitude      based on fault area, slip, and rock rigidity; the modern
                        standard, valid for the largest events where the older
                        Richter scale saturates
  energy scaling        ~32x per magnitude unit; ~1000x per two units
  intensity             Modified Mercalli; location-specific shaking and damage
  focus / epicentre     the rupture point at depth / the point above it
  P, S, surface waves   P arrives first, S second, surface waves cause most damage
  liquefaction          saturated loose sediment loses strength during shaking
  site amplification    soft sediment amplifies ground motion substantially
  volcanic explosivity  VEI, logarithmic in erupted volume
  magma viscosity       rises with silica content; controls eruption style
  effusive vs explosive basaltic flows versus silicic explosive eruptions
  pyroclastic flow      the deadliest volcanic hazard; fast, hot, unsurvivable
  lahar                 volcanic mudflow; often the most damaging distal hazard

Earthquake prediction — specifying time, place, and magnitude in advance — has not been
achieved and there is reasonable doubt it is possible, since the process appears to be
critically sensitive to conditions that cannot be measured. Forecasting, in the sense of
probabilistic hazard over years to decades, is well established and is what building codes
rest on. Early warning systems occupy a different niche entirely: they detect the fast P wave
at stations near the epicentre and transmit an alert electronically, which outruns the
slower and more damaging S and surface waves, providing seconds to tens of seconds — enough
to stop trains, close valves, and take cover.

## Surface Processes, Stratigraphy, and Deep Time

Weathering breaks rock down in place, and erosion transports the products. Mechanical
weathering — frost wedging, thermal expansion, root growth, abrasion — increases surface area
without changing composition. Chemical weathering — dissolution, hydrolysis, oxidation —
changes composition and is greatly accelerated by the increased surface area, which is why the
two processes reinforce each other and why weathering is fastest in warm humid climates.

The silicate weathering reaction is worth knowing outside geology because it is Earth's
long-term climate thermostat. Weathering of silicate rock consumes atmospheric carbon
dioxide, producing dissolved bicarbonate that is eventually deposited as carbonate in the
ocean. The reaction rate increases with temperature and rainfall, so a warmer world weathers
faster and draws down more carbon dioxide, cooling it; a colder world weathers slower and
allows volcanic carbon to accumulate, warming it. This negative feedback has kept Earth's
climate within habitable bounds for billions of years, and it operates over hundreds of
thousands of years — far too slowly to counteract the current rate of emission.

Stratigraphy reads Earth history from layered rock, and its principles are simple and
powerful. Original horizontality holds that sediments deposit in near-horizontal layers, so
tilted strata have been deformed. Superposition holds that in an undisturbed sequence, lower
layers are older. Cross-cutting relationships hold that a feature cutting across another is
younger. Faunal succession holds that fossil assemblages change through time in a consistent
order, which allows correlation between distant sequences and is the basis of the geological
timescale. Unconformities are surfaces representing missing time — erosion or non-deposition
— and reading them is how gaps in the record are identified rather than overlooked.

Deep time and dating:
  relative dating        superposition, cross-cutting, faunal succession,
                         unconformities
  radiometric dating     decay of unstable isotopes at a constant rate
  carbon-14              half-life 5,730 yr; organic material to ~50,000 yr
  potassium-argon        half-life 1.25 Gyr; volcanic rocks
  uranium-lead           half-life 4.47 Gyr; zircon; the most precise deep-time
                         method, and zircon resists alteration
  Earth's age            4.54 Gyr, from meteorites and the oldest terrestrial
                         minerals
  oldest rocks           ~4.0 Gyr;  oldest minerals (zircon) ~4.4 Gyr
  Precambrian            ~88% of Earth history
  Great Oxidation        ~2.4 Gyr; photosynthetic oxygen transforms the atmosphere
  Cambrian explosion     ~540 Myr; rapid diversification of animal body plans
  mass extinctions       five major events; the end-Permian removed ~80% of marine
                         species, the end-Cretaceous ended non-avian dinosaurs

Radiometric dating works because radioactive decay proceeds at a rate independent of
temperature, pressure, and chemistry, so the ratio of parent to daughter isotope in a closed
system measures elapsed time. The assumptions that must be checked are that the system
remained closed, that the initial daughter abundance is known or can be determined, and that
the appropriate isotope system is used for the timescale — carbon-14 is useless beyond about
ten half-lives, and uranium-lead is useless for anything recent. Isochron methods determine
the initial daughter abundance from the data itself rather than assuming it, which is why they
are preferred where applicable.

Economic geology connects all of this to resources. Ore deposits form where geological
processes concentrate an element far above its crustal abundance — hydrothermal circulation
depositing metals from solution, magmatic segregation concentrating dense sulfides, placer
deposits sorting heavy minerals by flowing water, and supergene enrichment concentrating
metals by weathering. Fossil fuels require organic matter accumulating under anoxic
conditions, burial to appropriate temperature, a porous reservoir rock, and an impermeable
seal, and the conjunction of all four is what makes petroleum systems localized. Groundwater
occupies pore space in aquifers, and its sustainable yield is set by recharge rate rather
than by stored volume — which is why aquifers that took millennia to fill can be depleted in
decades and why extraction exceeding recharge causes land subsidence that permanently
destroys the aquifer's storage capacity.
