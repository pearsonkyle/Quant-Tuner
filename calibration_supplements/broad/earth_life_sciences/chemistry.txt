## Atomic Structure and Periodicity

Chemistry is the behaviour of electrons. The nucleus supplies charge and mass and is
otherwise a spectator in ordinary chemical processes; everything about how an element
reacts is determined by its outermost electrons. This is why the periodic table works at
all — arranging elements by atomic number produces recurring valence configurations, and
elements in the same column share chemical behaviour because they share the arrangement
of their outer electrons.

Quantum mechanics determines those arrangements. Electrons occupy orbitals characterized
by four quantum numbers: n for the principal shell and energy, l for the subshell shape
(s, p, d, f), m_l for orientation, and m_s for spin. The Pauli exclusion principle
permits no two electrons in an atom to share all four, which caps each orbital at two
electrons of opposite spin and is ultimately why matter occupies volume at all. The
Aufbau principle fills orbitals from lowest energy upward, and Hund's rule fills
degenerate orbitals singly before pairing, because electrons repel and prefer separate
orbitals when energetically equivalent.

Periodic trends follow from two competing quantities: effective nuclear charge, the net
positive pull an outer electron experiences after accounting for shielding by inner
electrons, and the principal quantum number, which sets how far out the electron sits.
Across a period, protons are added while shielding barely changes, so effective nuclear
charge rises and electrons are pulled in tighter. Down a group, a new shell is added and
the outer electrons are both farther out and better shielded.

Periodic trends, and the direction each runs:
  atomic radius          decreases left to right, increases down a group
  ionization energy      increases left to right, decreases down a group
  electron affinity      generally increases left to right (more negative)
  electronegativity      increases toward fluorine, the most electronegative element
  metallic character     decreases left to right, increases down
  cation vs neutral      cations are smaller (lost a shell or reduced repulsion)
  anion vs neutral       anions are larger (added repulsion, same nuclear charge)
  isoelectronic series   same electron count; radius decreases as nuclear charge
                         rises, so N3- > O2- > F- > Na+ > Mg2+ > Al3+

Irregularities in these trends are informative rather than exceptions to be memorized.
Ionization energy dips from nitrogen to oxygen because oxygen's fourth p electron must
pair in an already-occupied orbital, paying a repulsion cost. It dips from beryllium to
boron because boron's outermost electron is in a higher-energy p orbital rather than the
filled s. Both cases show the same principle: filled and half-filled subshells are
especially stable, which also explains chromium and copper adopting unexpected
configurations to achieve them.

Q: Which has the smallest atomic radius: Na, Mg, Al, or Si?
A. Na   B. Mg   C. Al   D. Si
Reasoning: All four are in period 3, so they have the same number of shells. Moving left
to right adds protons while shielding stays essentially constant, so effective nuclear
charge increases and the electron cloud contracts. Silicon, furthest right of the four,
is smallest. Answer: D.

## Bonding and Molecular Structure

Chemical bonds are the redistribution of electrons toward lower-energy configurations.
Ionic bonding transfers electrons from a low-ionization-energy metal to a
high-electron-affinity nonmetal, producing ions held by electrostatic attraction in an
extended lattice — which is why ionic compounds are hard, brittle, high-melting, and
conductive only when molten or dissolved, since the ions must be free to move. Covalent
bonding shares electron pairs between atoms of similar electronegativity, producing
discrete molecules with weaker intermolecular forces and correspondingly lower melting
points. Metallic bonding delocalizes valence electrons across a lattice of cations,
giving conductivity, malleability, and lustre.

The ionic-covalent distinction is a continuum rather than a dichotomy, indexed by
electronegativity difference. A difference above roughly 1.7 is conventionally called
ionic, below 0.4 nonpolar covalent, and between them polar covalent, where electrons are
shared unequally and the bond carries a dipole. Molecular polarity then depends on both
bond polarity and geometry: carbon dioxide has two polar bonds whose dipoles cancel by
symmetry, so the molecule is nonpolar, while water's bent geometry leaves a net dipole,
which is the origin of nearly all of water's anomalous properties.

VSEPR theory predicts geometry from the principle that electron domains — bonding pairs
and lone pairs alike — arrange to minimize repulsion. Count the domains around the
central atom, place them in the arrangement that maximizes separation, then describe the
shape by the positions of the atoms only. Lone pairs occupy more angular space than
bonding pairs because they are held by only one nucleus, which is why water's H-O-H
angle is 104.5 degrees rather than the ideal tetrahedral 109.5.

VSEPR geometries:
  2 domains, 0 lone      linear, 180 deg              CO2, BeCl2
  3 domains, 0 lone      trigonal planar, 120 deg     BF3, CO3^2-
  3 domains, 1 lone      bent, ~118 deg               SO2, O3
  4 domains, 0 lone      tetrahedral, 109.5 deg       CH4, NH4+
  4 domains, 1 lone      trigonal pyramidal, ~107     NH3
  4 domains, 2 lone      bent, ~104.5                 H2O
  5 domains, 0 lone      trigonal bipyramidal         PCl5
  6 domains, 0 lone      octahedral, 90 deg           SF6
  6 domains, 2 lone      square planar                XeF4

Intermolecular forces determine physical properties and are far weaker than the bonds
within molecules. London dispersion forces arise from instantaneous fluctuating dipoles
and exist in everything, strengthening with molecular size and polarizability — which is
why the halogens go from gas to liquid to solid down the group with no change in bonding
type. Dipole-dipole forces act between permanent dipoles. Hydrogen bonding, a
particularly strong dipole interaction where hydrogen bonded to nitrogen, oxygen, or
fluorine interacts with a lone pair on another such atom, is responsible for water's high
boiling point, ice's lower density than liquid water, DNA's base pairing, and protein
secondary structure.

## Stoichiometry and Reaction Types

The mole connects the atomic scale to the laboratory scale: one mole is Avogadro's
number, 6.022e23, of particles, and the molar mass in grams per mole is numerically equal
to the atomic or molecular mass in atomic mass units. Every quantitative calculation in
chemistry passes through moles, because balanced equations express fixed ratios in
particles and laboratory measurements are in grams or litres.

A balanced equation encodes conservation of mass and of atoms. The coefficients give the
molar ratios in which species react, so a reaction requiring two moles of hydrogen per
mole of oxygen will consume exactly that ratio regardless of how much of each is
supplied. The limiting reactant — the one that runs out first — determines the maximum
product, and identifying it requires converting each reactant to moles and dividing by
its coefficient, then comparing. The reactant giving the smallest quotient is limiting;
comparing raw masses or raw moles without dividing by coefficients is the standard error.

Percent yield compares actual to theoretical product and is always below 100% in
practice for reasons that are chemical rather than sloppy: side reactions consume
reactants, equilibria do not go to completion, and product is lost in transfer and
purification. A yield above 100% indicates impure or wet product, not exceptional
chemistry.

Stoichiometric relations:
  moles from mass        n = m / M
  moles from volume, gas n = PV / RT;  22.4 L/mol at STP
  moles from solution    n = c V
  molarity               c = n / V (litres of solution)
  molality               b = n / kg of solvent; used for colligative properties
  limiting reactant      smallest (moles / coefficient)
  percent yield          100 * actual / theoretical
  empirical formula      simplest whole-number ratio from percent composition
  molecular formula      empirical formula times (molar mass / empirical mass)
  ideal gas law          PV = nRT;  R = 8.314 J/(mol K) = 0.0821 L atm/(mol K)

Reaction types organize what would otherwise be an unbounded catalogue. Synthesis
combines simpler substances into a complex one; decomposition reverses it. Single
displacement has an element displacing another from a compound, governed by the activity
series. Double displacement exchanges partners between two compounds, driven by the
formation of a precipitate, a gas, or water. Combustion reacts a fuel with oxygen,
producing carbon dioxide and water for a hydrocarbon. Acid-base reactions transfer
protons. Redox reactions transfer electrons — and this last category cuts across the
others, since combustion and single displacement are both redox processes.

Q: 4.0 g of H2 reacts with 4.0 g of O2 to form water. The limiting reactant and the mass
of water formed are:
A. H2 limiting, 36 g   B. O2 limiting, 4.5 g   C. O2 limiting, 9.0 g   D. Neither, 8.0 g
Reasoning: 4.0 g H2 is 2.0 mol; 4.0 g O2 is 0.125 mol. The reaction 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O
needs 2 mol H2 per 1 mol O2, so dividing by coefficients gives 1.0 for H2 and 0.125 for
O2 — oxygen is limiting by a wide margin. It produces 0.25 mol water, which is 4.5 g.
Answer: B.

## Thermodynamics and Kinetics

Thermodynamics determines whether a reaction can occur; kinetics determines whether it
will occur at an observable rate. The two are independent, and conflating them is the
most common conceptual error in the subject. Diamond converting to graphite is
thermodynamically favourable at room temperature and kinetically forbidden on any human
timescale; a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen is enormously favourable and sits stable
indefinitely until a spark provides the activation energy.

The first law states that energy is conserved: the internal energy change equals heat
added minus work done. Enthalpy, defined as internal energy plus pressure times volume,
is the convenient bookkeeping quantity at constant pressure, where the enthalpy change
equals the heat absorbed or released. Exothermic reactions have negative enthalpy change
and release heat; endothermic reactions absorb it. Hess's law states that enthalpy change
is path-independent, so an overall reaction's enthalpy is the sum of any sequence of
steps summing to it — which is how enthalpies of reactions that cannot be measured
directly are determined from tabulated formation enthalpies.

The second law introduces entropy, a measure of the number of accessible microstates,
and states that the entropy of the universe increases in any spontaneous process. The
crucial subtlety is that a system's entropy may decrease — a crystal forming, a cell
organizing — provided the surroundings' entropy increases more. Gibbs free energy
packages this into a system-only criterion: at constant temperature and pressure, a
process is spontaneous when the change in G = H - TS is negative. The temperature
dependence is what makes reactions switch direction: an endothermic reaction with a
positive entropy change is non-spontaneous at low temperature and spontaneous above a
crossover, which is why ice melts above zero degrees and freezes below.

Thermodynamic and kinetic quantities:
  first law              dU = q - w
  enthalpy               dH = q at constant pressure
  Hess's law             dH is path-independent; sum any route
  standard formation     dH_rxn = sum(products) - sum(reactants) of dH_f
  entropy direction      gas > liquid > solid; more particles > fewer
  Gibbs free energy      dG = dH - T dS;  negative means spontaneous
  equilibrium relation   dG = -RT ln K
  rate law               rate = k [A]^m [B]^n;  orders are experimental, not from
                         the balanced equation
  Arrhenius              k = A exp(-Ea / RT);  rate roughly doubles per 10 K
  half-life, first order  t_half = ln 2 / k, independent of concentration
  catalyst               lowers Ea, speeds both directions equally, does not shift
                         the equilibrium position

That last point is worth emphasizing because it is frequently misstated. A catalyst
provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, accelerating the forward
and reverse reactions by the same factor. It therefore shortens the time to reach
equilibrium and leaves the equilibrium constant entirely unchanged, because the
equilibrium constant is thermodynamic and the catalyst changes only the kinetics.

Reaction orders must be determined experimentally and cannot be read from the balanced
equation, because the balanced equation describes overall stoichiometry while the rate law
reflects the slowest elementary step. A reaction that is second order overall may proceed
through a mechanism whose rate-determining step involves only one molecule, and observing
that mismatch is precisely how mechanisms are deduced. The method of initial rates —
varying one concentration at a time and observing the rate change — is the standard
experimental approach.

At 60 C your rate constant is 0.379 per hour, and first-order kinetics put 95%
conversion at 7.9 hours — so roughly four more hours than you have already run. Nothing
is wrong; you are simply on the flat part of an exponential approach, where each
additional increment of conversion costs disproportionately more time. Getting from 78%
to 95% takes as long as getting from 0% to 78% did.

Raising to 80 C multiplies the rate constant by 3.7 (from the Arrhenius equation with
your 85 kJ/mol activation energy), bringing 95% conversion to about 2.1 hours total —
so from where you are now, under half an hour.

Two things to check before raising the temperature, because they are what usually make
this the wrong choice. First, selectivity: side reactions generally have different
activation energies, and if a competing pathway has a higher one it accelerates faster
than your desired reaction, so yield can fall even as conversion rises. Run a small trial
and check purity, not just conversion. Second, thermal stability of the product and of
any sensitive reagent — a 20 degree rise is substantial for anything labile.

If both check out, raise the temperature; it is the better answer since it also improves
throughput. If selectivity degrades, extend the time instead, and consider whether a
catalyst is available — that lowers activation energy and speeds the reaction without
the selectivity penalty that comes from thermal acceleration of every pathway at once.

## Equilibrium, Acids, and Electrochemistry

Chemical equilibrium is dynamic: forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates,
so concentrations are constant while molecules continue to interconvert. The equilibrium
constant K is the ratio of product to reactant activities at equilibrium, each raised to
its stoichiometric coefficient, and it depends only on temperature. A large K means the
equilibrium lies toward products; a small one toward reactants. Comparing the reaction
quotient Q, computed from current concentrations, against K tells which direction the
system will move.

Le Chatelier's principle states that a system at equilibrium responds to a disturbance by
shifting to partially counteract it. Adding a reactant shifts toward products. Removing a
product shifts toward products, which is why driving a reaction to completion often means
continuously removing something. Increasing pressure shifts toward the side with fewer
moles of gas. Raising temperature shifts in the endothermic direction, since heat behaves
as a reactant or product — and this is the one disturbance that actually changes K rather
than merely shifting position along it.

Acid-base chemistry is proton transfer in the Bronsted-Lowry framework: an acid donates a
proton, a base accepts one, and every acid has a conjugate base differing by that proton.
Strong acids dissociate completely; weak acids establish an equilibrium characterized by
Ka. The pH scale is logarithmic, pH = -log[H+], so each unit is a tenfold change in
concentration, and at 25 degrees the neutral point is 7 because the ion product of water
is 1e-14.

Acid-base and electrochemical relations:
  pH                     -log[H+];  pOH = -log[OH-];  pH + pOH = 14 at 25 C
  weak acid              Ka = [H+][A-]/[HA];  pKa = -log Ka
  Henderson-Hasselbalch  pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA])
  buffer capacity        maximal when pH = pKa;  effective within about +/- 1 unit
  titration equivalence  strong-strong at pH 7; weak acid-strong base above 7
  half-equivalence       pH = pKa;  the practical way to measure pKa
  oxidation              loss of electrons, increase in oxidation number
  reduction              gain of electrons, decrease in oxidation number
  cell potential         E_cell = E_cathode - E_anode;  positive means spontaneous
  Nernst equation        E = E0 - (RT/nF) ln Q
  Faraday relation       moles of electrons = It / F;  F = 96485 C/mol
  free energy link       dG = -nFE

Buffers resist pH change and are the reason biological systems function, since enzymes
operate over narrow pH ranges. A buffer is a weak acid with its conjugate base in
comparable amounts; added acid is consumed by the base component and added base by the
acid component. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation shows that pH depends on the ratio
rather than the absolute concentrations, which is why dilution barely changes buffer pH
while it does reduce capacity. Blood is buffered principally by the bicarbonate system,
coupled to respiration — which allows the body to adjust pH by changing breathing rate,
an unusually direct link between a physiological control and a chemical equilibrium.

Electrochemistry connects chemical and electrical energy. In a galvanic cell a
spontaneous redox reaction drives current, with oxidation at the anode and reduction at
the cathode. In electrolysis an applied potential drives a non-spontaneous reaction. The
standard reduction potential table ranks species by their tendency to be reduced, and the
cell potential is the difference between the two half-reactions. Because potentials are
intensive, they are not multiplied by stoichiometric coefficients when combining half
reactions — a frequent error — though the free energy, being extensive, does scale with
the electron count through dG = -nFE.

## Organic Chemistry

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon, and carbon's dominance follows from its
four valence electrons, its ability to form stable bonds to itself in chains and rings of
unlimited length, and the comparable strength of its bonds to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen,
and the halogens. Silicon, directly below carbon, forms far weaker bonds to itself and
cannot support the same structural diversity, which is the chemical basis for expecting
carbon-based rather than silicon-based life.

Functional groups organize the field. A functional group is a specific arrangement of
atoms conferring characteristic reactivity, and the value of the concept is that a given
group behaves similarly regardless of the rest of the molecule. Learning perhaps a dozen
groups and their characteristic transformations covers most of introductory organic
chemistry, and the systematic naming conventions follow from identifying the parent
chain, the principal functional group, and the substituents.

Functional groups by increasing oxidation state of carbon:
  alkane        C-C, C-H only; unreactive, combustion and radical halogenation
  alkene        C=C; addition reactions, Markovnikov orientation
  alkyne        C-C triple bond; addition, acidic terminal hydrogen
  alcohol       C-OH; hydrogen bonding, oxidation to carbonyl
  ether         C-O-C; unreactive, common solvent
  amine         C-N; basic, nucleophilic
  aldehyde      CHO at chain end; readily oxidized
  ketone        C=O within chain; resistant to further oxidation
  carboxylic acid  COOH; acidic, pKa ~4-5
  ester         COOR; from acid plus alcohol, hydrolyzable
  amide         CONR2; the peptide bond, unusually stable
  aromatic      delocalized ring; substitution rather than addition

Isomerism is where organic structure becomes subtle. Constitutional isomers differ in
connectivity. Stereoisomers share connectivity and differ in spatial arrangement:
geometric isomers (cis and trans across a double bond or ring) and optical isomers
(enantiomers, non-superimposable mirror images arising from a carbon with four different
substituents). Enantiomers have identical physical properties except for the direction
they rotate polarized light, and identical chemical properties except toward other chiral
molecules — which is the entire issue in pharmacology, since biological receptors are
chiral and typically bind one enantiomer while the other is inactive or harmful.

Reaction mechanisms explain transformations in terms of electron movement, conventionally
drawn with curved arrows from electron source to electron sink. The major mechanistic
families are nucleophilic substitution (SN1 through a carbocation intermediate with
racemization, SN2 in one concerted step with inversion of configuration), elimination
(E1 and E2, competing with substitution and favoured by heat and strong bulky bases),
addition to multiple bonds, and radical chains. Predicting which pathway dominates
requires weighing substrate structure, nucleophile strength, leaving group ability, and
solvent — tertiary substrates with weak nucleophiles in polar protic solvents favour SN1,
primary substrates with strong nucleophiles in polar aprotic solvents favour SN2, and
recognizing that pattern converts a great deal of apparent memorization into reasoning.
