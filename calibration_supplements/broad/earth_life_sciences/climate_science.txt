## The Planetary Energy Balance

Earth's climate is fundamentally an energy balance problem. Solar radiation arrives,
some is reflected, the rest is absorbed and eventually re-emitted as infrared. When
incoming and outgoing energy balance, temperature is steady; when they do not, the system
gains or loses heat until a new balance is reached.

The arithmetic is straightforward. The solar constant at Earth's distance is about 1361
watts per square metre, intercepted over a disc of the planet's cross-sectional area and
distributed over the whole sphere, giving 340 watts per square metre averaged globally.
About 30 percent is reflected by clouds, aerosols, ice, and the surface — the planetary
albedo — leaving roughly 240 watts per square metre absorbed. Setting that equal to
blackbody emission gives an effective temperature of about 255 kelvin, or minus 18 degrees
Celsius.

The observed global mean surface temperature is about 15 degrees Celsius, some 33 degrees
warmer. That difference is the greenhouse effect, and its existence is not in dispute by
anyone: without it the planet would be frozen. The scientific questions concern its
magnitude under changed composition, the feedbacks that amplify or damp the response, and
the regional and temporal distribution of the consequences.

Energy balance quantities:
  solar constant           ~1361 W/m^2 at the top of the atmosphere
  global mean insolation   ~340 W/m^2 (divided by 4 for sphere vs disc)
  planetary albedo         ~0.30
  absorbed shortwave       ~240 W/m^2
  effective temperature    255 K from Stefan-Boltzmann
  observed surface mean    ~288 K
  greenhouse warming       ~33 K
  current imbalance        ~+0.9 W/m^2 measured; energy accumulating
  ocean heat uptake        >90% of the excess energy goes into the ocean
  CO2 doubling forcing     ~3.7 W/m^2

The measured top-of-atmosphere energy imbalance is the single most direct evidence that
the system is out of equilibrium. Satellite radiometry combined with ocean heat content
measurements from autonomous floats shows the planet absorbing roughly 0.9 watts per square
metre more than it emits. That is a small number relative to 340, and integrated over the
Earth's surface and over years it is an enormous amount of energy — the equivalent of
several Hiroshima-scale detonations per second, more than 90 percent of which is going into
the ocean rather than the atmosphere. This is why ocean heat content is a better metric of
the energy imbalance than surface air temperature, which is noisy and reflects a small
fraction of the total.

Q: Earth's effective radiating temperature is 255 K while its surface averages 288 K.
This difference is because:
A. The Sun's output has increased
B. Greenhouse gases absorb outgoing infrared and re-emit, raising the altitude of effective
   emission to space and warming everything below
C. The atmosphere generates heat
D. Albedo has decreased
Reasoning: The effective temperature is the temperature at which the planet radiates to
space. Greenhouse gases are largely transparent to incoming visible light and opaque to
outgoing infrared, so radiation to space occurs from high, cold layers rather than the
surface. Since the atmosphere cools with altitude and the emission level must be at 255 K to
balance, the surface below is correspondingly warmer. Answer: B.

## Greenhouse Physics and Radiative Forcing

A greenhouse gas is one that absorbs and emits infrared radiation, which requires a
molecule with a dipole moment that changes during vibration. Diatomic molecules of
identical atoms — nitrogen and oxygen, which together are 99 percent of the atmosphere —
have no such mode and are therefore transparent to infrared. Water vapour, carbon dioxide,
methane, nitrous oxide, and ozone all do absorb, which is why trace gases can dominate the
planet's radiative properties.

The mechanism is often described loosely as heat being trapped, and the more accurate
framing is about emission altitude. Adding greenhouse gas makes the atmosphere more opaque
in the relevant infrared bands, so photons escaping to space are emitted from higher in the
atmosphere. Because temperature decreases with altitude in the troposphere, that higher
emission level is colder and radiates less, creating an imbalance. The surface and
troposphere then warm until the emission level, still at higher altitude, has warmed enough
to restore balance.

Radiative forcing quantifies the imbalance a perturbation causes before the climate
responds, measured in watts per square metre. Doubling carbon dioxide produces about 3.7
watts per square metre. The relationship is logarithmic rather than linear — each doubling
adds roughly the same forcing — because the strongest absorption bands become saturated at
the centre and additional absorption occurs in the band wings. This logarithmic dependence
means the forcing does not run away with concentration, and it also means that reducing
concentration from a high level buys back less than adding it cost.

Greenhouse gases and their properties:
  water vapour     the strongest greenhouse gas; a feedback, not a forcing, since
                   its concentration is set by temperature and its residence time
                   is days
  carbon dioxide   the dominant anthropogenic forcing; extremely long-lived, with
                   a substantial fraction persisting for millennia
  methane          ~80x CO2 potency over 20 years, ~28x over 100; ~12 year
                   lifetime, so cuts act fast
  nitrous oxide    ~270x CO2 over 100 years; ~115 year lifetime; mainly
                   agricultural
  halocarbons      thousands of times more potent per molecule; regulated under
                   the Montreal Protocol, which also proved a large climate benefit
  ozone            tropospheric ozone warms; stratospheric ozone loss cools
  aerosols         mostly cooling, by scattering sunlight and seeding clouds;
                   short-lived, regionally concentrated, and the largest source of
                   uncertainty in the total forcing
  forcing of 2xCO2 ~3.7 W/m^2
  logarithmic law  forcing ≈ 5.35 ln(C/C0) W/m^2

The distinction between a forcing and a feedback is essential and is where a common
argument goes wrong. Water vapour is indeed the largest contributor to the natural
greenhouse effect, and it is not a driver of change, because its atmospheric concentration
is controlled by temperature through the Clausius-Clapeyron relation and its residence time
is about nine days. Adding water vapour to the atmosphere does nothing lasting; it rains
out. Adding carbon dioxide raises temperature, which raises the equilibrium water vapour
content, which amplifies the warming — water vapour acts as a feedback multiplying whatever
forcing is applied rather than as an independent cause.

## The Carbon Cycle

Carbon moves between reservoirs — atmosphere, ocean, land biosphere, soils, rocks — on
timescales spanning years to millions of years. The fast cycle exchanges enormous fluxes:
photosynthesis and respiration each move roughly 120 gigatonnes of carbon per year, and
ocean-atmosphere exchange moves about 80, against an atmospheric stock of around 875
gigatonnes. Human emissions of roughly 10 gigatonnes per year are small against those
gross fluxes and are not balanced by an offsetting return flux, which is why they
accumulate.

About half of anthropogenic emissions remain in the atmosphere; the ocean absorbs roughly a
quarter and the land biosphere roughly a quarter. These sinks are services performed at a
cost: ocean uptake acidifies seawater, with surface pH having fallen about 0.1 units since
preindustrial times, a 30 percent increase in hydrogen ion concentration, with direct
consequences for organisms building calcium carbonate structures. Land uptake depends on
ecosystems whose future behaviour under warming and drought is uncertain, and there is
evidence that sink efficiency is declining.

Carbon dioxide's atmospheric lifetime is frequently misstated because it is not a simple
exponential. An individual molecule exchanges out of the atmosphere within a few years, but
the perturbation to atmospheric concentration decays much more slowly, because the removed
carbon largely enters the ocean surface which then re-equilibrates. Roughly half of an
emission pulse is gone within a few decades, a quarter persists for centuries, and something
like ten to twenty percent remains for tens of thousands of years until silicate weathering
removes it. This long tail is why cumulative emissions rather than emission rate determine
peak warming.

Carbon cycle numbers:
  preindustrial CO2      ~278 ppm
  current CO2            >420 ppm and rising ~2.5 ppm/yr
  atmospheric stock      ~875 GtC (2.13 GtC per ppm)
  annual emissions       ~10 GtC (~37 Gt CO2) from fossil fuels and land use
  airborne fraction      ~45%
  ocean sink             ~25%; causes acidification
  land sink              ~30%; uncertain and possibly weakening
  ocean pH change        -0.1 units since preindustrial (~30% more H+)
  permafrost carbon      ~1400 GtC, roughly twice the atmospheric stock
  transient response     ~1.65 C per 1000 GtC cumulative emissions

The near-linear relationship between cumulative emissions and warming is one of the more
useful results in the field. Because carbon dioxide's long residence time and the ocean's
thermal inertia happen to offset each other, peak warming is approximately proportional to
total carbon emitted regardless of the emission pathway. This produces the carbon budget
framing: a given temperature target corresponds to a total quantity of carbon that can be
emitted, and once it is spent, the target requires net zero emissions. It also means that
stabilizing temperature requires net zero rather than merely reduced emissions, which is a
frequently misunderstood point — cutting emissions in half slows the accumulation without
stopping the warming.

## Feedbacks and Climate Sensitivity

Climate sensitivity is the equilibrium warming resulting from a doubling of carbon
dioxide, and it is the single most consequential number in the field. The direct radiative
effect alone would produce about 1.2 degrees; feedbacks amplify this substantially. Current
assessment places the likely range at 2.5 to 4 degrees with a best estimate around 3, a
range that has narrowed only modestly over decades because the dominant uncertainty is
clouds.

The feedbacks are individually well characterized in sign if not in magnitude. Water vapour
is strongly positive: warmer air holds more moisture at about 7 percent per degree, and
water vapour is a greenhouse gas. The lapse rate feedback is negative, because warming aloft
exceeds surface warming in the tropics, increasing emission to space. Surface albedo is
positive: melting ice and snow expose darker ocean and land, absorbing more sunlight, and
this is amplified at high latitudes, producing Arctic warming at roughly three times the
global rate. Clouds are the problem: they both reflect sunlight (cooling) and trap infrared
(warming), and which dominates depends on altitude, thickness, and phase, so the net cloud
feedback has been the largest uncertainty since the beginning.

Feedback inventory:
  water vapour       strongly positive; roughly doubles the direct forcing
  lapse rate         negative; partially offsets the water vapour feedback
  combined WV+LR     positive, and better constrained together than separately
  surface albedo     positive; ice-albedo, strongest in the Arctic
  cloud              net positive by current assessment, largest uncertainty;
                     low clouds cool, high thin clouds warm
  Planck response    the fundamental negative feedback; a warmer planet radiates
                     more, which is what makes the system stable at all
  carbon cycle       positive; warming reduces sink efficiency
  permafrost         positive; thaw releases CO2 and methane
  ocean circulation  uncertain sign; changes to overturning redistribute heat

Tipping elements are components of the system that may pass thresholds beyond which change
becomes self-sustaining and effectively irreversible on human timescales. The candidates
include the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, whose loss would raise sea level by
metres over centuries; Atlantic overturning circulation, whose slowdown would substantially
alter European and tropical climate; Amazon forest dieback converting a sink into a source;
and widespread permafrost thaw. Confidence in specific thresholds is low, and their
existence is well established in paleoclimate records — the system has undergone abrupt
reorganizations before, which is the principal reason for concern about them.

## Observations, Paleoclimate, and Models

The observational record rests on multiple independent lines that agree. Surface
thermometers extend back to the mid-nineteenth century, with careful treatment of station
moves, instrument changes, and urban heat effects; several groups process the raw data
independently with different methods and obtain closely matching results. Satellite
measurements provide global coverage since 1979. Ocean heat content from floats gives the
best measure of the energy imbalance. Sea level rise is measured by tide gauges and
altimetry. Ice sheet mass loss is measured by satellite gravimetry. Glacier retreat, sea
ice decline, and shifts in the timing of biological events all corroborate.

Paleoclimate reconstruction extends the record using proxies. Ice cores from Antarctica and
Greenland trap air bubbles giving direct measurements of past atmospheric composition back
800,000 years, alongside isotopic temperature proxies. Ocean sediment cores give
temperature and ice volume over millions of years. Tree rings, corals, speleothems, and
pollen assemblages fill in regional detail. These records establish that the current carbon
dioxide concentration is higher than at any point in at least the last 800,000 years and
probably several million, and that the current rate of increase is roughly a hundred times
faster than the fastest natural change in the ice core record.

Attribution — determining what caused observed change — uses fingerprinting. Different
forcings produce distinct spatial and temporal patterns, and the observed pattern matches
greenhouse forcing rather than the alternatives. Greenhouse warming warms the troposphere
while cooling the stratosphere; increased solar output would warm both. Greenhouse warming
produces more warming at night than during the day, and more in winter than summer. Solar
output has been flat or slightly declining since the 1980s while temperature has risen.
Each of these is an independent discriminating test.

Model evaluation deserves comment because model skill is often questioned in general terms
rather than assessed. Projections made in the 1970s through 1990s can now be compared
against what happened, and when adjusted for the actual emissions that occurred rather than
the scenarios assumed, most were skillful. Models reproduce the observed response to
volcanic eruptions, which are natural experiments applying a known forcing over a known
period. They reproduce the vertical structure of warming, the seasonal cycle, and
paleoclimate states with different configurations. They remain weak at regional detail,
precipitation extremes, and clouds — which is precisely where their published uncertainty is
largest, indicating that the uncertainties are being reported honestly rather than
concealed.

Natural factors cannot account for it. Solar output has been flat to slightly declining
since the 1980s while temperature rose sharply, so the sign is wrong. Volcanic activity
produces brief cooling episodes, not sustained warming. Internal variability — ocean
oscillations such as ENSO — redistributes heat between ocean and atmosphere and does not
add energy to the system, so it produces year-to-year noise around a trend rather than a
trend, and its contribution over 170 years is bounded at a couple of tenths.

The discriminating evidence that makes this more than a process of elimination is the
pattern. Greenhouse warming makes a specific prediction: the troposphere warms while the
stratosphere cools, because more infrared is absorbed below and less reaches the layers
above. Increased solar input would warm both. The stratosphere has cooled, measured by
satellite since 1979. Additional independent fingerprints all match: nights warm faster than
days, winters faster than summers, the Arctic faster than the tropics, and the isotopic
signature of atmospheric carbon shows the added carbon is depleted in carbon-13 and
carbon-14 exactly as fossil carbon is.

## Impacts and Response

Warming is not the impact; it is the driver of impacts, which are distributed unevenly and
are largely nonlinear. Heat extremes increase disproportionately with mean temperature
because they occur in the tail of a distribution — a modest shift in the mean produces a
large multiplication in the frequency of what were rare extremes. A warmer atmosphere holds
more moisture, intensifying heavy precipitation while also increasing evaporative demand and
therefore drought severity in dry periods. Sea level rises from thermal expansion and ice
loss, with the commitment extending centuries beyond stabilization because ice sheets
respond slowly.

Ecosystem impacts include range shifts toward poles and higher elevations, mismatches
between interdependent species whose seasonal timing responds differently, coral bleaching
from marine heatwaves compounded by acidification, and elevated extinction risk for species
unable to move or adapt fast enough. Agricultural effects are regionally mixed but net
negative at higher warming levels, with carbon dioxide fertilization providing some benefit
offset by heat stress, water limitation, and reduced nutritional content.

Response divides into mitigation, reducing emissions, and adaptation, reducing vulnerability
to what occurs. Both are necessary: some warming is already committed by past emissions and
requires adaptation, and unmitigated warming would eventually exceed any plausible adaptive
capacity.

Mitigation approaches by sector and maturity:
  electricity        the most advanced; solar and wind are now the cheapest new

                     the remaining challenge

  buildings          heat pumps, efficiency, electrification; a large near-term

  agriculture        methane from livestock and rice, nitrous oxide from

The economics have shifted substantially and unevenly. Solar photovoltaic costs fell by
roughly ninety percent over a decade and batteries similarly, making decarbonized
electricity the low-cost option rather than a premium one across most of the world. That
does not extend to the harder sectors, and the remaining difficulty is concentrated in
industrial heat, chemical process emissions, aviation, shipping, and agriculture. The
framing that the problem is either solved or hopeless misrepresents a situation in which
some parts have become straightforward and others have not, and where the binding
constraints are increasingly institutional — permitting, transmission, capital allocation,
and coordination — rather than technological.
