## Atmospheric Structure and Composition

The atmosphere is layered by its temperature profile, and the layers exist because different
heating mechanisms dominate at different altitudes. The troposphere, from the surface to roughly
ten kilometres, is heated from below by the surface and therefore cools with height, which makes
it convectively unstable and is why essentially all weather occurs there. The stratosphere above
it warms with height because ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation, which makes it stably stratified
and largely free of vertical mixing — the reason aircraft cruise there and the reason material
injected into it persists for years.

Pressure falls approximately exponentially with height because the atmosphere is compressible and
in hydrostatic balance. The scale height, roughly eight kilometres, is the altitude over which
pressure falls by a factor of e, and it depends on temperature, which is why the profile differs
between warm and cold air columns. Half the atmosphere's mass lies below about 5.5 kilometres,
which is why altitude affects human physiology so sharply and why mountain observatories are
above a substantial fraction of the atmosphere's absorption.

Composition is dominated by nitrogen and oxygen, which together are ninety-nine percent, and both
are transparent to infrared and therefore radiatively inactive. The radiatively important
constituents are trace gases — water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, ozone, nitrous oxide — whose
combined abundance is well under one percent and whose infrared absorption determines the
planet's surface temperature. Water vapour is the most abundant and the most variable, ranging
from near zero to several percent, and its concentration is set by temperature rather than being
independent.

Q: Nitrogen and oxygen constitute 99% of the atmosphere and contribute essentially nothing to the
greenhouse effect. The reason is:
A. Their concentration is too low
B. Diatomic molecules of identical atoms have no changing dipole moment during vibration, so they
   do not absorb infrared radiation
C. They are too light
D. They are located too high
Reasoning: Infrared absorption requires a vibrational mode that changes the molecule's dipole
moment. Symmetric diatomic molecules have no dipole and none of their modes create one, so they
are transparent to infrared. Molecules with three or more atoms, or with dissimilar atoms, have
such modes, which is why trace gases dominate the radiative properties. Answer: B.

## Radiation, Energy, and Thermodynamics

The atmosphere is driven by the imbalance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing infrared,
distributed unevenly over the surface. The tropics receive more energy than they radiate and the
poles radiate more than they receive, and the resulting gradient is what drives atmospheric and
oceanic circulation — the entire weather system is a heat engine transporting energy poleward.

Energy is transported by three mechanisms whose relative importance varies with height and
latitude. Radiation transfers energy directly between the surface, the atmosphere, and space.
Conduction is negligible in air except in the millimetre adjacent to the surface. Convection
transports enormous quantities vertically, and latent heat transport — evaporating water at the
surface and releasing the heat where it condenses aloft — is a substantial component that a purely
sensible-heat analysis misses entirely.

Atmospheric thermodynamics is dominated by the behaviour of rising air. A parcel rising expands
because pressure falls, and expansion cools it — adiabatically, since the parcel exchanges
negligible heat with its surroundings on the relevant timescale. The dry adiabatic lapse rate is
about ten degrees per kilometre, and once condensation begins the released latent heat partially
offsets the cooling, giving a moist rate of roughly five to six degrees per kilometre that varies
with temperature.

Stability follows from comparing the environmental lapse rate to the adiabatic rates. If the
environment cools with height faster than a rising parcel does, the parcel remains warmer than its
surroundings and continues rising — unstable, and convection develops. If the environment cools
more slowly, a displaced parcel returns — stable. The interesting case is conditional instability,
where the environment is stable for unsaturated air and unstable for saturated air, so convection
develops only if a parcel is lifted far enough to reach saturation.

Convective inhibition deserves emphasis because it explains why severe convection is explosive
rather than gradual. A stable layer aloft — a cap — prevents parcels from reaching their level of
free convection, so surface heating accumulates buoyant energy without releasing it. When the cap
is finally broken, by continued heating or by lifting at a front, the accumulated energy is
released at once and produces far more violent convection than would have occurred had it been
released continuously. This is why the most severe thunderstorms develop in environments with both
large available energy and a cap, and why forecasting them involves predicting when the cap will
break.

## Dynamics and Circulation

Atmospheric motion results from pressure gradients, modified by rotation, friction, and
curvature. The pressure gradient force pushes air from high to low pressure, and if it acted alone
the air would flow directly across the isobars and equalize the pressure within hours. It does not,
because Earth's rotation deflects moving air.

The Coriolis effect deflects motion to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in
the southern, with magnitude proportional to speed and to the sine of latitude — so it vanishes at
the equator and is strongest at the poles. Above the friction layer, the balance between the
pressure gradient and the Coriolis deflection produces geostrophic flow, which runs parallel to
the isobars rather than across them. This is why weather systems have circulation around them
rather than flow into them, and why wind direction can be read from a pressure map.

Near the surface, friction slows the wind, which weakens the Coriolis effect and permits some
flow across the isobars toward low pressure. The consequence is convergence into low pressure
systems, which forces ascent, which produces cloud and precipitation — and divergence from high
pressure, which forces subsidence and clear conditions. This is why lows are associated with
unsettled weather and highs with settled weather, and it follows from friction rather than from
anything about the pressure itself.

The general circulation organizes into cells. The Hadley cell rises at the equator where heating
is greatest, moves poleward aloft, and descends around thirty degrees latitude — which is where
the world's major deserts are, because descending air warms and its relative humidity falls. The
Ferrel and polar cells complete the pattern, and the jet streams flow along the boundaries where
temperature gradients are strongest.

The thermal wind relationship deserves note because it links the temperature and wind fields
directly. The vertical change in the geostrophic wind is proportional to the horizontal temperature
gradient, so a strong temperature contrast necessarily produces strong shear and a jet aloft. This
is why the polar jet sits above the boundary between polar and mid-latitude air, why it
strengthens in winter when that contrast is greatest, and why a reduced equator-to-pole temperature
gradient would be expected to weaken it — which is the basis of the argument that Arctic warming
affects mid-latitude circulation, an argument that remains contested.

## Weather Systems and Severe Weather

Mid-latitude weather is dominated by extratropical cyclones, which develop along the boundary
between air masses of different temperature. The process is baroclinic instability: a horizontal
temperature gradient stores available potential energy, and a wave disturbance on the boundary
grows by converting it, with warm air rising poleward and cold air sinking equatorward. The
resulting system has a characteristic structure of warm and cold fronts that produces a
recognizable sequence of weather as it passes.

Fronts are boundaries between air masses and produce different weather according to which air is
advancing. A warm front has warm air overriding retreating cold air along a shallow slope,
producing extensive layered cloud and prolonged light precipitation ahead of it. A cold front has
cold air undercutting warm air along a steeper slope, forcing rapid ascent and producing a narrow
band of intense convective precipitation. An occluded front forms when the faster cold front
overtakes the warm one, lifting the warm air entirely off the surface.

Thunderstorms require moisture, instability, and a lifting mechanism, and their severity depends
substantially on vertical wind shear. In weak shear, the downdraught falls into the updraught and
cuts off the storm's inflow, so the storm is short-lived. In strong shear the updraught is tilted
so the downdraught falls elsewhere, the storm sustains itself, and it can organize into a
supercell with a rotating updraught — which is the structure producing most strong tornadoes and
the largest hail.

The distinction between tropical and extratropical cyclones deserves stating because they are
different phenomena with a shared name. An extratropical cyclone is cold-cored and derives its
energy from horizontal temperature contrasts, so it requires a front and it strengthens with the
contrast. A tropical cyclone is warm-cored and derives its energy from latent heat released by
condensation of water evaporated from a warm ocean, so it requires warm water and it is destroyed
by the wind shear that extratropical systems require. Their structures, energy sources,
forecasting methods, and hazards differ accordingly.

atmosphere is chaotic, which means small differences in the initial state grow exponentially.

The mechanism: whatever error exists in your initial conditions — and there is always some, since
observations are finite in number and imperfect in accuracy — doubles roughly every day and a half.
An error too small to measure grows to dominate the forecast after enough doublings, and the number
of doublings available before the error reaches the size of the signal is what sets the horizon.
At current accuracy that is roughly seven to fourteen days depending on the situation and the
quantity being forecast.

The critical property is that improving the initial conditions helps only logarithmically. Halving
the initial error buys one additional doubling time — about a day and a half of forecast horizon.
Reducing it by a factor of a thousand buys about ten doublings, or two weeks. There is no
achievable observational improvement that extends the horizon substantially, which is why this is a
property of the atmosphere rather than of the forecasting system.

The response is to forecast probabilistically rather than deterministically. Run an ensemble of
forecasts from slightly perturbed initial conditions and slightly perturbed model formulations, and
the spread among them estimates the uncertainty. Where the members agree, confidence is high; where
they diverge, the atmosphere is in a sensitive state and the forecast is genuinely uncertain. This
is more useful than a single forecast even at short range, because it distinguishes a confident
prediction from an uncertain one.

Two things remain predictable well beyond the weather horizon. Climate — the statistics of the
atmosphere rather than its specific state — is a boundary value problem rather than an initial
value problem, so it is predictable in ways individual weather is not. And slowly varying boundary
conditions such as sea surface temperatures give seasonal predictability of anomaly statistics,
which is why El Niño's effects can be anticipated months ahead while the weather on a particular
day cannot.

## Forecasting and Air Quality

Numerical weather prediction solves the governing equations on a grid, and its accuracy has
improved steadily for decades — a modern five-day forecast is about as accurate as a three-day
forecast was thirty years ago. The improvements come from higher resolution, better representation
of processes too small to resolve, more and better observations, and improved methods for
combining observations with the model's prior state.

Data assimilation is the step that receives the least attention and contributes the most. The
model's forecast provides a prior estimate of the atmosphere's state, observations provide
information about it, and assimilation combines them weighted by their respective uncertainties.
The number of observations is far smaller than the number of model variables, so the model's prior
does most of the work, and the assimilation determines how effectively each observation
constrains the state.

Air quality is a separate atmospheric concern with its own dynamics, and the key insight is that
the pollutant concentration depends on the emission rate divided by the volume it disperses into,
so meteorology matters as much as emissions. A stable atmosphere with a shallow mixing layer
concentrates the same emissions into a fraction of the volume, which is why pollution episodes
coincide with stagnant high-pressure conditions and with temperature inversions.

The ozone control paradox deserves the closing note because it demonstrates why atmospheric
chemistry requires modelling rather than intuition. Ground-level ozone forms from nitrogen oxides
and volatile organic compounds reacting under sunlight, so reducing nitrogen oxides seems
straightforwardly beneficial. In regions where volatile organics are the limiting reactant,
reducing nitrogen oxides increases ozone, because those oxides also participate in reactions that
destroy it. Which regime a given location is in depends on the ratio of the two precursors and
varies by location, by season, and by time of day — so an effective control strategy requires
determining the regime, and a strategy applied on general reasoning can worsen the problem it
addresses.
