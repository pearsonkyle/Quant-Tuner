## Origins and the Failure of Classical Physics

Quantum mechanics was forced on physics by a set of experimental facts that classical
theory could not accommodate, and it is worth knowing which ones, because each isolates a
distinct classical assumption that had to be abandoned.

Blackbody radiation was the first. Classical statistical mechanics, applying equipartition
to the electromagnetic modes in a cavity, predicts energy density rising without bound at
short wavelength — the ultraviolet catastrophe. Planck resolved it in 1900 by assuming
energy exchange occurs in discrete quanta proportional to frequency, E = h nu, which
suppresses the high-frequency modes because a single quantum there costs more energy than
is thermally available. Planck regarded this as a mathematical device; it was the first
appearance of the quantum.

The photoelectric effect isolated a different assumption. Light striking a metal ejects
electrons, and classically the electron energy should depend on intensity while the
emission threshold should not depend on frequency. Both predictions fail: electron energy
depends on frequency alone, intensity affects only the number, and below a threshold
frequency no electrons are emitted regardless of intensity. Einstein's 1905 explanation
treated light itself as quantized into photons, each interacting with one electron, which
made the quantum a property of radiation rather than of exchange.

Atomic spectra provided the third. Atoms emit and absorb only at discrete frequencies, and
classical electrodynamics is worse than merely unable to explain this — it predicts that
an orbiting electron, being accelerated, radiates continuously and spirals into the nucleus
in about ten picoseconds. That matter is stable at all is inexplicable classically. Bohr's
1913 model postulated stable orbits with quantized angular momentum, reproducing hydrogen's
spectrum exactly while offering no reason why such orbits should exist.

The experimental foundations:
  blackbody spectrum      quantized energy exchange; E = h nu
  photoelectric effect    light is quantized; KE_max = h nu - work function
  atomic spectra          discrete energy levels; the Rydberg formula
  Compton scattering      photons carry momentum p = h/lambda and scatter as
                          particles
  electron diffraction    matter exhibits interference; de Broglie lambda = h/p
  specific heats          heat capacity falls at low temperature as modes freeze
                          out; classical equipartition fails
  stability of matter     classical atoms would collapse in ~1e-11 s

De Broglie's 1924 hypothesis that matter has wave properties, with wavelength h divided by
momentum, unified these. It explains Bohr's quantization condition as the requirement that
an integer number of wavelengths fit the orbit, and it was confirmed directly by electron
diffraction from crystals. The wavelength is tiny for macroscopic objects — a thrown
baseball has a de Broglie wavelength around 1e-34 metres, far smaller than any aperture —
which is precisely why wave behaviour is invisible in ordinary experience.

Q: Increasing the intensity of light below a metal's threshold frequency produces:
A. More electrons with the same energy
B. Fewer electrons with more energy
C. No electrons at all
D. Electrons only after a delay
Reasoning: Each photon carries energy h nu, and an electron is ejected only if a single
photon supplies at least the work function. Below threshold no individual photon suffices,
and intensity increases the number of photons rather than their individual energy. The
classical prediction — that enough accumulated energy would eventually eject an electron —
is not observed, which is exactly the evidence for quantization. Answer: C.

## Wave Functions and the Schrodinger Equation

The state of a quantum system is described by a wave function, a complex-valued function
whose squared modulus gives the probability density for finding the particle at a given
position. This is the Born rule, and it is a postulate rather than a derived result. The
wave function must be normalized so total probability is one, and it must be
single-valued, continuous, and have a continuous first derivative wherever the potential is
finite — conditions that, applied to a bound system, are what produce discrete energy
levels.

The Schrodinger equation governs the wave function's evolution. The time-dependent form
describes how a state evolves; the time-independent form is an eigenvalue equation whose
solutions are the stationary states with definite energy. Stationary states are not static
— the wave function's phase rotates — but every measurable quantity is time-independent,
which is why they are the natural basis for describing atoms.

Quantization emerges from boundary conditions, and this is the conceptual core worth
holding onto. There is no separate quantization postulate; solving a differential equation
subject to the requirement that the solution be normalizable and satisfy conditions at the
boundaries admits solutions only at particular energies, exactly as a vibrating string
fixed at both ends supports only particular frequencies. Discreteness is a wave phenomenon
under confinement, not a mysterious additional rule.

Foundational relations:
  time-dependent SE     i hbar dPsi/dt = H Psi
  time-independent SE   H psi = E psi
  Hamiltonian           H = -hbar^2/(2m) lap + V
  Born rule             probability density = |Psi|^2
  normalization         integral of |Psi|^2 over all space = 1
  de Broglie            lambda = h / p
  Planck-Einstein       E = h nu = hbar omega
  uncertainty           delta_x delta_p >= hbar/2;  delta_E delta_t >= hbar/2
  expectation value     <A> = integral Psi* A Psi
  superposition         any linear combination of solutions is a solution

The uncertainty principle is not a statement about measurement disturbance, though that
is how it is usually first introduced. It is a mathematical property of conjugate
variables: position and momentum wave functions are Fourier transforms of each other, and
a function narrow in one domain is necessarily broad in the other. A particle does not
have a definite position and momentum that we are merely unable to measure simultaneously;
the state itself does not assign definite values to both. This distinction matters
because it is what makes quantum mechanics different in kind from a classical theory with
measurement limitations.

## Operators, Measurement, and the Formalism

Physical observables correspond to Hermitian operators, whose eigenvalues are real —
which is required, since measurement outcomes are real numbers — and whose eigenvectors
form a complete orthonormal basis. Any state can therefore be expanded in the eigenbasis
of any observable, and the coefficients of that expansion determine the probabilities of
each outcome through the Born rule. Measuring the observable yields one eigenvalue, with
probability equal to the squared modulus of its coefficient, and leaves the system in the
corresponding eigenstate.

That last clause is the measurement postulate, and it is where quantum mechanics becomes
philosophically contentious. Between measurements the state evolves deterministically and
reversibly under the Schrodinger equation. At measurement it changes discontinuously,
probabilistically, and irreversibly. These are two entirely different kinds of evolution,
and the theory as ordinarily stated does not specify what constitutes a measurement — the
measurement problem, which every interpretation of quantum mechanics is an attempt to
address.

Commutators encode compatibility. Two observables whose operators commute share a common
eigenbasis and can be simultaneously determined; observables that do not commute cannot.
The canonical commutation relation between position and momentum is what generates the
uncertainty relation between them, through a general theorem bounding the product of
uncertainties by half the magnitude of the expectation of the commutator. Compatibility is
therefore an algebraic property of the operators rather than a practical limitation of
apparatus.

Formalism reference:
  Hermitian operator     A = A-dagger;  real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors
  commutator             [A, B] = AB - BA
  canonical relation     [x, p] = i hbar
  angular momentum       [L_x, L_y] = i hbar L_z, cyclic
  compatible observables commute; simultaneously measurable
  general uncertainty    delta_A delta_B >= |<[A,B]>| / 2
  Dirac notation         |psi> a state, <psi| its dual, <phi|psi> an inner product
  completeness           sum over n of |n><n| = identity
  Ehrenfest theorem      expectation values obey classical equations of motion
  correspondence         quantum results approach classical ones at large quantum
                         numbers

Ehrenfest's theorem and the correspondence principle together explain why the classical
world looks classical. The expectation values of position and momentum obey Newton's
equations, so the centre of a wave packet follows a classical trajectory. For macroscopic
objects the wave packet is extraordinarily narrow relative to any relevant scale, so the
spread is unobservable. Decoherence completes the picture: a macroscopic system interacts
constantly with its environment, and that interaction destroys the phase relationships
needed for interference on timescales far shorter than any observation, which is why
superpositions of macroscopically distinct states are never seen.

## Canonical Systems

A small set of exactly solvable systems provides the intuition applied everywhere else.

The infinite square well confines a particle to a region with impenetrable walls. The wave
function must vanish at the boundaries, which admits only sinusoidal solutions with an
integer number of half-wavelengths, giving energies proportional to n squared. Two
features generalize: the ground state energy is nonzero, because a wave function confined
to a finite region must have curvature and therefore kinetic energy, and the level spacing
increases with confinement, which is why quantum dots emit at wavelengths tunable by their
physical size.

The harmonic oscillator, with a quadratic potential, has evenly spaced levels separated by
hbar omega with a ground state energy of half that. It is the most important solvable
system because any smooth potential near a minimum is approximately quadratic, so it
describes molecular vibrations, phonons in solids, and — when quantized field modes are
treated this way — photons themselves. Its zero-point energy is physically real, observable
in the residual motion of atoms at absolute zero and in the Casimir force between
conducting plates.

The hydrogen atom is the only atom solvable exactly, and its solution explains the
periodic table's structure. The Coulomb potential yields energies depending only on the
principal quantum number, giving the observed spectrum, while the angular part produces
the orbital shapes labelled s, p, d, and f. The accidental degeneracy in the orbital
quantum number is specific to the exact inverse-square potential and is broken in
multi-electron atoms by electron-electron screening, which is why the filling order
follows the observed pattern rather than simple principal-quantum-number order.

Solved systems and their signatures:
  infinite square well   E proportional to n^2;  nonzero ground state
  finite well            finitely many bound states; wave function penetrates the
                         classically forbidden region
  harmonic oscillator    E = (n + 1/2) hbar omega;  evenly spaced, zero-point energy
  rigid rotor            E proportional to J(J+1);  molecular rotation spectra
  hydrogen atom          E = -13.6 eV / n^2;  quantum numbers n, l, m, s
  free particle          continuous spectrum; plane waves, not normalizable
  potential barrier      tunnelling; transmission falls exponentially with barrier
                         width and with the square root of (V - E)
  periodic potential     energy bands and gaps; the foundation of solid-state
                         physics

Tunnelling has no classical analogue and is technologically central. A particle with
energy below a barrier has a nonzero probability of appearing on the far side, because the
wave function decays exponentially inside the barrier rather than vanishing. The
exponential dependence on barrier width is what makes the scanning tunnelling microscope
work — a change of one atomic diameter in tip height changes the current by about an order
of magnitude, giving atomic resolution. It is also what allows nuclear fusion in stellar
cores at temperatures far below the Coulomb barrier, what limits how thin a transistor's
gate oxide can be made before leakage dominates, and what drives alpha decay.

## Spin, Identical Particles, and Entanglement

Spin is intrinsic angular momentum with no classical counterpart. It is not rotation of a
physical object — a treatment of the electron as a spinning sphere requires a surface
speed exceeding light — but an intrinsic property as fundamental as charge or mass. It was
forced on the theory by the Stern-Gerlach experiment, in which a beam of silver atoms
passing through an inhomogeneous magnetic field splits into exactly two components rather
than a continuous spread, demonstrating a two-valued angular momentum with no orbital
explanation.

The spin quantum number determines a particle's statistics, through the spin-statistics
theorem. Half-integer spin particles are fermions, obey the Pauli exclusion principle, and
have antisymmetric multi-particle wave functions. Integer spin particles are bosons, can
occupy the same state without limit, and have symmetric wave functions. This division
explains an enormous range of phenomena: the exclusion principle is why electrons fill
successive atomic shells rather than collapsing into the ground state, hence why chemistry
exists, why matter is rigid, and why white dwarfs and neutron stars are supported against
gravity. Bosonic statistics permit lasers, superfluidity, and Bose-Einstein condensation.

Entanglement arises when a multi-particle state cannot be written as a product of
individual states. Measuring one particle then determines the outcome for the other,
regardless of separation. Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen argued in 1935 that this implied
quantum mechanics was incomplete — that the particles must carry predetermined values, or
hidden variables, and that the correlation merely revealed them.

Bell's theorem settled the question. Bell showed in 1964 that any theory in which
particles carry predetermined local values must satisfy an inequality constraining the
correlations across different measurement settings, and that quantum mechanics predicts
violations of it. Experiments since the 1970s, progressively closing the detection and
locality loopholes, consistently violate Bell inequalities by wide margins. Local realism
is not merely unnecessary; it is ruled out by experiment. Crucially, entanglement does not
permit faster-than-light signalling — the local outcomes are random, and the correlation
is only visible after comparing results through a classical channel.

Spin and entanglement essentials:
  electron spin          s = 1/2;  two states, conventionally up and down
  Stern-Gerlach          discrete splitting; direct evidence for spin quantization
  Pauli exclusion        no two identical fermions in the same quantum state
  fermions               half-integer spin; electrons, protons, neutrons, quarks
  bosons                 integer spin; photons, gluons, the Higgs, helium-4 atoms
  Bell inequality        bounds correlations under local hidden variables
  experimental result    violated; local realism excluded
  no-signalling          entanglement transmits no information
  no-cloning             an unknown quantum state cannot be copied; the basis of
                         quantum key distribution's security
  decoherence            environmental entanglement destroys interference, producing
                         apparently classical behaviour

## Interpretation and Applications

The mathematics of quantum mechanics is unambiguous and extraordinarily well confirmed;
what it says about reality is not settled. Interpretations agree on every prediction and
differ on what the wave function is and what happens at measurement.

The Copenhagen interpretation treats the wave function as a computational device encoding
knowledge, with measurement causing collapse and the classical/quantum boundary left
unspecified. Many-worlds takes the Schrodinger equation as complete, denies collapse, and
accepts that all outcomes occur in branching non-interacting components of a universal
wave function. Pilot-wave theory restores determinism with particles having definite
positions guided by a wave, at the cost of explicit non-locality. QBism treats the wave
function as an agent's subjective degrees of belief. Objective collapse theories modify
the dynamics so that collapse is a physical process, which makes them experimentally
distinguishable in principle and increasingly constrained in practice.

Because they agree on predictions, choosing among them is not an empirical question with
current technology, and treating one as established rather than as a framework is a
common overreach. What is established is the formalism and its predictions, verified to
extraordinary precision — the electron's magnetic moment agrees with quantum
electrodynamics to twelve significant figures, the most accurate confirmed prediction in
science.

The applications are what make quantum mechanics the most economically consequential
theory ever developed. The transistor depends on band structure and doping, which are
quantum solid-state physics. The laser depends on stimulated emission and population
inversion. Magnetic resonance imaging depends on nuclear spin transitions. Flash memory
depends on tunnelling. Light-emitting diodes, photovoltaics, atomic clocks — and hence
satellite navigation — all rest directly on quantum mechanics. A large fraction of the
modern economy is downstream of a theory developed to explain spectral lines.

Quantum information is the current frontier. A qubit can be in a superposition of two
states, and n qubits span a state space of dimension 2^n, which is the source of quantum
computing's potential advantage. Specific algorithms exploit this for specific problems:
Shor's algorithm factors integers in polynomial time, threatening widely deployed public
key cryptography; Grover's provides a quadratic speedup for unstructured search;
simulation of quantum systems is the application where advantage is most clearly
established, since simulating quantum mechanics classically requires exponential
resources.

The practical obstacle is decoherence. Qubits lose their quantum properties through
environmental interaction on timescales from microseconds to seconds depending on the
physical implementation, and computation must complete or be error-corrected within that
window. Quantum error correction is possible in principle — the threshold theorem
guarantees that arbitrarily long computation is achievable if the physical error rate is
below a threshold — but it requires many physical qubits per logical one, with current
estimates ranging from hundreds to thousands. This overhead, rather than any question of
principle, is what separates present devices from cryptographically relevant ones, and it
is why the field's near-term applications concentrate on simulation and optimization
heuristics rather than on the algorithms that motivated the field.
