## Qubits, States, and Measurement

A classical bit is zero or one; a qubit is a superposition of both, described by two complex
amplitudes whose squared magnitudes give the probabilities of each outcome on measurement. The
amplitudes carry a relative phase as well as magnitudes, and that phase has no classical analogue —
it is what makes interference possible and it is where quantum computing's advantage originates.

The state of n qubits requires two to the power n amplitudes to describe, which is the source of both
the promise and the persistent misunderstanding. The exponential state space does not mean a quantum
computer evaluates all possibilities in parallel and reads out the best one. Measurement collapses
the state and returns a single outcome sampled from the probability distribution, so the algorithmic
work is arranging interference so that amplitudes for wrong answers cancel and those for right
answers reinforce. Algorithms that fail to do this gain nothing from superposition.

Measurement is destructive and basis-dependent. Measuring in the computational basis returns zero or
one and destroys the superposition; measuring in a different basis asks a different question and
returns a different distribution. This is not ignorance being revealed — the pre-measurement state
genuinely lacks a definite value, as the violation of classical correlation bounds establishes
experimentally.

Q: A quantum algorithm places a register in an equal superposition of all inputs and evaluates a
function. Does this find the desired input efficiently?
A. Yes; all inputs are evaluated simultaneously
B. No — measurement returns one uniformly random outcome, so this gains nothing over classical
   random guessing; an advantage requires subsequent operations that concentrate amplitude on the
   desired outcome through interference
C. Only for small inputs
D. Yes, with enough measurements
Reasoning: Superposition and function evaluation are the easy parts. The hard and essential part is
arranging the amplitudes so the measurement is likely to return what you want. Every quantum
algorithm with a proven advantage does something specific here, and the absence of that step is what
distinguishes a genuine algorithm from the naive picture. Answer: B.

## Gates, Circuits, and Entanglement

Quantum gates are unitary operations, which means they are reversible and preserve total probability.
Single-qubit gates rotate the state on the Bloch sphere; two-qubit gates create correlations between
qubits. A small set of gates suffices to approximate any unitary operation to arbitrary accuracy,
which is the quantum analogue of classical universality, and the standard sets combine a few
single-qubit rotations with one entangling two-qubit gate.

Entanglement is the correlation between qubits that has no classical description: a joint state that
cannot be written as a product of individual states. Measuring one qubit of an entangled pair
immediately determines the correlated outcome for the other regardless of separation, and this does
not permit signalling because the individual outcome is random and only the correlation — visible
after classical communication — carries the structure.

Circuits are read as time flowing left to right, with wires as qubits and boxes as gates. Circuit
depth is the number of sequential gate layers and is the quantity that matters for a noisy machine,
since decoherence accumulates with time rather than with gate count. Compilation maps an abstract
circuit onto a physical device's available gates and connectivity, and on a device where only
neighbouring qubits interact, this inserts swap operations that can multiply the depth substantially
— which is why device connectivity is as important a specification as qubit count.

## Algorithms and Where the Advantage Lies

The known quantum algorithms with proven advantages fall into a few families and the list is shorter
than public discussion suggests. Period-finding underlies the factoring algorithm, which would break
widely deployed public key cryptography and provides an exponential speedup over the best known
classical methods. Amplitude amplification gives a quadratic speedup for unstructured search, which
is provably optimal for that problem and is a much more modest advantage than exponential.

Simulation of quantum systems is the application with the clearest physical motivation and possibly
the earliest practical value. Simulating the dynamics of a many-body quantum system on a classical
computer costs exponentially in the system size for general cases, and a quantum computer does it
naturally, which is the original motivation for the field. Chemistry, materials, and nuclear physics
are the target domains.

Linear algebra and optimization applications are more equivocal. Algorithms exist with impressive
asymptotic claims, and their preconditions are restrictive — data must be loaded into quantum states
efficiently, matrices must be well-conditioned and sparse, and the output is a quantum state from
which only limited information can be extracted. Several results initially presented as quantum
speedups have been matched by improved classical algorithms inspired by them, which is a healthy
scientific outcome and a caution about reading asymptotic claims without their conditions.

## Decoherence and Error Correction

Quantum states are fragile. Any interaction with the environment entangles the system with it,
which destroys the phase relationships the computation depends on. Decoherence times — how long a
qubit maintains its state and its phase — bound the circuit depth achievable, and the ratio of
decoherence time to gate time is the figure of merit that determines how much computation fits within
coherence.

Error correction is what would make arbitrary-length computation possible, and it is harder than the
classical case for two reasons. States cannot be copied, so the classical strategy of redundant
copies is unavailable. And errors are continuous rather than discrete, since a qubit can rotate by
any small angle. Both are addressed by the same insight: encoding logical information across many
physical qubits and measuring parity-type operators that reveal whether an error occurred without
revealing the encoded state, which also projects continuous errors onto a discrete set.

The threshold theorem states that if physical error rates are below a threshold, arbitrarily long
computation is possible with overhead growing only polylogarithmically in the length. This is the
theoretical foundation of the whole enterprise. The practical difficulty is the overhead: current
codes require on the order of a thousand physical qubits per logical qubit at realistic error rates,
so a machine running an algorithm requiring thousands of logical qubits needs millions of physical
ones, and closing that gap is the central engineering challenge.

## Hardware Platforms and Assessment

The platforms competing to build quantum computers make different tradeoffs. Superconducting
circuits operate at millikelvin temperatures, have fast gates, and have limited connectivity and
relatively short coherence times. Trapped ions have excellent coherence and fidelity and
all-to-all connectivity within a trap, with much slower gates and a scaling challenge in connecting
traps. Neutral atoms in optical tweezers offer flexible reconfigurable connectivity at large qubit
counts. Photonic approaches operate at room temperature with the difficulty that photons do not
interact directly. Spin qubits in semiconductors promise manufacturing leverage and lag in
fidelity.

No platform is clearly ahead on every axis, and comparing them requires looking past qubit count.
The relevant specifications are two-qubit gate fidelity, coherence time relative to gate time,
connectivity, measurement fidelity, and the demonstrated ability to scale without fidelity degrading.
A machine with many qubits and poor two-qubit fidelity performs worse than one with fewer good ones,
because circuit success probability falls exponentially with the number of gates.

The honest assessment of the field's status is that quantum computers exist, run algorithms, and do
not yet perform any commercially valuable task better than classical computers. Demonstrations of
advantage have been on contrived sampling problems chosen to be hard classically, several have been
substantially eroded by better classical simulation, and the useful applications require error
correction at a scale not yet built. This does not diminish the achievement or the prospects; it
distinguishes what has been demonstrated from what has been projected, and that distinction is what
allows a realistic assessment of when to invest attention.
