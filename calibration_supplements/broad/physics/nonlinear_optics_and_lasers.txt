## Laser Fundamentals

A laser produces light through stimulated emission, in which a photon passing an excited atom
triggers the emission of a second photon identical in frequency, phase, direction, and
polarization. This copying process is what distinguishes laser light from thermal light: the
photons are coherent, so the output is directional, monochromatic, and capable of being focused to
a spot limited only by diffraction.

Three components are required. A gain medium supplies the excited atoms; a pump supplies the energy
to excite them; and a resonator — typically two mirrors — returns light through the gain medium
repeatedly so that amplification compounds. Oscillation begins when the round-trip gain exceeds the
round-trip loss, which defines the threshold, and above threshold the output rises approximately
linearly with pump power while the internal field clamps the gain at the loss value.

Population inversion — more atoms in the upper state than the lower — is required for net
amplification, and it cannot be achieved in a two-level system by optical pumping, because the same
transition that excites also stimulates emission and the populations equalize at best. Three-level
and four-level schemes solve this by using separate transitions for pumping and lasing, and the
four-level arrangement is markedly better because its lower laser level empties rapidly, so
inversion is achieved at low pump power rather than requiring more than half the population to be
excited.

Q: A laser's output power rises steeply above a certain pump power and is essentially zero below
it. This threshold occurs because:
A. The gain medium activates suddenly
B. Below it, round-trip gain is less than round-trip loss, so oscillation cannot build; above it,
   the field grows until gain saturates back down to equal the loss
C. The mirrors change reflectivity
D. The pump is nonlinear
Reasoning: Laser oscillation requires net round-trip gain exceeding unity. Below that condition
spontaneous emission is not amplified into a coherent field. Above it the intracavity intensity
grows until saturation clamps the gain at exactly the loss, and additional pump power appears as
output. Answer: B.

## Cavity Modes and Beam Quality

The resonator supports discrete modes, and they divide into two families. Longitudinal modes are
the standing-wave patterns along the cavity axis, spaced in frequency by the inverse round-trip
time, and a typical cavity supports many of them within the gain bandwidth. Transverse modes
describe the intensity pattern across the beam, and the fundamental one has a Gaussian profile that
is the most useful in nearly every application.

A Gaussian beam has a specific propagation behaviour that must be understood to use lasers
correctly. It has a minimum radius at the waist, expands hyperbolically away from it, and
approaches a constant far-field divergence angle inversely proportional to the waist size. Tight
focusing and low divergence are therefore in direct opposition — a beam focused to a small spot
diverges rapidly, and a collimated beam cannot be focused arbitrarily small.

Beam quality quantifies departure from the ideal Gaussian, expressed as a factor describing how much
worse the beam's focusability is than the diffraction limit. It matters because it determines the
minimum achievable spot size and therefore the achievable intensity, and a high-power laser with
poor beam quality can be less useful for cutting or for coupling into a fibre than a lower-power
one with a clean mode. Power alone is not the specification that matters.

## Pulsed Operation

Continuous operation is limited in peak power by the pump. Pulsed operation concentrates energy in
time, achieving peak powers orders of magnitude above the average, and the two dominant techniques
achieve very different regimes.

Q-switching holds the cavity loss high while the gain medium accumulates inversion, then abruptly
lowers it, releasing the stored energy in a single short pulse. The pulse duration is set by the
cavity round-trip time and the gain, typically nanoseconds, and the peak power is the stored energy
divided by that duration — kilowatts to megawatts from a modest average power. It is the standard
approach for material processing, ranging, and pumping other nonlinear processes.

Mode-locking is different in mechanism and produces much shorter pulses. If the many longitudinal
modes of a cavity are forced into a fixed phase relationship, they interfere constructively at one
point per round trip and destructively elsewhere, producing a single circulating pulse whose
duration is inversely proportional to the number of locked modes. A broad gain bandwidth therefore
permits a short pulse, which is why titanium-doped sapphire with its enormous bandwidth is the
classic ultrafast medium and why femtosecond pulses require materials chosen for bandwidth rather
than for efficiency.

## Nonlinear Optics and Frequency Conversion

At ordinary intensities a material's polarization response to a field is linear, which is why
optical frequencies do not mix and superposition holds. At the intensities lasers provide the
response acquires higher-order terms, and those terms couple different frequencies, allowing light
to generate light at new frequencies. Nonlinear optics is the study of these processes and it is
what makes laser wavelengths available outside the wavelengths that lasing media naturally provide.

Second-order processes require a material without inversion symmetry, which restricts them to
certain crystal classes. Second harmonic generation converts two photons into one at double the
frequency, and it is the standard way to reach green from infrared. Sum and difference frequency
generation combine two inputs. Parametric processes split one photon into two, and an optical
parametric oscillator built around this is a widely tunable source.

Phase matching is the practical requirement that determines whether any of these work. The generated
wave must stay in phase with the driving polarization over the interaction length, which requires
the refractive indices at the involved frequencies to satisfy a specific relationship. Since
dispersion normally prevents this, the standard techniques exploit birefringence — using different
polarizations whose indices can be matched by choosing the crystal angle — or quasi-phase-matching,
which periodically reverses the crystal's nonlinear coefficient so that the accumulated phase error
resets before it cancels the output.

## Ultrafast Optics and Applications

Ultrashort pulses have properties that follow directly from their bandwidth. A pulse of femtosecond
duration necessarily spans a broad spectrum, and any dispersive element stretches it because
different frequencies travel at different speeds. Managing this dispersion is the central practical
task in ultrafast optics, and it requires compensating elements — prism or grating pairs, chirped
mirrors — that introduce the opposite sign of dispersion.

The peak intensities available from short pulses open regimes inaccessible otherwise. Material
processing with ultrashort pulses removes material before heat diffuses out of the interaction
region, producing clean features with no melted rim, which is why they are used for precision
machining and for surgery on transparent tissue. High harmonic generation drives electrons in a gas
so violently that they radiate at very high harmonic orders, producing coherent extreme ultraviolet
light and attosecond pulses that resolve electron motion.

Frequency combs connect the optical and microwave domains and earned recognition as a
transformative measurement tool. A mode-locked laser's spectrum is a comb of evenly spaced lines
whose spacing is the repetition rate and whose offset can be measured and stabilized. Once both are
locked to a radio-frequency reference, every optical line has a precisely known frequency, which
turns optical frequency measurement from a heroic undertaking into a routine one and is what made
optical atomic clocks practical.
