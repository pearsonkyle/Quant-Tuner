## Crystal Structure and Bonding

Condensed matter physics studies the emergent behaviour of enormous numbers of interacting
atoms, and its recurring theme is that collective properties are not simply the sum of atomic
ones. Superconductivity, magnetism, and the very existence of electrical conductors and
insulators are properties of the assembly rather than of any constituent, which is why the
field's characteristic slogan is that more is different.

A crystal is a periodic arrangement of atoms, described by a lattice specifying the repeating
translational pattern and a basis specifying what sits at each lattice point. Fourteen Bravais
lattices exhaust the possible three-dimensional periodicities, and the common metallic and
semiconductor structures are a small subset: face-centred cubic and hexagonal close-packed
achieve the densest possible sphere packing at 74 percent, body-centred cubic is slightly
less dense and common in transition metals, and the diamond structure — two interpenetrating
face-centred cubic lattices — is what silicon and germanium adopt.

The reciprocal lattice is the Fourier transform of the direct lattice and is where most
physics is done, because waves in a periodic medium are naturally described by wavevector.
Diffraction occurs when the scattering vector equals a reciprocal lattice vector, which is
Bragg's law in reciprocal space, and this is how crystal structures are determined
experimentally. The Brillouin zone, the primitive cell of the reciprocal lattice, is the
region within which all distinct wave states lie, and electronic band structures and phonon
dispersions are always plotted along high-symmetry paths within it.

Structure and bonding:
  Bravais lattices     14 distinct three-dimensional periodicities
  FCC / HCP            close packed, 74% filling, coordination 12
  BCC                  coordination 8; many transition metals
  diamond structure    tetrahedral coordination; Si, Ge, C
  basis                what decorates each lattice point
  reciprocal lattice   Fourier dual; where wave physics lives
  Brillouin zone       primitive cell of the reciprocal lattice
  Bragg condition      2d sin(theta) = n lambda
  ionic bonding        electron transfer; hard, brittle, high melting, insulating
                       until molten
  covalent bonding     shared pairs, directional; hard, often semiconducting
  metallic bonding     delocalized electron sea; ductile, conducting, opaque
  van der Waals        induced dipoles; weak, soft, low melting; holds layered
                       materials together
  hydrogen bonding     strong dipolar; water's anomalies, biomolecular structure

Bond type predicts bulk properties with reasonable reliability, and the reasoning is
mechanical. Metallic bonding is non-directional, so planes of atoms can slide past one
another without breaking bonds, which is why metals are ductile. Covalent bonding is
directional, so displacement breaks bonds and the material fractures, which is why covalent
crystals are hard and brittle. Ionic crystals are brittle for a related reason: sliding brings
like charges into contact and the crystal cleaves. Van der Waals bonding between layers with
covalent bonding within them gives materials such as graphite that are simultaneously very
strong in plane and easily sheared between planes.

Q: Metals are ductile while ionic crystals are brittle primarily because:
A. Metals have lower melting points
B. Metallic bonding is non-directional, so planes can slide without breaking bonds, while
   sliding an ionic lattice brings like charges together
C. Metals are less dense
D. Ionic crystals have stronger bonds
Reasoning: The mechanism is what happens under shear. The delocalized electron sea in a metal
maintains bonding regardless of relative atomic positions, permitting dislocation motion.
Displacing an ionic lattice by one lattice spacing aligns cations with cations, producing
strong repulsion and cleavage. Bond strength alone does not determine ductility. Answer: B.

## Lattice Vibrations and Thermal Properties

Atoms in a crystal vibrate about their equilibrium positions, and the normal modes of that
vibration are called phonons when quantized. The dispersion relation between frequency and
wavevector has acoustic branches, in which neighbouring atoms move in phase and the frequency
goes to zero at long wavelength, and optical branches when the basis contains more than one
atom, in which atoms within a cell move against each other at finite frequency even at zero
wavevector.

Phonons carry heat in insulators and contribute in metals, and their scattering determines
thermal conductivity. At low temperature, scattering from boundaries and defects dominates
and conductivity rises with temperature as more modes are populated; at high temperature,
phonon-phonon scattering dominates and conductivity falls as one over temperature. The peak
between them is characteristic, and high thermal conductivity requires both few defects and
weak anharmonicity — which is why isotopically pure diamond has the highest thermal
conductivity of any bulk material, exceeding copper by a factor of five despite having no
free electrons at all.

Heat capacity was one of the early triumphs of quantum theory. Classical equipartition
predicts a constant molar heat capacity of 3R for a solid, the Dulong-Petit value, which is
observed at high temperature and fails badly at low temperature where the measured capacity
falls toward zero. Einstein's model, treating all modes as having the same frequency,
captured the qualitative behaviour; Debye's model, treating the modes as a continuum up to a
cutoff, gives the correct cubic temperature dependence at low temperature. The physical reason
is that modes with energy quanta exceeding the thermal energy cannot be excited and are frozen
out.

Thermal properties:
  phonon                quantized lattice vibration
  acoustic branch       in-phase motion; frequency to zero at long wavelength
  optical branch        out-of-phase within the cell; finite frequency at zero k
  Debye temperature     the characteristic scale; above it, classical behaviour
  Dulong-Petit          3R molar heat capacity at high temperature
  Debye T^3 law         low-temperature heat capacity of a lattice
  electronic heat cap.  linear in T in a metal; only electrons near the Fermi
                        level participate
  thermal expansion     arises from bond potential anharmonicity; a perfectly
                        harmonic solid would not expand
  thermal conductivity  phonons in insulators, electrons in metals
  Wiedemann-Franz       the ratio of thermal to electrical conductivity in a metal
                        is proportional to temperature with a universal constant
  Umklapp scattering    phonon collisions that reverse momentum; what gives a
                        crystal finite thermal resistance at all

Thermal expansion deserves note because its origin is easy to miss. If the interatomic
potential were exactly harmonic, increasing vibrational amplitude would not shift the average
separation, and the solid would not expand. Real potentials are asymmetric — steeply repulsive
at short range and gently attractive at long — so larger amplitude shifts the time-averaged
position outward. Expansion is therefore a direct consequence of anharmonicity, and materials
engineered for near-zero expansion achieve it by structural mechanisms that compensate rather
than by making bonds harmonic.

## Electronic Structure and Semiconductors

The behaviour of electrons in a periodic potential is the central problem of the field, and
its resolution explains the existence of metals, insulators, and semiconductors. Bloch's
theorem states that electron states in a periodic potential take the form of a plane wave
modulated by a function with the lattice periodicity, which means an electron in a perfect
crystal propagates without scattering — resistance comes from deviations from periodicity,
not from the atoms themselves.

Energy bands arise because the periodic potential opens gaps at Brillouin zone boundaries,
where the electron wavelength satisfies the Bragg condition and forward and backward waves
mix. Whether a material conducts depends on where the electrons fill. A partially filled band
means states are available at infinitesimally higher energy, so an applied field accelerates
electrons and the material conducts — a metal. A completely filled band separated by a gap
from the next means no states are accessible at small energy, so the material insulates. A
small gap means thermal excitation populates the upper band appreciably at room temperature —
a semiconductor.

The semiconductor's usefulness comes from doping. Adding impurities with one more valence
electron than the host contributes electrons to the conduction band, giving n-type material;
impurities with one fewer contribute holes to the valence band, giving p-type. Carrier
concentration is thereby controlled over many orders of magnitude by adding parts per million
of impurity, and it is this controllability, together with silicon's stable native oxide, that
made silicon the basis of electronics rather than any superiority in intrinsic properties.

Electronic structure:
  Bloch state           plane wave times a lattice-periodic function
  band gap              from Bragg reflection at zone boundaries
  metal                 partially filled band
  insulator             filled band, large gap
  semiconductor         filled band, small gap; Si 1.12 eV, Ge 0.66, GaAs 1.42
  direct vs indirect    whether the band extrema share a wavevector; direct gap
                        materials emit light efficiently, indirect ones do not,
                        which is why LEDs are GaAs-based and not silicon
  effective mass        the curvature of the band; determines mobility
  hole                  the absence of an electron in a nearly full band, behaving
                        as a positive carrier
  doping                n-type donors, p-type acceptors
  Fermi level           the chemical potential; midgap for intrinsic material,
                        shifted toward the relevant band by doping
  pn junction           built-in field from carrier diffusion; the basis of diodes,
                        transistors, and photovoltaics
  depletion region      carrier-free zone at the junction; widens under reverse bias

The pn junction is the single structure from which most of semiconductor electronics is
built. Electrons diffuse from the n side and holes from the p side, leaving behind fixed
ionized dopants that create an electric field opposing further diffusion, and equilibrium is
reached when drift balances diffusion. Forward bias reduces the barrier and current flows
exponentially with voltage; reverse bias widens the depletion region and only a small leakage
current flows. Adding a third region gives a transistor, and controlling the depletion region
with an insulated gate gives the field-effect transistor that constitutes essentially all
modern digital logic.

## Magnetism and Superconductivity

Magnetism in solids arises from electron spin and orbital angular momentum, and its
collective forms come from the exchange interaction — a consequence of the Pauli exclusion
principle rather than of magnetic dipole coupling, which is far too weak to explain ordering
at room temperature. Because identical fermions must have an antisymmetric total wavefunction,
the spatial and spin parts are correlated, and the resulting energy difference between
parallel and antiparallel spin configurations is electrostatic in origin and large.

Diamagnetism is universal and weak, arising from induced currents opposing an applied field.
Paramagnetism occurs when unpaired moments align partially with a field and is disordered
without one. Ferromagnetism is spontaneous parallel alignment below the Curie temperature,
producing a moment with no applied field. Antiferromagnetism is spontaneous antiparallel
alignment with no net moment. Ferrimagnetism has antiparallel sublattices of unequal magnitude,
giving a net moment, and includes the magnetite that made lodestones and the ferrites used in
inductors.

Domain structure explains why a ferromagnet is not always magnetized. The material divides
into regions of uniform magnetization oriented so as to reduce the external field energy, and
in the demagnetized state the domains cancel. Applying a field grows favourably oriented
domains at the expense of others, and this process is partly irreversible because domain walls
pin on defects, which is the origin of hysteresis. Hard magnetic materials have many pinning
sites and retain magnetization; soft materials have few and follow the applied field, which is
what transformer cores require.

Magnetism and superconductivity:
  diamagnetism          universal, weak, opposes the field
  paramagnetism         unpaired moments, aligns partially, disordered without field
  ferromagnetism        spontaneous parallel order below the Curie temperature
  antiferromagnetism    spontaneous antiparallel order below the Neel temperature
  exchange interaction  electrostatic in origin via Pauli exclusion; the reason
                        ordering survives to high temperature
  domains               reduce external field energy; explain unmagnetized ferro-
                        magnets
  hysteresis            from domain wall pinning; area is the energy loss per cycle
  hard vs soft          permanent magnets versus transformer cores
  superconductivity     zero resistance below a critical temperature
  Meissner effect       expulsion of magnetic flux; not merely perfect conductivity
  Cooper pairs          electrons bound via phonon exchange; bosonic, condense
  BCS theory            explains conventional superconductors
  type I / type II      complete flux expulsion versus flux penetration in
                        quantized vortices; type II tolerates high fields and is
                        what practical magnets use
  high-Tc cuprates      above liquid nitrogen temperature; mechanism still not
                        settled after nearly four decades

The Meissner effect is what makes superconductivity a distinct thermodynamic phase rather
than merely the limit of vanishing resistance. A hypothetical perfect conductor would trap
whatever flux was present when it became perfectly conducting; a superconductor actively
expels flux regardless of its history, which is a statement about the equilibrium state.
This is why the transition is a genuine phase transition with a well-defined free energy and
why the superconducting state can be described by a macroscopic quantum wavefunction — the
basis of flux quantization, the Josephson effect, and superconducting quantum interference
devices sensitive enough to measure the magnetic fields produced by the brain.

## Defects, Mechanical Properties, and Low-Dimensional Systems

Real crystals contain defects, and mechanical properties are governed almost entirely by
them. Point defects — vacancies, interstitials, substitutional impurities — are
thermodynamically required at any finite temperature, since the entropy gain from disorder
outweighs the energy cost at low concentration. Line defects, dislocations, are the crucial
ones for mechanics. Planar defects include grain boundaries and stacking faults, and
volume defects include voids and precipitates.

The theoretical shear strength of a perfect crystal, computed by displacing whole planes
simultaneously, exceeds the observed strength of real metals by two to three orders of
magnitude. The resolution is dislocations: plastic deformation occurs by the propagation of a
line defect through the crystal, breaking and reforming bonds a few at a time rather than all
at once, which requires far less stress. The analogy is moving a heavy rug by propagating a
ripple across it rather than dragging the whole thing.

That mechanism immediately explains every strengthening technique in metallurgy, because all
of them impede dislocation motion. Work hardening increases dislocation density until they
obstruct each other. Grain refinement introduces boundaries that dislocations cannot cross,
giving strength increasing as the inverse square root of grain size. Solid solution
strengthening introduces size-mismatched substitutional atoms whose strain fields impede
motion. Precipitation hardening introduces second-phase particles that dislocations must cut
through or bow around. The universal tradeoff is that everything impeding dislocation motion
increases strength and decreases ductility, because ductility is dislocation motion.

Defects and mechanical behaviour:
  vacancy               thermodynamically required; enables diffusion
  dislocation           line defect; carries plastic deformation
  Burgers vector        the lattice displacement a dislocation carries
  slip system           the plane and direction of easy dislocation motion; FCC has
                        many, which is why FCC metals are ductile
  work hardening        dislocation density impedes further motion
  Hall-Petch            strength scales as inverse square root of grain size
  solid solution        size mismatch strain fields impede dislocations
  precipitation         second-phase particles obstruct motion
  strength vs ductility the universal tradeoff; both come from dislocation
                        behaviour
  creep                 time-dependent deformation at high temperature; diffusion
                        mediated
  fatigue               failure under cyclic loading well below the yield stress;
                        crack nucleation and growth
  fracture toughness    resistance to crack propagation; brittle materials fail
                        from pre-existing flaws far below theoretical strength

Low-dimensional materials have become a major subfield because confinement qualitatively
changes electronic behaviour rather than merely scaling it. Graphene, a single layer of
carbon, has a linear rather than quadratic dispersion near the Fermi level, so its charge
carriers behave as massless relativistic particles, giving extremely high mobility and an
anomalous quantum Hall effect. Transition metal dichalcogenides transition from indirect to
direct band gap in the monolayer limit, making them emissive. Quantum wells, wires, and dots
confine carriers in one, two, and three dimensions respectively, and the resulting discrete
energy levels are tunable by size — which is why quantum dot emission wavelength is set by
particle diameter and why they are used as tunable phosphors in displays.

The general principle underlying all of these is that when a dimension becomes comparable to
the electron wavelength, the continuum of states in that direction collapses into discrete
levels, and the density of states changes shape. That change in the density of states alters
optical absorption, electrical transport, and heat capacity together, which is why
low-dimensional systems are not merely small versions of bulk materials but genuinely
different ones — the same collective-behaviour lesson that opened the subject, appearing again
at a different scale.
