## Nuclear Structure

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons bound by the residual strong force, and its
existence poses an immediate question: the protons repel each other electrostatically with
enormous force at nuclear separations, so something stronger must hold them. The strong
interaction is roughly a hundred times stronger than the electromagnetic at those distances
and is very short-ranged, falling off over about a femtometre, which explains why nuclei exist
and why they cannot grow indefinitely.

That competition between short-range attraction and long-range repulsion determines nuclear
stability. Each nucleon interacts only with its immediate neighbours because the strong force
saturates, so the binding energy grows roughly linearly with nucleon count. Electrostatic
repulsion, being long-ranged, grows with the square of the proton count. As nuclei get larger
the repulsion grows faster, which is why heavy nuclei need progressively more neutrons to
remain bound and why there is an upper limit to stability.

Binding energy per nucleon is the quantity that organizes nuclear energetics. It rises steeply
for the lightest nuclei, peaks near iron-56 at about 8.8 million electronvolts per nucleon, and
declines slowly thereafter. The peak is why fusion of light nuclei releases energy and fission
of heavy ones does too, and why iron is the endpoint of stellar nucleosynthesis — beyond it,
neither process releases energy, so a stellar core of iron has no remaining fuel and collapses.

Nuclear structure:
  nucleons             protons and neutrons; the neutron is slightly heavier
  strong force         binds nucleons; short-ranged, saturating, ~100x
                       electromagnetic at nuclear distances
  binding energy       the mass deficit times c squared
  binding per nucleon  peaks at iron-56, ~8.8 MeV
  fusion below iron    releases energy
  fission above iron   releases energy
  isotopes             same protons, different neutrons
  nuclear radius       ~1.2 fm times the mass number to the one third power
  liquid drop model    volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing terms;
                       reproduces binding energies well
  shell model          nucleons in orbitals; explains magic numbers
  magic numbers        2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126; closed shells give extra
                       stability
  valley of stability  the neutron-to-proton ratio for bound nuclei; rises from 1
                       for light nuclei toward about 1.5 for heavy ones
  neutron excess       needed in heavy nuclei to dilute proton repulsion

The magic numbers are worth noting because they are direct evidence that nucleons occupy
discrete energy levels rather than forming an undifferentiated fluid. Nuclei with a magic
number of protons or neutrons are unusually stable and abundant, doubly magic nuclei such as
helium-4 and lead-208 exceptionally so, in the same way that noble gases are chemically inert
because their electron shells are closed. That the same shell logic applies to two entirely
different force laws is a nontrivial and instructive result.

Q: Both fusion of hydrogen and fission of uranium release energy. This is because:
A. Both increase the total mass
B. Binding energy per nucleon peaks at iron, so moving toward iron from either direction
   releases energy
C. Fusion releases energy and fission absorbs it
D. Both split heavy nuclei
Reasoning: The binding energy per nucleon curve peaks near iron-56. Fusing light nuclei moves
up the curve toward the peak, and splitting heavy nuclei also moves toward the peak from the
other side. In both cases the products are more tightly bound, so the excess mass is released
as energy. Answer: B.

## Radioactivity and Decay

An unstable nucleus decays toward a more stable configuration, and the mode depends on which
way it is unstable. Alpha decay emits a helium-4 nucleus and occurs in heavy nuclei where the
Coulomb repulsion is large; it proceeds by quantum tunnelling through the Coulomb barrier,
which is why the half-lives vary over more than twenty orders of magnitude while the emitted
energies vary by only a factor of two — the exponential sensitivity of tunnelling to barrier
height and energy.

Beta decay converts a neutron to a proton or the reverse, mediated by the weak interaction.
Beta-minus emits an electron and an antineutrino and occurs in neutron-rich nuclei; beta-plus
emits a positron and a neutrino and occurs in proton-rich ones, as does electron capture. The
neutrino was postulated precisely because the emitted electron's energy spectrum is continuous
rather than discrete, which appeared to violate energy conservation until a third undetected
particle carrying the remainder was proposed — a proposal made in 1930 and confirmed
experimentally only in 1956.

Gamma decay emits a photon from a nucleus in an excited state, changing neither the proton nor
the neutron count. It typically follows alpha or beta decay, which frequently leaves the
daughter nucleus excited, and the discrete gamma energies are characteristic of the emitting
nuclide, which is what makes gamma spectroscopy a precise identification technique.

Decay and radiation:
  alpha                emits helium-4; heavy nuclei; tunnelling, so half-lives span
                       an enormous range
  beta-minus           neutron to proton plus electron and antineutrino
  beta-plus            proton to neutron plus positron and neutrino
  electron capture     an inner electron absorbed; competes with beta-plus
  gamma                photon from an excited nucleus; characteristic energies
  decay law            N = N_0 exp(-lambda t);  half-life = ln2 / lambda
  activity             decays per second; becquerel
  penetration          alpha stopped by paper, beta by millimetres of aluminium,
                       gamma requires centimetres of lead
  ionizing power       inverse to penetration; alpha is most damaging internally
                       and harmless externally
  absorbed dose        gray; energy per unit mass
  equivalent dose      sievert; weighted by radiation type
  background           ~2-3 mSv per year typical, dominated by radon and by
                       cosmic rays at altitude
  half-life range      from microseconds to billions of years

The inverse relationship between penetration and ionizing power has a consequence that is
frequently misunderstood. Alpha particles are stopped by a sheet of paper or the dead outer
layer of skin, so an external alpha source is essentially harmless. The same particles deposit
all their energy in a very short path, so an alpha emitter inhaled or ingested delivers an
intense localized dose and is among the most hazardous internal contaminants. The hazard
depends on the exposure pathway, not on the radiation type alone.

Radiometric dating applies the decay law in reverse, and its reliability rests on decay rates
being independent of temperature, pressure, and chemical environment — which has been verified
across the ranges accessible to experiment. The assumptions that must be checked in any
application are that the system remained closed to gain or loss of parent and daughter, and
that the initial daughter abundance is known or determinable, which isochron methods establish
from the data itself rather than assuming.

## Fission, Fusion, and Applications

Fission splits a heavy nucleus into fragments, releasing energy and typically two or three
neutrons. Those neutrons can induce further fissions, and whether the reaction sustains itself
depends on the multiplication factor: the average number of subsequent fissions caused by each
one. Below one the reaction dies out, at exactly one it is critical and self-sustaining at
constant rate, and above one it grows.

Reactor control rests on a detail that makes it possible at all. About 0.65 percent of fission
neutrons are delayed, emitted seconds to minutes after the fission by decaying fragments rather
than promptly. A reactor is operated so that it is subcritical on prompt neutrons alone and
critical only with the delayed contribution, which means the reactor's response time is
governed by the delayed neutron timescale of seconds rather than by the prompt timescale of
microseconds. Without delayed neutrons, mechanical control would be impossible.

Fusion combines light nuclei and releases more energy per unit mass than fission, with fuel
that is abundant and products that are not long-lived waste. The difficulty is the Coulomb
barrier: nuclei must approach within the range of the strong force against their mutual
repulsion, which requires temperatures of order a hundred million kelvin. At those temperatures
matter is a plasma that must be confined, and the two approaches are magnetic confinement,
holding the plasma in a magnetic field for long periods at low density, and inertial
confinement, compressing a small target so rapidly that it fuses before it disassembles.

Fission and fusion:
  fission              heavy nucleus splits; ~200 MeV per event
  chain reaction       neutrons induce further fissions
  multiplication factor  below 1 subcritical, 1 critical, above 1 supercritical
  delayed neutrons     ~0.65%; what makes reactor control possible
  moderator            slows neutrons to thermal energies where fission cross
                       sections are far larger; water, heavy water, graphite
  control rods         neutron absorbers; adjust reactivity
  enrichment           raising the uranium-235 fraction; ~3-5% for power reactors
  breeder reactor      produces more fissile material than it consumes
  fission products     highly radioactive; the waste problem
  fusion, D-T          deuterium plus tritium; the easiest reaction, and it
                       produces a fast neutron that activates the structure
  Lawson criterion     the density, temperature, and confinement time product
                       required for net energy
  magnetic confinement tokamaks and stellarators; long confinement, low density
  inertial confinement laser or beam compression; short confinement, high density
  aneutronic fuels     avoid neutron activation; require much higher temperatures

The waste question is worth stating accurately because the discussion is frequently
imprecise. Fission products are intensely radioactive and mostly short-lived, decaying to
background levels over a few hundred years. Transuranic actinides produced by neutron capture
are far less intensely radioactive and persist for tens of thousands of years, and they are what
drives the long-term disposal requirement. Reprocessing separates them and they can in principle
be transmuted in fast reactors, which converts a hundred-thousand-year problem into a
several-hundred-year one — a technically demonstrated capability whose deployment is limited by
economics and by proliferation concerns rather than by physics.

## The Standard Model

The standard model describes all known fundamental particles and three of the four forces, and
it is the most precisely tested theory in science. Matter consists of fermions in three
generations: quarks that feel the strong force and leptons that do not. Forces are mediated by
gauge bosons. The Higgs field gives mass to the fermions and to the weak bosons through
spontaneous symmetry breaking.

The three generations are a genuine puzzle. The second and third generations are heavier
copies of the first with otherwise identical properties, they decay rapidly into first
generation particles, and ordinary matter consists entirely of the first. Why there are three,
and why the masses span such an enormous range — the top quark is roughly three hundred
thousand times heavier than the electron — is unexplained by the theory, which takes the masses
as input parameters rather than predicting them.

Quarks are never observed in isolation because of colour confinement. The strong force between
them does not weaken with distance as other forces do; the field energy grows, so separating
two quarks eventually supplies enough energy to create a new quark-antiquark pair, and the
result is two hadrons rather than two free quarks. The complementary property, asymptotic
freedom, is that the interaction weakens at very short distances, so quarks behave as nearly
free particles inside a hadron — which is why high-energy scattering experiments can probe them
at all.

The standard model:
  quarks               up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom; fractional charge,
                       carry colour
  leptons              electron, muon, tau and their neutrinos
  three generations    heavier copies; unexplained
  photon               electromagnetic; massless, infinite range
  W and Z bosons       weak; massive, hence very short range
  gluons               strong; carry colour themselves, which is why the force
                       behaves so differently
  Higgs boson          the excitation of the field giving mass; confirmed 2012
  confinement          quarks are never free; the field energy grows with
                       separation
  asymptotic freedom   the strong interaction weakens at short distance
  hadrons              baryons of three quarks, mesons of quark-antiquark
  mass origin          most of a proton's mass is binding energy, not quark mass
  antimatter           same mass, opposite charge; annihilates with matter
  neutrino oscillation  neutrinos change flavour, which requires nonzero mass;
                       the first confirmed departure from the original standard
                       model

Neutrino oscillation deserves emphasis because it is established physics beyond the standard
model as originally formulated. Neutrinos produced in one flavour are detected as another,
which requires that the flavour states be superpositions of states with different masses — and
therefore that at least two neutrino masses are nonzero. The original theory assumed them
massless. The absolute mass scale, the ordering of the masses, and whether neutrinos are their
own antiparticles all remain open, and each is the target of substantial current experimental
effort.

## Accelerators, Detectors, and Open Questions

Particle accelerators probe short distances by achieving high energy, and the connection is
direct: the de Broglie wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum, so resolving smaller
structures requires higher energy. They also create heavy particles, since energy converts to
mass, which is why discovering a particle of a given mass requires a collider capable of
supplying it.

Colliding beams rather than striking a fixed target is what makes the highest energies
accessible. In a fixed-target collision much of the energy goes into the momentum of the
products rather than into the interaction, and the useful centre-of-mass energy grows only as
the square root of the beam energy. In a symmetric collider the centre-of-mass frame is the
laboratory frame and the full energy is available, which is why every high-energy frontier
machine is a collider.

Detectors reconstruct what happened from what emerges, and modern designs are layered
cylinders each identifying different particles. Tracking detectors in a magnetic field measure
charged particle trajectories, with the curvature giving momentum and the sign of the charge.
Electromagnetic calorimeters absorb electrons and photons and measure their energy. Hadronic
calorimeters do the same for hadrons. Muon chambers on the outside detect the only charged
particles that penetrate everything else. Neutrinos escape entirely and are inferred from
missing transverse momentum.

Accelerators, detectors, and open problems:
  wavelength and energy  higher energy resolves smaller structures
  fixed target vs collider  collider energy is fully available; fixed target
                         wastes most of it to momentum
  synchrotron radiation  charged particles radiate when accelerated; the reason
                         electron rings are limited and proton rings are not
  luminosity             collision rate; determines how fast rare processes
                         accumulate
  tracking detector      curvature in a magnetic field gives momentum and charge
  calorimeter            absorbs and measures energy
  muon system            the outermost layer; muons penetrate everything else
  missing momentum       infers neutrinos and any invisible particle
  trigger                selects which of billions of collisions per second to
                         record; most data is discarded in microseconds and is
                         unrecoverable, so trigger design determines what can be
                         discovered
  five sigma             the discovery convention in particle physics; a
                         one-in-3.5-million false positive rate, chosen because
                         the number of searches is enormous
  open: dark matter      no standard model candidate
  open: matter asymmetry  standard model CP violation is far too small
  open: neutrino mass    origin, scale, and ordering
  open: hierarchy        why the Higgs mass is so far below the Planck scale
  open: gravity          not included in the standard model at all
  open: parameters       around nineteen free parameters taken from experiment

The trigger deserves the last technical word because it determines what is discoverable.
A modern collider produces collisions faster than any system could record, so hardware and
software triggers select a tiny fraction within microseconds based on coarse criteria, and
everything else is discarded permanently. This means the experiment can only find what the
trigger was designed to keep, and a signature nobody anticipated is thrown away before anyone
sees it. Considerable effort goes into designing triggers that preserve unanticipated
signatures, and it is a genuine limit on discovery rather than a technicality.

The applications of nuclear and particle physics extend far beyond the field. Medical imaging
uses positron emission from radiotracers and nuclear magnetic resonance. Radiotherapy uses
beams shaped to deposit dose in a tumour, with proton and carbon ion therapy exploiting the
Bragg peak to concentrate the dose at a controllable depth rather than along the whole path.
Carbon dating and other radiometric methods date archaeological and geological material.
Neutron scattering probes material structure. Accelerator-produced isotopes are used in
industry and medicine. And the World Wide Web was created at a particle physics laboratory to
share data among collaborators — the most economically consequential outcome of the field, and
one that no funding case could have predicted.
