## Wave Fundamentals

A wave is a disturbance propagating through a medium or field, transporting energy and
momentum without net transport of matter. The distinction between the motion of the disturbance
and the motion of the medium is the first thing to establish: in a water wave the water moves in
small circles while the wave travels for kilometres, and in a sound wave air molecules oscillate
by micrometres while the sound crosses a room.

Waves are classified by the direction of the medium's motion relative to propagation.
Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel — light, waves on a string,
seismic S waves. Longitudinal waves oscillate along it, producing compressions and rarefactions
— sound in fluids, seismic P waves. The distinction has consequences: transverse waves can be
polarized because there are two independent perpendicular directions, while longitudinal waves
cannot, and shear waves cannot propagate in a fluid because a fluid has no shear strength, which
is exactly how the Earth's liquid outer core was discovered.

The wave equation relates the second time derivative to the second spatial derivative through
the square of the propagation speed, and that speed follows from the medium's properties: a
restoring force divided by an inertia. For a string it is tension over linear density; for sound
in a gas it is the bulk modulus over density, which via the ideal gas law makes it proportional
to the square root of absolute temperature and independent of pressure. The temperature
dependence is why sound travels faster on a hot day and why atmospheric temperature gradients
refract sound, bending it toward the cooler region.

Acoustic impedance is the concept doing the most work in applications, because it determines
what happens at a boundary. When a wave meets an interface between media of different
impedance, part reflects and part transmits, with the proportion set by the impedance ratio. A
large mismatch reflects nearly everything, which is why sound from air barely enters water — the
impedance ratio is about 3600 to one and over 99.9 percent reflects — and why an ultrasound probe
requires coupling gel to displace the air between it and the skin.

Q: An ultrasound examination performed without coupling gel produces no usable image. The
reason is:
A. The gel amplifies the signal
B. The air gap creates an enormous impedance mismatch, so nearly all the ultrasound reflects
   at the probe-air interface and never enters the body
C. The gel conducts electricity
D. The gel reduces friction
Reasoning: Acoustic impedance differs between the transducer and air by several orders of
magnitude, and the reflection coefficient at such a mismatch approaches unity. Gel with
impedance close to tissue displaces the air and provides a matched path. This is impedance
matching, the same principle as an anti-reflection coating in optics. Answer: B.

## Sound Propagation and Perception

Sound intensity spans an enormous range — the threshold of hearing to the threshold of pain is
about twelve orders of magnitude in intensity — which is why the decibel scale is logarithmic.
Sound pressure level references twenty micropascals, chosen as the approximate threshold of
hearing at one kilohertz, and doubling the pressure adds six decibels while doubling the
intensity adds three.

Propagation losses come from several mechanisms with different distance dependence. Geometric
spreading dominates: a point source in free space spreads its power over a sphere, so intensity
falls as the inverse square and level falls six decibels per doubling of distance. A line source
spreads over a cylinder and falls three decibels per doubling, which is why traffic noise from a
busy road decays more slowly than intuition suggests. Atmospheric absorption adds a
frequency-dependent loss that is negligible at low frequency and substantial at high, which is
why distant sounds are dull — thunder from nearby cracks and from far away rumbles.

Refraction bends sound where the speed varies, and because sound speed depends on temperature
and on wind, atmospheric structure shapes propagation over distance. A temperature inversion,
with warm air above cold, bends sound downward and can carry it far beyond the normal range,
which is why distant sounds are audible on still cold nights. The normal daytime gradient bends
sound upward, creating a shadow zone. Wind gradients do the same asymmetrically, which is why
sound carries downwind and not upwind.

  diffraction           bending around obstacles; strong at low frequency where

  Doppler shift         moving source or observer; the pitch change on passing

Diffraction explains why noise barriers work poorly for low frequencies and it follows from a
general property of waves: bending around an obstacle is significant when the wavelength is
comparable to or larger than the obstacle. A one-hundred-hertz tone has a wavelength of about
3.4 metres and diffracts freely over a three-metre barrier, while a four-kilohertz tone with an
eight-centimetre wavelength is effectively blocked. This is why traffic noise heard behind a
barrier is a low rumble with the higher frequencies removed, and why barriers must be tall
relative to the wavelengths they are intended to attenuate.

## Interference, Resonance, and Room Acoustics

Superposition means waves add, and where they overlap coherently the result depends on their
relative phase. Constructive interference occurs where they are in phase and destructive where
opposed, producing spatial patterns of loud and quiet. Standing waves form when a wave reflects
and interferes with itself, producing fixed nodes and antinodes rather than a travelling
pattern.

Resonance occurs when a system is driven at a frequency matching one of its natural modes, and
the response is amplified by a factor related to the quality factor. Every bounded system has
natural modes determined by its geometry and boundary conditions: a string fixed at both ends
supports modes at integer multiples of its fundamental, an open pipe supports the same series,
and a pipe closed at one end supports only odd harmonics — which is why a clarinet sounds
different from a flute of the same length and plays an octave lower.

Room acoustics is the behaviour of sound in an enclosed space, and it separates into distinct
regimes by frequency. At low frequencies where the wavelength is comparable to the room
dimensions, discrete modal resonances dominate and produce large position-dependent variations —
which is why bass response in a small room changes drastically with listener position and why
room modes are the hardest acoustic problem to fix. At high frequencies where many modes overlap,
statistical treatment applies and the room is characterized by reverberation time.

The frequency dependence of absorption is the practical difficulty in acoustic treatment.
Porous absorbers work by viscous loss as air moves through them, which requires the material to
sit where the particle velocity is high — a quarter wavelength from a reflecting surface. For a
one-hundred-hertz wave that is 85 centimetres, so a thin panel on the wall absorbs high
frequencies effectively and low frequencies barely at all. Treating a room with thin absorptive
panels therefore removes the highs and leaves the lows, producing a space that is
simultaneously dead and boomy — the characteristic result of acoustic treatment applied without
attention to frequency.

Below about 138 Hz your room behaves modally rather than statistically: sound at those
frequencies forms standing waves with fixed nodes and antinodes, so the level at a given
frequency depends strongly on where you sit. Moving a foot moves you relative to those patterns,
which is exactly what you observe. Your lowest axial modes are at 40.9, 50.5, and 68.6 Hz, and
you have two modes nearly coincident at 101 Hz, which will produce a pronounced peak there — the
boominess.

Your 5 cm foam is effective above roughly 1 kHz and does essentially nothing below 500 Hz. A
porous absorber works by viscous loss where air particle velocity is high, which is a quarter
wavelength from the boundary — 85 cm at 100 Hz. Thin panels sit in the pressure maximum and
velocity minimum at low frequency and absorb almost nothing there. The result is a room with
the highs removed and the lows untouched, which sounds both dead and boomy at once.

Three interventions, in order of effect per effort.

Corner bass traps. Room corners are pressure maxima for every axial mode simultaneously, so
absorption there is the most efficient placement available. Use thick porous absorbers — 30 cm
of mineral wool or equivalent, straddling the corner with an air gap behind — or purpose-built
membrane or Helmholtz absorbers tuned near your problem frequencies. Treat all four vertical
corners first, then the wall-ceiling edges.

Move the listening position. Modal nulls and peaks are at predictable locations, and the worst
position is usually the room centre for one axis and against a wall for another. Positioning
yourself at roughly 38% of the room length from the front wall is a common starting rule, and
measuring with a sweep and a microphone will do better than any rule.

Then reconsider the foam. Once the low end is under control, the mid and high absorption you
already have may be excessive for a small room, and some of it is probably better replaced with
diffusion to keep the space from sounding dead.

Measure before and after with a free measurement tool and a calibrated microphone. Room
acoustics is the area where subjective impression is least reliable and measurement is most
informative, because the problems are frequency- and position-specific in ways that listening
does not localize.

## Transducers, Ultrasound, and Applications

A transducer converts between acoustic and electrical energy. Microphones convert pressure to
voltage, and the two main principles have complementary characteristics: dynamic microphones use
a coil moving in a magnetic field, which is robust, needs no power, and has limited
high-frequency response; condenser microphones use a charged diaphragm forming a capacitor,
which is more sensitive and extended in response and requires polarizing voltage.

Directional response is determined by how the microphone senses pressure. A sealed capsule
responds to pressure alone, which is a scalar, so it is omnidirectional. A capsule open at both
sides responds to the pressure difference across it, which depends on the direction of arrival,
giving a figure-of-eight pattern. Combining the two in different proportions produces the
intermediate patterns, and the cardioid — sensitive in front and null behind — is the sum of
equal parts of each.

Ultrasound uses frequencies above human hearing, and its applications exploit the short
wavelength. Medical imaging transmits pulses and forms an image from the echo timing and
amplitude, with resolution set by the wavelength and penetration limited by frequency-dependent
attenuation — which is the fundamental tradeoff, since higher frequency gives better resolution
and less depth. Non-destructive testing finds internal flaws in materials by the same principle.
Therapeutic ultrasound uses focused high intensity to heat or ablate tissue without incision.

## Noise Control and Vibration

Noise control follows a hierarchy from source to receiver, and interventions closer to the
source are more effective and usually cheaper. Reducing the source is best — a quieter machine,
a lower speed, a resilient mounting. Interrupting the path is next — enclosure, barrier,
absorption. Protecting the receiver is last — hearing protection, which is effective and
depends on consistent correct use.

Transmission through a partition is governed by mass at most frequencies: the transmission loss
rises about six decibels per doubling of surface mass, which is the mass law. This is why a
lightweight wall transmits sound regardless of how absorptive its surface is, and why the
effective strategies are mass, decoupling the two faces so vibration does not bridge them, and
damping. The weakest element dominates the whole partition, so a well-built wall with an
unsealed gap around a door performs at the level of the gap.

Vibration isolation reduces transmission between a machine and its support, and the mechanism
is a mass-spring system whose natural frequency must be well below the excitation frequency.
Isolation begins only above the square root of two times the natural frequency, and below that
the mount amplifies rather than isolates — which is why a poorly chosen mount makes the problem
worse, and why isolating low-frequency excitation requires very soft mounts with correspondingly
large static deflection.

The confusion between absorption and isolation is the most common practical error in acoustic
work and is worth stating plainly. Absorptive material placed on a wall reduces the reverberant
sound level within the room by converting reflected energy to heat. It does almost nothing to
reduce sound passing through the wall to the next room, because transmission depends on the
partition's mass, stiffness, and damping rather than on its surface absorption. Someone
attempting to soundproof a room by lining it with foam has treated the wrong problem, and the
correct interventions — mass, decoupled construction, sealing every penetration — are
structural and expensive rather than surface treatments.
