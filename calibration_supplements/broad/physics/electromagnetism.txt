## Electrostatics

Coulomb's law gives the force between two point charges as k q1 q2 / r^2 along the line
joining them, with k = 1/(4 pi eps_0) approximately 8.99e9 N m^2/C^2. Its inverse-square
form is not an approximation but has been verified to extraordinary precision, and that
precision matters because the exponent being exactly two is equivalent to the photon
being exactly massless. The law's structural similarity to Newtonian gravitation is
useful, but the differences dominate practice: charge comes in two signs so
electrostatic forces can cancel, and the electric force between two protons is roughly
10^36 times their gravitational attraction, which is why gravity is irrelevant at
atomic scales and why bulk matter is electrically neutral to extraordinary accuracy.

The electric field E is force per unit charge, a vector field defined at every point
whether or not a test charge is present. Treating the field as physically real rather
than as a bookkeeping device is not merely a preference: fields carry energy and
momentum, and they propagate at finite speed, so the interaction between two charges is
mediated locally rather than acting at a distance. Field lines visualize the field —
originating on positive charge, terminating on negative, never crossing, with density
indicating magnitude.

Gauss's law states that the electric flux through any closed surface equals the enclosed
charge divided by eps_0. It is equivalent to Coulomb's law for static fields but is far
more powerful when symmetry is available, because a well-chosen surface makes the flux
integral trivial. Three canonical results follow in a line each: a spherically symmetric
charge distribution produces an external field identical to a point charge at its
centre; an infinite line of charge gives a field falling as 1/r; an infinite plane gives
a uniform field independent of distance. That last result is the basis of the
parallel-plate capacitor and is worth internalizing because the distance-independence
surprises people every time.

Electrostatics reference:
  Coulomb force          F = k q1 q2 / r^2,  k = 1/(4 pi eps_0) ≈ 8.99e9
  electric field         E = F/q;  point charge E = k q / r^2
  Gauss's law            flux of E through closed surface = Q_enc / eps_0
  potential              V = k q / r;  E = -grad V
  potential energy       U = q V;  for two charges U = k q1 q2 / r
  capacitance            C = Q/V;  parallel plate C = eps_0 eps_r A / d
  stored energy          U = (1/2) C V^2 = (1/2) Q V = Q^2/(2C)
  energy density         u = (1/2) eps_0 E^2
  dipole moment          p = q d;  torque in a field = p cross E

Electric potential is the potential energy per unit charge and is a scalar, which makes
it far easier to work with than the field. Superposition of potentials is a simple sum
where superposition of fields requires vector addition, so the standard technique for a
complicated charge distribution is to compute the potential and then take the negative
gradient. Only potential differences are physical; the zero point is a convention,
usually taken at infinity for localized distributions and at "ground" in circuits.

Q: A hollow conducting sphere carries charge Q. The electric field inside the cavity,
with no charge in the cavity, is:
A. kQ/r^2 pointing outward   B. Zero   C. Uniform and nonzero   D. Infinite at the
centre
Reasoning: Apply Gauss's law to a surface inside the conductor's material: the field
there is zero in electrostatic equilibrium, so the enclosed charge is zero, so all
charge resides on the outer surface. A Gaussian surface inside the cavity encloses no
charge and by symmetry the field vanishes. This is electrostatic shielding — the
principle behind the Faraday cage and behind shielded cable. Answer: B.

Q: Two capacitors, 2 uF and 4 uF, are connected in series across 12 V. The charge on
the 2 uF capacitor is:
A. 24 uC   B. 16 uC   C. 48 uC   D. 8 uC
Reasoning: Series capacitors carry equal charge. The equivalent capacitance is
(2*4)/(2+4) = 4/3 uF, so Q = C V = (4/3)(12) = 16 uC on each. Note this is the reverse
of resistors, where series elements share current and parallel ones share voltage; for
capacitors the series combination adds reciprocals. Answer: B.

## Conductors, Dielectrics, and Circuits

In electrostatic equilibrium a conductor has zero internal field, since any field would
drive its free charges until it is cancelled. Three consequences follow: all excess
charge resides on the surface, the surface is an equipotential, and the field just
outside is perpendicular to the surface with magnitude sigma/eps_0. Charge concentrates
where curvature is greatest, so the field is strongest at points and edges — which is
why lightning rods are pointed, why high-voltage hardware uses large-radius corona
rings, and why a sharp burr on a PCB trace is where dielectric breakdown starts.

A dielectric placed in a field polarizes: its molecules develop or align dipole moments,
producing a bound surface charge that partially cancels the applied field. The internal
field is reduced by the relative permittivity eps_r, so a capacitor with a dielectric
holds eps_r times more charge at the same voltage. Real dielectrics also have a
breakdown strength — air about 3 kV/mm, common polymers 15-25 kV/mm — beyond which they
ionize and conduct, which together with eps_r sets the fundamental size-versus-voltage
tradeoff for every capacitor.

Circuit analysis reduces field theory to lumped elements under the quasi-static
approximation, valid when the circuit is small compared with the wavelength at the
operating frequency. Ohm's law V = IR is a material property rather than a law of
nature, holding for ohmic conductors over a limited range and failing for
semiconductors, gas discharges, and anything heated by its own current. Kirchhoff's laws
are the real foundation: current into a node equals current out (charge conservation),
and voltages around a loop sum to zero (energy conservation, or equivalently the
electrostatic field being conservative).

Circuit relations:
  Ohm's law            V = I R
  power                P = V I = I^2 R = V^2 / R
  resistance           R = rho L / A
  series resistors     R = R1 + R2 + ...
  parallel resistors   1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...
  series capacitors    1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + ...
  parallel capacitors  C = C1 + C2 + ...
  RC time constant     tau = RC; 63% in one tau, 99% in five
  RL time constant     tau = L/R
  inductor voltage     V = L dI/dt
  capacitor current    I = C dV/dt
  impedance            Z_R = R, Z_C = 1/(j omega C), Z_L = j omega L
  resonance            omega_0 = 1/sqrt(LC)

Impedance extends resistance to sinusoidal steady state by using complex numbers, which
turns differential equations into algebra. A capacitor's impedance falls with frequency,
so it blocks DC and passes high frequencies; an inductor's rises, so it passes DC and
blocks high frequencies. This immediately gives filters: a series resistor with a shunt
capacitor is a low-pass with corner frequency 1/(2 pi RC), and swapping them gives a
high-pass. Series RLC resonance occurs where the capacitive and inductive reactances
cancel, leaving only resistance, so the impedance is minimized and current peaks.

The quasi-static assumption fails as frequency rises and is the boundary between circuit
theory and transmission-line theory. The rule of thumb is that a conductor must be
treated as a transmission line when its length exceeds roughly a tenth of the signal
wavelength — or, for digital signals, when the propagation delay exceeds about a sixth
of the edge rise time, since a fast edge contains high-frequency content regardless of
the clock rate. This is why a 10 MHz clock on a short board is a simple net while a 1 ns
edge on the same trace requires controlled impedance and termination.

## Magnetostatics and Induction

Magnetic fields are produced by moving charge. The Biot-Savart law gives the field from
a current element, and Ampere's law — the circulation of B around a closed loop equals
mu_0 times the enclosed current — plays the role Gauss's law plays in electrostatics,
making symmetric problems easy. A long straight wire gives B = mu_0 I/(2 pi r); a
solenoid gives a nearly uniform interior field B = mu_0 n I depending only on turns per
unit length; a toroid confines the field entirely within itself, which is why toroidal
inductors radiate so little and are preferred where interference matters.

The magnetic force on a moving charge is q v cross B, perpendicular to both velocity and
field. Because it is always perpendicular to velocity, the magnetic force does no work —
it changes direction but never speed. A charge moving perpendicular to a uniform field
therefore travels in a circle of radius r = mv/(qB), the cyclotron radius, at a
frequency independent of speed, which is what makes the cyclotron work and what
determines confinement in magnetic traps and mass spectrometers.

There are no magnetic monopoles, so far as any experiment has shown, which is the
content of Gauss's law for magnetism: the flux of B through any closed surface is zero.
Field lines therefore always close on themselves. The simplest magnetic source is the
dipole, produced by a current loop, and its field falls as 1/r^3 rather than the 1/r^2
of an electric monopole — which is why magnetic interactions fall off faster with
distance and why magnetic shielding works differently from electrostatic shielding,
requiring high-permeability material to redirect flux rather than a conductor to
terminate field lines.

Faraday's law of induction states that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit
induces an electromotive force equal to the negative rate of change of flux. Lenz's law
is the meaning of that minus sign: the induced current opposes the change producing it,
which is required by energy conservation — an induced current that reinforced its cause
would produce runaway energy from nothing. Flux can change by changing the field, the
area, or the orientation, which correspond respectively to transformers, to sliding-bar
generators, and to rotating-coil alternators.

Magnetic quantities:
  force on charge        F = q v cross B
  force on wire          F = I L cross B
  Biot-Savart            dB = (mu_0/4 pi) I dl cross r_hat / r^2
  Ampere's law           circulation of B = mu_0 I_enc
  long wire              B = mu_0 I / (2 pi r)
  solenoid               B = mu_0 n I  (interior, long solenoid)
  Faraday's law          emf = -d(flux)/dt
  motional emf           emf = B L v
  inductance             L = flux linkage / I;  emf = -L dI/dt
  stored energy          U = (1/2) L I^2
  energy density         u = B^2 / (2 mu_0)
  mutual inductance      transformer: V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p

Eddy currents are induced in any bulk conductor experiencing changing flux, and they
dissipate energy resistively. This is a loss mechanism in transformer cores, addressed
by laminating the core so induced current paths are interrupted, or by using ferrite
whose high resistivity suppresses them. It is also the operating principle of induction
heating, induction cooktops, metal detectors, eddy-current brakes, and the damping in
analog meter movements — a good example of the same physics being a defect in one
context and the product in another.

The breakdown: mutual inductance between the two runs is about 62 nH, and at your 45
A/us switching rate that induces only 2.8 mV. Capacitive coupling through roughly 4 pF
of mutual capacitance accounts for 37 mV of the 40 you measured. The reason capacitive
coupling dominates is your victim impedance — displacement current through the mutual
capacitance develops a voltage across whatever impedance it finds, and 10 kilohms
develops a thousand times more voltage than 10 ohms would for the same current.

Fixes, in order of effectiveness for capacitive coupling specifically:

Lower the victim impedance. This is the biggest lever and often the easiest. Buffer the
sensor with an op-amp near the sensor itself so the long run is driven from a low
impedance. Dropping from 10 k to 100 ohms cuts the coupled voltage by a factor of a
hundred.

Add an electrostatic shield. A grounded shield around the sensor line intercepts the
displacement current and returns it to ground instead of to your signal. Ground the
shield at one end only for low-frequency signals to avoid a ground loop. Note this is
the fix for capacitive coupling; it does almost nothing for magnetic coupling, which
needs twisted pair or a high-permeability shield instead.

Increase separation and reduce parallel run length. Capacitance falls with separation,
so routing the sensor line away from the motor cable — ideally crossing at right angles
rather than running parallel — helps directly.

Add a low-pass filter at the receiver. Your signal is presumably far below 20 kHz, so an
RC filter with a corner around 1 kHz removes the switching noise entirely. Do this even
after the other fixes; it costs two components.

If it had been inductive coupling the prescription would be different — twisted pair,
minimized loop area, and slower switching edges — which is why identifying which
mechanism dominates is the first step rather than applying all the remedies at once.

## Maxwell's Equations and Electromagnetic Waves

Maxwell's four equations describe all classical electromagnetism. Gauss's law relates
electric flux to charge. Gauss's law for magnetism states that magnetic flux through a
closed surface is zero, encoding the absence of monopoles. Faraday's law relates the
circulation of E to changing magnetic flux. The Ampere-Maxwell law relates the
circulation of B to current plus the rate of change of electric flux.

That last term, the displacement current, was Maxwell's addition and it is the one that
changed physics. Ampere's law as originally stated is inconsistent for time-varying
currents — apply it to a surface passing between the plates of a charging capacitor and
it gives a different answer than a surface cutting the wire. Adding a term proportional
to the rate of change of electric flux restores consistency, and it makes the equations
symmetric: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field, and a changing electric
field produces a magnetic field. Each sustaining the other permits a self-propagating
disturbance.

Maxwell's equations, differential form in vacuum:
  div E   = rho / eps_0                      Gauss
  div B   = 0                                no monopoles
  curl E  = -dB/dt                           Faraday
  curl B  = mu_0 J + mu_0 eps_0 dE/dt        Ampere-Maxwell
  Lorentz force  F = q(E + v cross B)
  wave speed     c = 1/sqrt(mu_0 eps_0) ≈ 2.998e8 m/s
  in a medium    v = c/n,  n = sqrt(eps_r mu_r)
  impedance of free space  Z_0 = sqrt(mu_0/eps_0) ≈ 377 ohms

Taking the curl of Faraday's law and substituting Ampere-Maxwell yields the wave
equation, with propagation speed 1/sqrt(mu_0 eps_0). That this computed number matched
the measured speed of light was the moment optics became a branch of electromagnetism —
one of the great unifications in physics, and one obtained by manipulating equations
rather than by experiment.

Electromagnetic waves are transverse, with E and B perpendicular to each other and to
the direction of propagation, in phase, with magnitudes related by E = cB. They carry
energy at a rate given by the Poynting vector S = (1/mu_0) E cross B, and they carry
momentum, which is why radiation pressure exists and why solar sails work. The full
spectrum from radio through gamma rays differs only in frequency; the same equations
govern all of it, and the apparent differences in behaviour come from how each frequency
range interacts with matter of a given structure size.

Polarization describes the orientation of the E field. Unpolarized light has random
orientation; a linear polarizer transmits one component, giving Malus's law where the
transmitted intensity is proportional to cos^2 of the angle between the polarizer and
the incident polarization. Circular polarization results from two perpendicular
components of equal amplitude and 90 degrees relative phase. Polarization is why
sunglasses reduce glare (light reflected at shallow angles is partially polarized
horizontally), how LCD displays work, and how radio links reuse frequency by
transmitting orthogonal polarizations on the same channel.

## Radiation and Antennas

Accelerating charge radiates. A static charge has a static field, a uniformly moving
charge carries its field along with it, but an accelerating charge produces a
disturbance that detaches and propagates away carrying energy. The Larmor formula gives
the radiated power as proportional to the square of the acceleration, which is why
radiation is negligible for slowly varying currents and dominant at high frequency.
This single fact is why classical physics fails for atoms — an orbiting electron
accelerates and should radiate away its energy in about 10^-11 seconds — and it was one
of the principal motivations for quantum mechanics.

An antenna converts guided waves on a transmission line into radiated waves in space,
and reciprocity guarantees that its transmitting and receiving properties are identical.
The half-wave dipole is the reference element: a conductor about half a wavelength long,
fed at the centre, with a radiation resistance near 73 ohms — conveniently close to
common coaxial cable impedances — and a doughnut-shaped pattern with nulls along its
axis. A quarter-wave monopole over a ground plane behaves like half a dipole, with about
36 ohms radiation resistance, and its performance depends critically on the ground plane
being adequate, which is why a handheld radio's antenna works differently depending on
how it is held.

Antenna gain is not amplification; it is a redistribution of the same radiated power
into a narrower beam. An antenna with 10 dBi gain concentrates power ten times more
intensely in its main lobe than an isotropic radiator would, necessarily at the expense
of other directions. Directivity and beamwidth are inversely related — higher gain means
a narrower beam and tighter pointing requirements — which is the central tradeoff in
link design, and why a satellite dish must be aimed carefully while an omnidirectional
whip need not.

Antenna and link quantities:
  wavelength             lambda = c / f;  300/f_MHz gives lambda in metres
  half-wave dipole       L ≈ 0.48 lambda, R_rad ≈ 73 ohms, gain 2.15 dBi
  quarter-wave monopole  L ≈ 0.24 lambda over a ground plane, R_rad ≈ 36 ohms
  gain vs aperture       G = 4 pi A_eff / lambda^2
  free-space path loss   FSPL_dB = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) + 32.45  (km, MHz)
  Friis equation         P_r = P_t G_t G_r (lambda / (4 pi d))^2
  VSWR                   ratio of max to min standing-wave voltage; 1:1 is a
                         perfect match, 2:1 reflects about 11% of power
  return loss            dB below incident power reflected; higher is better
  skin depth             delta = sqrt(2 rho / (omega mu)); current flows in a thin
                         surface layer at high frequency

Impedance matching is what determines whether power reaches the antenna or reflects
back down the feedline. A mismatch creates standing waves, so some power returns to the
transmitter — potentially damaging it — and the mismatch loss compounds with cable loss.
Matching networks, baluns, and antenna tuners all exist to transform the antenna's
complex impedance to the system impedance, conventionally 50 ohms for radio and 75 ohms
for video. The 50 ohm convention is itself a compromise: minimum coaxial cable
attenuation occurs near 77 ohms and maximum power handling near 30 ohms, and 50 is
roughly the geometric compromise between them.

Skin effect concentrates high-frequency current in a thin surface layer, so the
effective resistance rises with the square root of frequency. At 1 MHz in copper the
skin depth is about 65 micrometres and at 1 GHz about 2 micrometres, which means the
interior of a conductor is entirely unused at radio frequencies. The practical
consequences: hollow tubing performs as well as solid rod for RF, silver plating on
a base conductor is effective because only the surface carries current, litz wire with
many individually insulated strands reduces loss at intermediate frequencies, and PCB
trace surface roughness becomes a significant loss mechanism in multi-gigahertz designs
because the current is forced to follow the longer rough path.
