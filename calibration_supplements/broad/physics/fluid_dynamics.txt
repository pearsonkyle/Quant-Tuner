## Fluid Statics and Kinematics

A fluid is a substance that deforms continuously under shear stress, which distinguishes it
from a solid that reaches a static deformation. Both liquids and gases qualify, differing in
compressibility — liquids are nearly incompressible under ordinary conditions while gases are
not — and that distinction determines which equations apply.

Hydrostatic pressure increases with depth because the fluid above has weight, giving the
familiar linear relation for constant density. Its consequences include buoyancy, formalized as
Archimedes' principle: the upward force equals the weight of displaced fluid, which follows
directly from integrating the pressure over a submerged surface. Pascal's principle, that
pressure applied to an enclosed fluid transmits undiminished, is the basis of hydraulic force
multiplication — a small force on a small piston produces a large force on a large one, with
the work conserved because the large piston moves proportionally less.

The kinematic description of flow admits two viewpoints that recur throughout the subject.
The Lagrangian view follows individual fluid parcels, which is natural for deriving the
equations from Newton's laws applied to a mass. The Eulerian view describes the field at fixed
points in space, which is what instruments measure and what computation uses. Converting
between them introduces the material derivative, which accounts for both the local change at a
fixed point and the change from the parcel moving into a region with different conditions —
the convective term, and the source of the nonlinearity that makes fluid dynamics hard.

Q: A block floats with 80% of its volume submerged in water. Its density is closest to:
A. 200 kg/m^3   B. 800 kg/m^3   C. 1250 kg/m^3   D. 1000 kg/m^3
Reasoning: Floating equilibrium requires the buoyant force to equal the weight, so the
displaced fluid mass equals the object's mass. Displaced volume is 0.8 of the object's volume,
so 0.8 times water density equals the object's density, giving 800 kg/m^3. Answer: B.

## Conservation Laws and Bernoulli

The governing equations express conservation of mass, momentum, and energy for a fluid.
Continuity states that mass cannot accumulate in a fixed volume without a net inflow, which for
incompressible flow reduces to the velocity field being divergence-free. In a pipe of varying
area this gives the familiar result that velocity is inversely proportional to cross-sectional
area, so a constriction speeds the flow.

Bernoulli's equation relates pressure, velocity, and elevation along a streamline for steady,
incompressible, inviscid flow. Its content is energy conservation: the sum of pressure energy,
kinetic energy, and potential energy per unit volume is constant along a streamline. The
counterintuitive consequence — that faster flow has lower pressure — explains the lift on a
curved surface, the operation of a venturi meter, and why a shower curtain is pulled inward.

The equation's assumptions are restrictive and routinely violated in casual application.
Steady flow excludes anything time-varying. Incompressibility excludes gas flow above roughly
thirty percent of the speed of sound. Inviscid excludes anything where friction matters, which
includes flow near a wall and any flow that has separated. And it applies along a streamline,
so comparing points on different streamlines requires additionally that the flow be
irrotational. The standard incorrect explanation of aircraft lift — that air travelling over
the longer upper surface must arrive simultaneously and therefore goes faster — fails on the
premise itself, since there is no reason for simultaneous arrival, and the parcels above in
fact arrive earlier.

Lift is worth stating correctly because the popular explanation is wrong and a better one is
available without mathematics. A wing deflects air downward, and by Newton's third law the air
pushes the wing upward — this is complete and correct as far as it goes. The circulation
picture is the more quantitative version: the flow around a lifting wing has net circulation,
and the Kutta-Joukowski theorem relates lift to that circulation directly. The angle of attack
and the trailing-edge shape together determine the circulation, which is why a symmetric
aerofoil generates lift at positive angle of attack and none at zero, contradicting any
explanation resting on the upper surface being longer.

## Navier-Stokes and Dimensionless Numbers

The Navier-Stokes equations are Newton's second law applied to a fluid element, with terms
for pressure gradient, viscous stress, and body forces balanced against the material
acceleration. They are believed to describe all classical fluid behaviour, and their general
solution is unknown — whether smooth solutions always exist in three dimensions is one of the
Millennium Prize problems, and the difficulty comes from the convective nonlinearity.

Because analytical solutions exist only for a handful of simplified cases, dimensional
analysis carries much of the practical load. The Buckingham pi theorem states that a
relationship among n variables involving k independent dimensions can be expressed among n-k
dimensionless groups, which reduces the parameter space enormously and identifies which
combinations govern behaviour.

The Reynolds number is the most important of these. It is the ratio of inertial to viscous
forces, and it determines the character of the flow rather than merely its magnitude. Below a
critical value the flow is laminar, with smooth layers and viscous forces dominating; above it
the flow is turbulent, with chaotic eddies and inertia dominating. Two geometrically similar
flows at the same Reynolds number behave identically regardless of their physical scale, which
is dynamic similarity and is what makes scale-model testing meaningful.

Dimensionless groups:

  Weber          rho v^2 L / sigma;  inertia over surface tension; droplet breakup
  Prandtl        momentum over thermal diffusivity; boundary layer thickness ratio
  Nusselt        convective over conductive heat transfer
  Rayleigh       buoyancy-driven convection onset
  Strouhal       vortex shedding frequency; f L / v, about 0.2 for a cylinder over

  Peclet         advection over diffusion

  pipe transition   laminar below Re ~2300, turbulent above ~4000
  dynamic similarity  matched dimensionless groups give matched behaviour

Matching dimensionless numbers is what makes model testing valid and is also its central
difficulty. A ship model at one-fiftieth scale cannot simultaneously match Reynolds and Froude
numbers — matching Froude requires a velocity scaled by the square root of length, and matching
Reynolds requires velocity scaled inversely with length, which are incompatible. The standard
resolution is to match Froude, since wave-making resistance dominates, and to compute the
viscous contribution separately using empirical correlations. Recognizing which group governs
the phenomenon of interest is therefore a prerequisite to designing a valid experiment.

## Laminar Flow, Turbulence, and Boundary Layers

Laminar flow is orderly, with fluid moving in smooth layers that do not mix except by
molecular diffusion. It is analytically tractable, and pipe flow is the canonical case:
the velocity profile is parabolic, the flow rate depends on the fourth power of the radius by
the Hagen-Poiseuille relation, and pressure drop is linear in flow rate. That fourth-power
dependence has striking consequences — halving a pipe's radius reduces flow to one sixteenth at
the same pressure, which is why small changes in arterial diameter have large physiological
effects.

Turbulent flow is chaotic, with velocity fluctuating irregularly and eddies spanning a wide
range of scales. It is dramatically better at mixing and at transferring heat and momentum, and
dramatically worse for pressure loss, with drop scaling closer to the square of flow rate.
The velocity profile is much flatter than the laminar parabola because turbulent mixing
transports momentum across the pipe, and the transition between the regimes occurs over a
range rather than at a sharp threshold, with the transition point sensitive to disturbances,
surface roughness, and inlet conditions.

The energy cascade is the central conceptual picture of turbulence. Energy enters at large
scales set by the geometry, transfers to progressively smaller eddies through nonlinear
interaction without dissipation, and is finally dissipated by viscosity at the smallest scale.
Kolmogorov's theory predicts that the intermediate range has a universal statistical structure
independent of how the energy was injected, giving the observed spectrum with a characteristic
power law. The dissipation scale shrinks as Reynolds number rises, which is why direct
numerical simulation resolving all scales is computationally infeasible for practical Reynolds
numbers — the required grid points scale as Reynolds number to the power nine-fourths.

Boundary layer separation is the phenomenon that dominates practical aerodynamics and
hydrodynamics. Where the pressure rises in the flow direction — an adverse gradient — fluid
near the wall, already slowed by friction, may lack the momentum to continue and reverses,
detaching the layer from the surface. The resulting wake is a low-pressure region producing
pressure drag far exceeding skin friction, and on a wing it causes stall. Streamlining is
fundamentally about delaying separation by making the pressure recovery gradual, which is why a
teardrop shape has a small fraction of the drag of a sphere with the same frontal area.

The dimpled golf ball is the counterintuitive case worth understanding. Dimples trip the
boundary layer into turbulence, which increases skin friction and, far more importantly, gives
the layer more momentum near the wall so it resists separation longer. The wake narrows
substantially, pressure drag falls, and the net drag is roughly halved — an increase in one
drag component producing a large decrease in the total.

## Compressible Flow and Applications

When flow speed becomes comparable to the speed of sound, density changes are no longer
negligible and the character of the flow changes qualitatively. The Mach number, the ratio of
flow speed to sound speed, is the governing parameter, with compressibility effects becoming
significant above roughly 0.3 and dominant near and above one.

The most striking consequence is that the relationship between area and velocity reverses. In
subsonic flow, a constriction accelerates the flow, as continuity and Bernoulli require. In
supersonic flow, a constriction decelerates it and an expansion accelerates it, because the
density change outweighs the area change. This is why a converging-diverging nozzle is required
to produce supersonic exhaust: the flow accelerates to exactly sonic at the throat and
continues accelerating in the diverging section, which is the geometry of every rocket nozzle
and every supersonic wind tunnel.

Shock waves are discontinuities that form when a supersonic flow must adjust to conditions it
cannot reach smoothly. Across a normal shock, the flow abruptly becomes subsonic with a rise in
pressure, density, and temperature and a loss of stagnation pressure — an irreversible process
that generates entropy. Oblique shocks turn the flow through an angle and are what forms on
supersonic aircraft, and the sonic boom is the pressure discontinuity of the shock system
sweeping across the ground.

Computational fluid dynamics has become the primary analysis tool, and its central practical
issue is turbulence modelling. Resolving all turbulent scales directly is infeasible at
engineering Reynolds numbers, so the effect of the unresolved scales is modelled. Reynolds-
averaged approaches model all turbulence and are cheap, robust, and dependent on empirical
constants calibrated for particular flow classes — they are reliable for attached flows and
unreliable for separation, which is exactly what many engineering questions are about. Large
eddy simulation resolves the large scales and models only the small, at far greater cost and
with substantially better fidelity for separated and unsteady flows.

The consequence for anyone using these tools is that a computational result requires
validation rather than acceptance. Grid convergence must be demonstrated by refining the mesh
and showing the answer stops changing. The turbulence model must be appropriate for the flow
regime. Boundary conditions must reflect the real situation, and the near-wall mesh resolution
must satisfy the requirements of the wall treatment being used. A converged solution on an
inadequate mesh with an inappropriate model produces a colourful and confident picture of
something that did not happen, and the discipline of validating against experimental data for
a similar configuration is what separates useful computation from decoration.
