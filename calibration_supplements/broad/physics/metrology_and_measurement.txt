## Units and the Redefined SI

Measurement requires a unit, and the question of what defines a unit has a history worth knowing
because the modern answer is structurally different from the historical one. A unit defined by an
artifact — a particular metal bar, a particular cylinder — is only as stable as the artifact, and
comparing against it requires physical access to it or to a copy whose relationship to it is
uncertain.

The 2019 redefinition completed a shift begun with the metre: every base unit is now defined by
fixing the numerical value of a fundamental constant. The second is defined by fixing a caesium
transition frequency, the metre by fixing the speed of light, the kilogram by fixing the Planck
constant, the ampere by fixing the elementary charge, the kelvin by fixing the Boltzmann constant,
the mole by fixing the Avogadro constant, and the candela by fixing a luminous efficacy.

The consequences are practical rather than philosophical. A definition based on a constant can be
realized anywhere by anyone with adequate apparatus rather than requiring comparison against a
unique object. It cannot drift, since the constant is fixed by definition rather than measured.
And the realization can improve without redefining the unit — a better experiment produces a more
accurate realization of the same definition, where previously an improved measurement of the
artifact changed what the unit meant.

Units and the SI:
  artifact definition  only as stable as the object; comparison requires access
  constant definition  realizable anywhere, cannot drift, and improves without
                       redefinition
  second               a fixed caesium hyperfine transition frequency
  metre                a fixed speed of light; defined via the second
  kilogram             a fixed Planck constant; realized by the Kibble balance and
                       by the silicon sphere route
  ampere               a fixed elementary charge
  kelvin               a fixed Boltzmann constant
  mole                 a fixed Avogadro constant; now independent of the kilogram
  candela              a fixed luminous efficacy; the only base unit incorporating
                       human perception
  derived units        combinations; newton, joule, watt, volt, ohm
  dimensional analysis  check that both sides of an equation have the same
                       dimensions; catches a large fraction of algebra errors
  the hierarchy        the second is the most accurately realized, and units
                       defined through it inherit that accuracy

Q: A unit defined by fixing a fundamental constant rather than by an artifact has the advantage
that:
A. It is easier to understand
B. It can be realized independently anywhere, cannot drift, and its realization can improve
   without changing the definition
C. It requires no apparatus
D. It is more precise by definition
Reasoning: An artifact definition makes the unit whatever that object is, so the unit drifts if the
object does and improving the measurement changes the unit. Fixing a constant separates the
definition from any realization, so better apparatus produces a better realization of an unchanged
unit and any laboratory with adequate equipment can realize it. Answer: B.

## Uncertainty

Every measurement has uncertainty, and a measurement reported without it is incomplete rather
than merely undocumented. The uncertainty states the range within which the true value is believed
to lie, together with the confidence attached, and it is what permits two measurements to be
compared or a measurement to be compared against a specification.

The distinction between error and uncertainty is worth keeping precise. Error is the difference
between the measured and the true value, which is unknown since the true value is unknown.
Uncertainty is the quantified doubt about the result, which is what can be stated. A known error is
corrected rather than reported; what remains after correction is uncertainty.

Uncertainty components are classified by how they are evaluated rather than by their nature. Type A
evaluation uses statistical analysis of repeated observations, giving the standard deviation of the
mean. Type B evaluation uses any other means — calibration certificates, manufacturer
specifications, physical reasoning, prior experience — converted to a standard uncertainty by
assuming a distribution. The classification is by method rather than by whether the component is
random or systematic, which is a common confusion.

Uncertainty:
  error                measured minus true; unknown, and corrected when known
  uncertainty          quantified doubt about the result; what is reported
  type A               evaluated statistically from repeated observations
  type B               evaluated by other means; converted assuming a distribution
  the classification   by method, not by random versus systematic
  standard uncertainty  expressed as a standard deviation
  combined uncertainty  components combined in quadrature when independent
  correlated components  do not combine in quadrature; the covariance term matters
                       and is frequently omitted
  expanded uncertainty  multiplied by a coverage factor; k = 2 for approximately
                       95% coverage
  coverage factor      state it, or the interval's meaning is undefined
  degrees of freedom   how well the uncertainty itself is known; few repeats give
                       a poorly known uncertainty
  sensitivity coefficient  how much the result changes per unit change in an input
  propagation          combine input uncertainties weighted by their sensitivity
                       coefficients
  significant figures  a crude uncertainty statement; report the uncertainty
                       explicitly instead
  the dominant term    identify it; reducing a component that is not dominant
                       accomplishes nothing

The quadrature combination deserves emphasis because it determines where effort is worth
spending. Independent uncertainty components combine as the square root of the sum of squares, so
a component half the size of the dominant one contributes only about ten percent to the total, and
one a third the size contributes about five percent. Effort spent reducing a minor component is
therefore nearly wasted, and identifying the dominant term before optimizing is what makes
measurement improvement efficient.

## Calibration and Traceability

Calibration establishes the relationship between an instrument's indication and the corresponding
value of the measurand, and it is what converts a reading into a measurement. An uncalibrated
instrument produces numbers whose relationship to reality is unknown, however precise and
repeatable those numbers are.

Traceability is the property that a measurement result can be related to a reference through a
documented unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to the uncertainty. This is what makes
measurements made in different places comparable — two laboratories whose instruments are traceable
to the same reference are measuring the same thing, and two whose instruments are not have no basis
for comparing their results.

The distinction between accuracy and precision is the one most often confused and it has direct
practical consequences. Precision is repeatability — how much the readings scatter. Accuracy is
closeness to the true value. An instrument can be precise and inaccurate, producing consistently
wrong readings, and this is the dangerous case because the consistency inspires confidence. It is
corrected by calibration; scatter is reduced by averaging, and no amount of averaging corrects an
offset.

Calibration and traceability:
  calibration          establishes the relationship between indication and value
  traceability         an unbroken documented chain to a reference
  the chain            each link adds uncertainty; the total accumulates
  primary standard     realizes the unit directly
  transfer standard    carries the value between laboratories
  working standard     used routinely; calibrated against a higher standard
  accuracy             closeness to the true value; corrected by calibration
  precision            repeatability; reduced by averaging
  the dangerous case   precise and inaccurate; consistency inspires confidence
  bias                 systematic offset; averaging does not remove it
  drift                change over time; sets the calibration interval
  calibration interval  from the observed drift, not from convention
  test uncertainty ratio  the standard's uncertainty relative to the tolerance
                       being verified; 4:1 is the conventional minimum
  guard banding        tightening the acceptance limit by the measurement
                       uncertainty; required when the uncertainty is not negligible
                       relative to the tolerance
  as-found and as-left  the readings before and after adjustment; the as-found
                       data is what tells you whether previous measurements were
                       valid

The as-found reading deserves emphasis because it is what makes calibration retrospectively
useful. An instrument found out of tolerance at calibration was presumably out of tolerance for
some period before, which calls into question every measurement made with it since the last
calibration. Recording the as-found value permits assessing how far out it was and therefore what
the consequences are, and discarding it in favour of only recording the adjusted state destroys
exactly the information needed to determine whether any previous result was affected.

## Precision Measurement Techniques

Precision measurement techniques share a set of strategies for defeating the noise and drift that
limit ordinary measurement, and understanding the strategies transfers across specific
applications.

Null methods compare the unknown against a reference and adjust until the difference is zero,
which means the detector's accuracy does not matter — only its ability to detect zero. A bridge
circuit measuring resistance by balancing against known resistances is accurate to the standards'
accuracy rather than to the detector's, which is why bridges dominated precision electrical
measurement.

Differential measurement measures the difference between two similar quantities rather than each
separately, which cancels whatever affects both. Two adjacent sensors in the same thermal and
electromagnetic environment share their disturbances, so the difference is far cleaner than either
signal.

Modulation moves the measurement to a frequency where the noise is lower. Low-frequency noise
grows as frequency falls — the ubiquitous flicker noise — so measuring a slowly varying quantity
directly puts the signal exactly where the noise is worst. Chopping the signal at a chosen
frequency and detecting synchronously at that frequency, which is what a lock-in amplifier does,
recovers signals far below the broadband noise floor.

Precision techniques:
  null method          balance against a reference; the detector needs only to
                       detect zero
  bridge circuit       the electrical null method
  substitution         replace the unknown with a known that produces the same
                       indication; eliminates the instrument's nonlinearity
  differential         measure the difference; cancels common disturbances
  ratio measurement    ratios are frequently measurable far more accurately than
                       absolute values
  modulation           move the signal away from low-frequency noise
  lock-in detection    synchronous detection at the modulation frequency; extracts
                       signals far below the noise
  averaging            random noise falls as the square root of the count;
                       systematic error does not fall at all
  interferometry       measure displacement in wavelengths; the standard for
                       length at high precision
  frequency comparison  frequencies are measurable more accurately than any other
                       quantity, so converting a measurement into a frequency
                       measurement is a recurring strategy
  temperature control  the dominant systematic in most precision work
  vibration isolation  and acoustic isolation; the second dominant systematic
  Abbe error           an offset between the measurement axis and the axis of
                       interest turns an angular error into a linear one
  cosine error         a misaligned measurement axis reads short by the cosine of
                       the angle

The strategy of converting a measurement into a frequency measurement deserves emphasis because
it explains a great deal of precision instrument design. Frequency is measurable to parts in ten to
the eighteenth, better than any other physical quantity by many orders of magnitude, because
counting cycles over a long interval accumulates precision. A quantity converted into a frequency
shift — a resonator whose frequency depends on the measurand, an interferometer whose fringe rate
depends on velocity, an atomic transition whose frequency depends on a field — inherits that
precision. This is why quartz crystal microbalances, optical frequency combs, and atomic
magnetometers are built the way they are.

Two identifiable contributions and one residual.

Your two-wire connection puts the lead resistance in series with the sensor, and the instrument
cannot distinguish it from the sensor's own resistance. At 0.8 ohms of lead resistance with a
platinum RTD, that is about 0.21 degrees of offset, always in the same direction. A four-wire
connection carries the excitation current on one pair and senses the voltage on another that
carries no current, so the lead resistance drops out entirely. This is the single largest and most
straightforward fix.

Self-heating from the excitation current contributes about 0.06 degrees. One milliamp through the
sensor dissipates power that raises its own temperature above the medium's, and the magnitude
depends on the thermal coupling — worse in still air, better in flowing liquid. Reducing the
excitation reduces the heating quadratically and reduces the signal linearly, so there is an
optimum. Measuring at two excitation levels and extrapolating to zero is the standard way to
quantify it.

The remainder is the sensor's own uncalibrated tolerance, which for a standard-grade element is a
few tenths of a degree. No wiring change addresses it; it requires calibration against a reference,
which gives you a correction to apply.

The general point worth taking from this: your repeatability of 0.01 degrees tells you what your
resolution and noise permit, and it says nothing whatever about accuracy. The two are independent,
and a highly repeatable instrument that is consistently wrong is the more dangerous situation
because the consistency reads as reliability. Comparing against a traceable reference is the only
thing that establishes accuracy, and it is what converts your readings from relative to absolute.

## Time, Frequency, and Statistical Treatment

Time and frequency are the most accurately realized quantities, and their accuracy propagates to
everything defined through them. A caesium standard realizes the second to parts in ten to the
sixteenth, and optical clocks based on transitions at far higher frequency reach parts in ten to
the eighteenth — accurate enough that the gravitational redshift over a centimetre of height
difference is measurable.

Frequency stability is characterized differently from other measurements because the noise is not
white. The Allan deviation, computed from the differences between successive averages, is used
instead of the standard deviation because the standard deviation of a signal with drift does not
converge as the averaging time grows. Plotting the Allan deviation against the averaging time
reveals the noise types present: white noise falls as the inverse square root of the averaging
time, flicker noise is flat, and drift rises — so the plot identifies the optimum averaging time
where the falling and rising contributions cross.

Statistical treatment of measurement data has requirements that general statistics does not
emphasize. The distinction between repeatability — the same operator, instrument, and conditions
over a short interval — and reproducibility — different operators, instruments, or laboratories —
matters because they differ substantially and quoting one when the other is meant misstates the
uncertainty by a large factor.

The Allan deviation deserves the technical emphasis because it answers a question the standard
deviation cannot. For a stationary signal with white noise, averaging longer always improves the
estimate and the standard deviation is the right measure. For a real oscillator with flicker noise
and drift, averaging longer helps up to a point and then makes things worse, and the standard
deviation grows without bound as more data is included — which is the opposite of what a statistic
should do. The Allan deviation is constructed to converge for these signals and to identify which
noise process dominates at each timescale, which is exactly the information needed to choose an
averaging time.

## Instrumentation Practice

Practical measurement has a set of considerations that determine whether the theoretical accuracy
is achieved, and most measurement failures arise here rather than in the instrument.

Loading is the measurement disturbing what it measures. A voltmeter draws current, an ammeter drops
voltage, a thermometer conducts heat, a pressure sensor occupies volume, a flow sensor obstructs.
The disturbance is negligible when the instrument's impedance is far from the source's — high input
impedance for a voltage measurement, low for a current — and it is substantial when they are
comparable, which is exactly the case where the measurement seemed straightforward.

Grounding and shielding determine the noise floor of any sensitive electrical measurement. Ground
loops, where two points nominally at ground are connected by two paths, admit circulating current
that appears as noise. Single-point grounding at low frequency and a continuous reference plane at
high frequency are the standard responses, and the transition between them is a source of
persistent confusion.

Environmental effects are the systematic errors that appear when the measurement moves from the
laboratory. Temperature affects almost every sensor and most instruments. Humidity affects
capacitive sensors and insulation resistance. Vibration affects anything mechanical. Ambient light
affects optical measurements. Electromagnetic interference affects everything electrical. Each is
characterizable and each is more easily controlled than corrected.

Instrumentation practice:
  loading              the measurement disturbs the measurand
  input impedance      far from the source impedance, in the appropriate direction
  four-wire            separates excitation from sensing; eliminates lead
                       resistance
  guarding             drive a shield to the signal potential; eliminates leakage

Thermal electromotive force deserves the closing emphasis because it dominates precision
low-voltage measurement and is invisible to anyone not looking for it. Any junction between
dissimilar metals generates a voltage proportional to its temperature, typically microvolts per
degree, and a measurement circuit contains many such junctions — connectors, solder joints,
switch contacts, the transition from copper wire to a component lead. If the junctions are at
different temperatures the voltages do not cancel, and the resulting offset is comparable to or
larger than the signal in any measurement below the millivolt level. The remedies are keeping
junction pairs isothermal, using low-thermal-EMF connectors and solder, allowing thermal
equilibration, and reversing the measurement polarity and averaging — which cancels the offset
because it does not reverse with the signal.
