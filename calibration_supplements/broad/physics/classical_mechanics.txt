## Kinematics and Newtonian Dynamics

Newton's three laws organize all of classical dynamics. The first defines the inertial
frame: a body with no net force maintains constant velocity, so any frame in which
this holds is inertial, and the law is best read as a statement about which frames the
second law applies in rather than as a special case of it. The second, F = dp/dt,
relates net force to the rate of change of momentum, reducing to F = ma only when mass
is constant — a caveat that matters for rockets, conveyor belts, and any system
shedding or accumulating mass. The third states that forces come in equal and opposite
pairs acting on *different* bodies, and the emphasis on "different" is what prevents the
common error of concluding that all forces cancel and nothing ever accelerates.

Free-body diagrams are the practical core of problem-solving, and most errors are
bookkeeping rather than conceptual. Draw one body at a time, include every force acting
*on* it and none of the forces it exerts on others, choose axes that align with the
expected acceleration, and resolve into components. On an inclined plane, tilting the
axes to lie along and perpendicular to the surface reduces the problem to two scalar
equations with the acceleration entirely in one of them. Normal force is a constraint
force whose magnitude adjusts to whatever prevents interpenetration, so it is never
simply "mg" except in the special case of a horizontal surface with no vertical
acceleration.

Friction has two regimes governed by different rules. Static friction is a constraint
force taking whatever value up to mu_s N is needed to prevent relative sliding;
writing f = mu_s N when the object is not on the verge of slipping is a standard and
consequential error. Kinetic friction, once sliding begins, has the definite magnitude
mu_k N opposing relative motion, with mu_k typically somewhat less than mu_s, which is
why an object breaks loose and then accelerates. Neither depends on contact area to
first order, a genuinely counterintuitive result explained by real contact occurring at
microscopic asperities whose total area scales with load rather than with apparent
area.

Kinematic relations for constant acceleration:
  v      = v0 + a t
  x      = x0 + v0 t + (1/2) a t^2
  v^2    = v0^2 + 2 a (x - x0)              time-independent; use when t is unwanted
  x      = x0 + (1/2)(v0 + v) t             average-velocity form

Projectile motion is the canonical application: horizontal and vertical motions
decouple because gravity acts only vertically, so the horizontal velocity is constant
and the vertical follows the constant-acceleration relations with a = -g. Range on
level ground is v0^2 sin(2 theta)/g, maximized at 45 degrees, and complementary angles
give equal range with different flight times. Air resistance breaks the decoupling
entirely, since drag depends on total speed and couples the components; for a
quadratic drag law there is no closed-form trajectory and the optimal launch angle
drops well below 45 degrees.

Q: A block sits on an incline without sliding, at an angle below the critical angle.
The friction force on it is:
A. mu_s * N
B. mg sin(theta), directed up the slope
C. mu_k * N
D. Zero
Reasoning: The block is in equilibrium, so friction must exactly balance the component
of gravity along the slope, which is mg sin(theta). Static friction is a constraint
force taking whatever value is required up to its maximum of mu_s N; it equals that
maximum only at the verge of slipping. Answer: B.

Q: A rocket in deep space ejects mass. Its acceleration is correctly obtained from:
A. F = ma with m constant
B. F = dp/dt, accounting for the changing mass and exhaust momentum
C. Conservation of kinetic energy
D. The impulse-momentum theorem with constant mass
Reasoning: Mass is not constant, so F = ma does not apply. The correct treatment tracks
total momentum of rocket plus exhaust, yielding the Tsiolkovsky equation delta_v = v_e
ln(m_initial/m_final). Kinetic energy is not conserved because the chemical energy of
the propellant is converted. Answer: B.

## Conservation Laws

Conservation laws are the most powerful tools in mechanics because they relate initial
and final states without requiring the trajectory between them. Energy is conserved
when all forces are conservative — derivable from a potential — which excludes
friction and other dissipative forces. Momentum is conserved for any system with no net
external force, regardless of the internal forces' nature, which is why it survives
collisions where energy does not. Angular momentum is conserved when there is no net
external torque.

Noether's theorem explains why these three and not others: each continuous symmetry of
the action yields a conserved quantity. Time-translation symmetry gives energy
conservation, space-translation symmetry gives momentum, rotational symmetry gives
angular momentum. This is not a reformulation but an explanation, and it tells you
immediately when a conservation law fails — a time-dependent potential breaks
time-translation symmetry and energy is not conserved, which is exactly the case of a
driven oscillator.

Collisions are classified by what happens to kinetic energy. Momentum is conserved in
all of them. Elastic collisions conserve kinetic energy as well, and in one dimension
the relative velocity of approach equals the relative velocity of separation — a
relation often faster to apply than the quadratic energy equation. Perfectly inelastic
collisions have the bodies moving together afterward, which is the maximum kinetic
energy loss consistent with momentum conservation. The coefficient of restitution
interpolates between the two, being the ratio of separation to approach speed.

Energy bookkeeping reference:
  kinetic, translational   K = (1/2) m v^2
  kinetic, rotational      K = (1/2) I omega^2
  gravitational, local     U = m g h
  gravitational, general   U = -G M m / r          zero at infinity, hence negative
  elastic (spring)         U = (1/2) k x^2
  work by a force          W = integral F . dr     path-independent iff conservative
  power                    P = F . v = dE/dt
  work-energy theorem      W_net = delta K         always, conservative or not

The distinction between the local and general gravitational potentials repays
attention. U = mgh is the first-order expansion of -GMm/r near the surface, valid only
while the height change is small compared with the planet's radius. The general form
being negative and approaching zero at infinity is a choice of reference point, and it
makes the escape condition transparent: a bound orbit has negative total energy, a
parabolic escape trajectory has exactly zero, and a hyperbolic one has positive energy
with speed remaining at infinity.

The virial theorem relates time-averaged kinetic and potential energies for a bound
system with a power-law potential: 2 average(K) = -n average(U) for U proportional to
r^n. For gravity, n = -1, giving 2K = -U, so the total energy is E = K + U = -K. This
has a startling consequence used constantly in astrophysics: a self-gravitating system
that loses energy — by radiating — gets *hotter*, since K = -E increases as E becomes
more negative. Stars contract and heat as they radiate, and gravitationally bound
systems therefore have an effective negative heat capacity.

## Rotational Motion

Rotational dynamics parallels translational dynamics with each quantity replaced by its
angular analogue: mass by moment of inertia, force by torque, momentum by angular
momentum, velocity by angular velocity. The parallel is exact, so every translational
result has a rotational counterpart, and the equation tau = I alpha is the rotational
second law.

Moment of inertia depends on the axis, not just on the body, and this is the main
practical difference from mass. It is the integral of r^2 dm with r measured
perpendicular to the axis, so mass far from the axis counts quadratically more. The
parallel axis theorem, I = I_cm + M d^2, gives the moment about any axis parallel to
one through the centre of mass, and it shows that the centre-of-mass axis always
minimizes the moment of inertia among parallel axes.

Moments of inertia about the centre of mass:
  point mass at radius r          m r^2
  thin ring / hoop, axis normal   M R^2
  solid disc / cylinder           (1/2) M R^2
  solid sphere                    (2/5) M R^2
  thin spherical shell            (2/3) M R^2
  rod, axis through centre        (1/12) M L^2
  rod, axis through end           (1/3) M L^2
  parallel axis theorem           I = I_cm + M d^2

The classic demonstration is a race down an incline. Objects rolling without slipping
share their energy between translation and rotation, and the split depends only on the
shape through the dimensionless factor c in I = c M R^2 — not on mass or radius at all.
The acceleration is g sin(theta)/(1 + c), so a solid sphere with c = 2/5 accelerates
at 5/7 g sin(theta), a disc with c = 1/2 at 2/3, a hoop with c = 1 at only half. A
sphere beats a disc beats a hoop, always, regardless of their masses or sizes, and any
of them loses to a frictionless sliding block that need not spin up at all.

Angular momentum conservation produces the most visible effects in mechanics. A skater
pulling in their arms reduces I and therefore increases omega, with kinetic energy
increasing because the skater does work pulling mass inward against the centripetal
requirement. A gyroscope precesses rather than falling because the torque from gravity
is perpendicular to the angular momentum and therefore rotates its direction rather
than changing its magnitude, with precession rate Omega = tau / L — faster spin means
slower precession, which is why a rapidly spinning top stands nearly upright and a
slowing one begins to wobble and fall.

[assistant] A solid steel disc of 400 mm diameter needs about 127 kg and would be
roughly 128 mm thick. That comes from E = (1/2) I omega^2 with omega = 628 rad/s,
requiring I = 2.53 kg m^2, and I = (1/2) M R^2 for a solid disc.

The stress number is the one that should drive your design. Rim speed is 126 m/s, and
the peak hoop stress in a spinning disc scales as rho times rim speed squared — it is
296 MPa here against a 710 MPa yield for 4340, so a safety factor of 2.4. That is
acceptable but not generous for a rotating part where failure is energetic.

The key scaling to internalize: stress depends on rim *speed*, not on radius or rpm
separately, so you cannot make the flywheel safer by scaling it up at the same tip
speed. Specific energy per unit mass is likewise set by the material's strength-to-
density ratio, not by geometry. If you need more margin, the levers are a
higher-strength alloy, a composite rim where carbon fibre's strength-to-density ratio
is several times steel's, or accepting a lower rpm and a heavier wheel.

Two practical points beyond the sizing. A solid disc is not the most mass-efficient
geometry — a thick-rim design puts mass where the r^2 weighting pays, giving more
inertia per kilogram, though it raises the stress concentration at the hub. And at 127
kg spinning at 6000 rpm you are storing the energy equivalent of roughly 120 grams of
TNT, so containment design and a burst shield are not optional.

## Oscillations

Simple harmonic motion arises whenever a restoring force is proportional to
displacement, F = -kx. The equation of motion m x'' + k x = 0 has solution x = A
cos(omega t + phi) with omega = sqrt(k/m). The defining property is that the period is
independent of amplitude, which is why pendulum clocks work and why the harmonic
approximation is so useful: any smooth potential near a minimum is locally quadratic,
so small oscillations about any stable equilibrium are harmonic with omega =
sqrt(U''(x0)/m).

Damping adds a velocity-dependent force, giving m x'' + b x' + k x = 0. The behaviour
depends on the damping ratio zeta = b/(2 sqrt(mk)). Underdamped (zeta < 1) oscillates
with exponentially decaying amplitude at a frequency slightly below the natural one.
Critically damped (zeta = 1) returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without
overshoot, which is the design target for door closers, vehicle suspensions, and
control loops. Overdamped (zeta > 1) returns without oscillating but more slowly,
because the large damping force itself impedes the approach — a fact that surprises
people who assume more damping is always faster.

Driven oscillators exhibit resonance. Forcing at frequency omega_d produces a
steady-state response whose amplitude peaks near the natural frequency, with the peak
height set by the quality factor Q = 1/(2 zeta). High Q gives a tall narrow resonance
and long ringing; low Q gives a broad shallow one that settles quickly. The response
also shifts phase, lagging the drive by 90 degrees exactly at resonance and
approaching 180 degrees well above it. Resonance is either the mechanism or the enemy
depending on the application: it is how radios select a station, how NMR works, and
how a wine glass shatters, and it is why bridges, buildings, and rotating machinery are
designed with their natural frequencies deliberately placed away from expected
excitation.

Oscillator quantities:
  natural frequency        omega_0 = sqrt(k/m)
  period                   T = 2 pi / omega_0
  damping ratio            zeta = b / (2 sqrt(mk))
  damped frequency         omega_d = omega_0 sqrt(1 - zeta^2)
  quality factor           Q = 1/(2 zeta) = omega_0 m / b
  energy decay             E(t) = E_0 exp(-omega_0 t / Q)
  resonant amplification   A_peak / A_static ~ Q for lightly damped systems
  simple pendulum          omega = sqrt(g/L), small angles only
  physical pendulum        omega = sqrt(m g d / I)
  torsional                omega = sqrt(kappa / I)

Coupled oscillators introduce normal modes: special patterns of motion in which every
part oscillates at a common frequency with fixed relative amplitudes. Any motion of the
system decomposes into a superposition of normal modes, which is an eigenvalue problem
in disguise — the mode shapes are eigenvectors of the stiffness matrix relative to the
mass matrix, and the squared frequencies are the eigenvalues. This is the bridge from
mechanics to linear algebra and, in the continuum limit, to Fourier analysis and the
wave equation.

Q: A damped oscillator has Q = 50. After how many oscillation periods does its energy
fall to about 1/e of its initial value?
A. 50   B. 8   C. 25   D. 314
Reasoning: Energy decays as exp(-omega_0 t / Q), so it falls to 1/e when omega_0 t = Q,
that is t = Q/omega_0. One period is T = 2 pi / omega_0, so the number of periods is
t/T = Q/(2 pi) = 50/6.28 ≈ 8. The useful rule is that Q is roughly 2 pi times the
number of oscillations in an energy decay time. Answer: B.

Q: A vehicle suspension is designed to be critically damped rather than overdamped
because:
A. It uses less energy
B. It returns to equilibrium fastest without overshoot
C. It has a higher resonant frequency
D. It is cheaper to manufacture
Reasoning: Critical damping is precisely the boundary case minimizing settling time
subject to no overshoot. Overdamped systems do not overshoot either but return more
slowly, since the damping force resists the return. Underdamped systems return quickly
but oscillate. Answer: B.

## Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Mechanics

The Lagrangian formulation replaces forces with energies. Define L = T - V, the kinetic
minus potential energy expressed in any convenient generalized coordinates, and the
equations of motion follow from the Euler-Lagrange equation, d/dt(dL/dq') - dL/dq = 0,
one for each coordinate. The advantages are substantial: constraint forces drop out
automatically because the coordinates are chosen to satisfy the constraints, the
formulation is coordinate-independent so polar or spherical coordinates require no
special treatment, and energies are scalars while forces are vectors that must be
resolved.

This comes from Hamilton's principle: the physical trajectory is the one that makes the
action, the time integral of L, stationary among all paths with fixed endpoints. That a
variational principle underlies mechanics is one of the deepest facts in physics, and
the same structure recurs in optics as Fermat's principle, in general relativity
through the Einstein-Hilbert action, and in quantum mechanics where Feynman's path
integral sums exp(iS/hbar) over all paths and the classical trajectory emerges as the
stationary-phase contribution.

Cyclic coordinates make conservation laws automatic. If L does not depend explicitly on
a coordinate q, then the corresponding conjugate momentum p = dL/dq' is conserved. A
central potential's Lagrangian has no explicit dependence on the azimuthal angle, so
angular momentum is conserved without further argument. If L has no explicit time
dependence, the Hamiltonian is conserved, and when the kinetic energy is quadratic in
the velocities and the constraints are time-independent, that Hamiltonian is the total
energy. This is Noether's theorem in its most usable form.

The Hamiltonian formulation performs a Legendre transform to generalized momenta,
giving H(q, p) = sum p q' - L and the symmetric pair of first-order equations q' =
dH/dp and p' = -dH/dq. Trading n second-order equations for 2n first-order ones is not
obviously progress, but it opens phase space as the natural arena. Liouville's theorem
states that phase-space volume is conserved under Hamiltonian flow, which is the
foundation of statistical mechanics. Canonical transformations preserve the structure
and lead to Hamilton-Jacobi theory and action-angle variables. And the Poisson bracket
{f, g} obeys algebraic relations that map directly onto the quantum commutator, making
the Hamiltonian formalism the natural bridge to quantum mechanics.

Worked comparison — bead on a rotating hoop, radius R, angular velocity Omega:
  Newtonian:   resolve gravity, normal force, and the rotating-frame pseudo-forces
               along the hoop; requires care with centrifugal and Coriolis terms
  Lagrangian:  T = (1/2) m R^2 (theta'^2 + Omega^2 sin^2 theta)
               V = -m g R cos theta
               L = T - V, then Euler-Lagrange in theta alone
  result:      R theta'' = R Omega^2 sin theta cos theta - g sin theta
  equilibria:  theta = 0 always; additionally cos theta = g/(R Omega^2) when
               Omega^2 > g/R — a pitchfork bifurcation at the critical rotation rate

That bifurcation is the physical content: below a critical spin rate the bead sits at
the bottom, and above it the bottom becomes unstable and two new stable equilibria
appear off-axis. The Lagrangian route delivers this in a few lines with no vector
resolution and no explicit treatment of the constraint force holding the bead on the
hoop, which is exactly the kind of problem where the formalism earns its abstraction.

## Chaos and Continuum Mechanics

Deterministic systems can be unpredictable. A chaotic system has sensitive dependence
on initial conditions: nearby trajectories diverge exponentially at a rate given by the
largest Lyapunov exponent, so an uncertainty of epsilon in the initial state grows to
order one after a time proportional to ln(1/epsilon). Because that dependence is
logarithmic, improving initial measurements by a factor of a thousand buys only a
constant additive increase in the prediction horizon. This is why weather forecasting
has a hard practical limit of a couple of weeks regardless of instrumentation, and it
is a statement about the dynamics rather than about the model's quality.

Chaos requires nonlinearity and at least three dimensions in a continuous autonomous
system, since the Poincare-Bendixson theorem confines two-dimensional flows to fixed
points and limit cycles. Discrete maps can be chaotic in one dimension, the logistic
map x -> r x(1 - x) being the canonical example, which undergoes a period-doubling
cascade to chaos as r increases, with the ratio of successive bifurcation intervals
approaching Feigenbaum's constant 4.669. That this constant is universal across
entirely different systems with a quadratic maximum was the discovery that made chaos a
subject rather than a collection of curiosities.

The double pendulum is the standard mechanical example: two coupled rigid pendulums,
four-dimensional phase space, fully deterministic, and chaotic for all but small
amplitudes. Its equations follow in a few lines from the Lagrangian and are hopeless to
derive by force balance. Strange attractors — the Lorenz attractor from a truncated
convection model being the famous case — are the geometric signature: trajectories are
bounded and never repeat, converging onto a fractal set of non-integer dimension.

Continuum mechanics treats matter as a continuous field rather than as particles, valid
when the length scales of interest greatly exceed the molecular mean free path. Stress
is a rank-two tensor mapping a surface normal to the traction vector across it, with
the diagonal entries normal stresses and the off-diagonal ones shear. Strain is the
symmetric gradient of the displacement field. For a linear elastic isotropic material
they are related by two constants, conventionally Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio,
which reduce to bulk and shear moduli for volumetric and shape changes respectively.

Continuum reference:
  Cauchy stress tensor        sigma_ij; symmetric by angular momentum balance
  small strain                eps_ij = (1/2)(du_i/dx_j + du_j/dx_i)
  Hooke's law, 1D             sigma = E eps
  Poisson's ratio             nu = -transverse strain / axial strain, ~0.3 metals
  shear modulus               G = E / (2(1 + nu))
  bulk modulus                K = E / (3(1 - 2 nu))
  wave speeds                 c_longitudinal = sqrt(E/rho), c_shear = sqrt(G/rho)
  Navier-Stokes               rho(dv/dt + v.grad v) = -grad p + mu lap v + f
  Reynolds number             Re = rho v L / mu; inertial over viscous forces

The Reynolds number is the single most important dimensionless group in fluid
mechanics because it determines the character of the flow rather than merely its
magnitude. Below roughly 2,000 in a pipe the flow is laminar, with smooth layers and a
parabolic velocity profile, and the pressure drop is linear in flow rate. Above roughly
4,000 it is turbulent, with chaotic eddies, a flatter profile, far greater mixing, and
a pressure drop closer to quadratic in flow rate. Between the two lies a transitional
regime sensitive to disturbances. Dynamic similarity means two flows with the same
Reynolds number behave alike regardless of scale, which is what makes wind-tunnel and
towing-tank testing of scale models meaningful, and what forces model tests to
compensate with altered fluid density, viscosity, or velocity to match the full-scale
number.
