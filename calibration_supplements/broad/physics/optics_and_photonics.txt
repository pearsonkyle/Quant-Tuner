## Geometric Optics

Geometric optics treats light as rays travelling in straight lines through homogeneous
media, bending at interfaces. It is the short-wavelength limit of wave optics, valid when
the structures involved are much larger than the wavelength, and it is entirely adequate for
designing lenses, mirrors, and imaging systems until diffraction becomes the limiting factor.

Refraction at an interface follows Snell's law, with the product of refractive index and the
sine of the angle from the normal conserved across the boundary. Light entering a denser
medium bends toward the normal and slows, since the refractive index is the ratio of the
speed of light in vacuum to that in the medium. Going from dense to less dense, the
refracted ray bends away from the normal, and beyond a critical angle it cannot escape at
all — total internal reflection, which is the mechanism of optical fiber, of prisms used as
mirrors in binoculars, and of the sparkle of a cut diamond, whose high index gives it a
critical angle of only 24 degrees.

The thin lens equation relates object distance, image distance, and focal length, with sign
conventions that must be stated because they differ between texts. Magnification is the
negative ratio of image to object distance, so a real image formed beyond the focal point is
inverted. The distinction between real and virtual images is worth precision: a real image
can be projected onto a screen because light actually converges there, while a virtual image
only appears to exist because diverging rays are extrapolated backward, which is what a
magnifying glass and a plane mirror produce.

Geometric optics relations:
  refractive index      n = c / v;  air ~1.0003, water 1.33, glass 1.5-1.9
  Snell's law           n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2)
  critical angle        sin(theta_c) = n2/n1,  n1 > n2
  thin lens             1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i
  magnification         m = -d_i / d_o
  lensmaker's equation  1/f = (n-1)(1/R1 - 1/R2)
  f-number              N = f / D;  light gathering scales as 1/N^2
  depth of field        increases with f-number, decreases with magnification
  numerical aperture    NA = n sin(theta_max);  resolution and light collection
  mirror equation       same form as thin lens; f = R/2

Aberrations are the departures from ideal imaging and each has a distinct signature.
Spherical aberration arises because a spherical surface does not focus all annuli to the
same point, producing a soft image with no sharp focus; it is corrected by aspheric surfaces
or by combining elements. Coma makes off-axis points image as comet shapes. Astigmatism
gives different focal positions for rays in perpendicular planes. Field curvature means the
sharp image surface is not flat. Distortion changes magnification with field position,
producing barrel or pincushion shapes without blurring. Chromatic aberration comes from the
refractive index varying with wavelength and is corrected by combining glasses with
different dispersions in an achromatic doublet — a defect mirrors do not have at all, which
is one reason large telescopes use them.

Q: A ray passes from glass (n = 1.5) into air. Total internal reflection occurs above an
incidence angle of approximately:
A. 30 degrees   B. 42 degrees   C. 48 degrees   D. It cannot occur
Reasoning: The critical angle satisfies sin(theta_c) = n_air/n_glass = 1/1.5 = 0.667, so
theta_c = 41.8 degrees. Total internal reflection requires going from higher to lower index,
which is satisfied here. Answer: B.

## Wave Optics

Wave optics accounts for interference and diffraction, phenomena geometric optics cannot
describe. Interference occurs when coherent waves superpose, adding constructively where
they are in phase and destructively where they are opposed. The requirement is coherence —
a fixed phase relationship — which is why interference from independent light sources is not
observed and why demonstrations split a single beam.

Young's double slit produces fringes whose spacing depends on wavelength, slit separation,
and screen distance, and it is the canonical demonstration that light is a wave. The same
experiment performed with single photons, or with electrons, produces the same fringe
pattern built up one detection at a time, which is the cleanest statement of wave-particle
duality and remains the most direct demonstration of quantum superposition.

Thin film interference explains the colours of oil slicks, soap bubbles, and anti-reflection
coatings. Light reflecting from the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film interferes, with
the path difference determined by film thickness and index, and a half-wavelength phase shift
occurring on reflection from a higher-index medium. Anti-reflection coatings exploit this
deliberately: a quarter-wave layer with index equal to the geometric mean of the two
surrounding media makes the two reflections cancel, and multilayer stacks extend the
cancellation across a broad band, which is why quality optics appear coloured in reflection
rather than white.

Diffraction is the spreading of waves at apertures and edges, and it sets the fundamental
resolution limit of every optical instrument. A circular aperture produces an Airy pattern
with a central disc surrounded by rings, and two point sources are conventionally resolvable
when the centre of one falls on the first minimum of the other — the Rayleigh criterion,
giving an angular resolution of 1.22 times wavelength divided by aperture diameter.

Wave optics relations:
  double slit maxima     d sin(theta) = m lambda
  single slit minima     a sin(theta) = m lambda
  Rayleigh criterion     theta = 1.22 lambda / D
  diffraction grating    d sin(theta) = m lambda;  resolving power R = mN
  thin film constructive 2 n t = (m + 1/2) lambda, with one phase-inverting
                         reflection
  anti-reflection        n_coat = sqrt(n1 n2),  thickness = lambda/(4 n_coat)
  coherence length       L = c / delta_nu ≈ lambda^2 / delta_lambda
  Abbe diffraction limit d = lambda / (2 NA);  microscope resolution
  Fresnel number         determines near-field versus far-field regime

Coherence length is the practical parameter governing whether interference is observable in
a given configuration. It is the path difference over which a source maintains a fixed phase
relationship, and it is inversely proportional to spectral bandwidth: a white light source
has a coherence length of a few micrometres, an LED perhaps tens, and a stabilized laser can
exceed kilometres. Interferometric measurement requires the path difference to stay within
the coherence length, which is exactly why laser interferometry can measure over long
baselines and white-light interferometry only works within a very narrow range — a property
turned into an advantage in optical coherence tomography, where the short coherence length
provides depth sectioning.

## Polarization

Polarization describes the orientation of the electric field oscillation in an
electromagnetic wave. Unpolarized light, such as that from thermal sources, has rapidly
randomizing orientation. Linear polarization confines the field to one plane; circular
polarization results from two perpendicular components of equal amplitude with a quarter-wave
phase difference, so the field vector rotates; elliptical is the general case.

A linear polarizer transmits the component along its axis. Malus's law gives the transmitted
intensity as the incident intensity times the squared cosine of the angle between the
polarization and the axis. For unpolarized input, a polarizer transmits half regardless of
orientation. The three-polarizer demonstration is the standard surprise: crossed polarizers
transmit nothing, and inserting a third at 45 degrees between them restores transmission,
because each polarizer projects the field onto a new axis rather than merely filtering a
pre-existing component.

Reflection polarizes light, which has direct practical consequence. Light reflecting from a
dielectric surface is preferentially polarized parallel to the surface, and at Brewster's
angle the reflected light is completely polarized. This is why polarized sunglasses reduce
glare from water and roads by blocking the horizontal component, and why photographers use
rotating polarizing filters to control reflections and to darken skies, since scattered
skylight is also partially polarized.

Polarization components and effects:
  polarizer            transmits one linear component; Malus's law
  Brewster's angle     tan(theta_B) = n2/n1;  reflected light fully polarized
  birefringence        different index for different polarizations; calcite,
                       quartz, stressed plastic
  wave plate           introduces a phase difference; quarter-wave converts linear
                       to circular, half-wave rotates linear polarization
  optical activity     rotation of polarization by chiral molecules; used to
                       measure sugar concentration
  Faraday rotation     magnetic-field-induced rotation; non-reciprocal, which is
                       what makes optical isolators possible
  photoelastic         stress-induced birefringence; visualizes stress fields
  LCD                  voltage-controlled birefringence between crossed polarizers
  Kerr and Pockels     electric-field-induced birefringence; fast optical modulators

The non-reciprocity of Faraday rotation deserves emphasis because it enables a component
with no passive equivalent. Most optical effects are reciprocal — light traversing a path
backward experiences the same effect — so a wave plate rotating polarization one way rotates
it back on return. Faraday rotation depends on the magnetic field direction rather than the
propagation direction, so the rotation adds rather than cancels on a return pass. Combining
it with polarizers produces an optical isolator that passes light one way and blocks the
reverse, which is essential in laser systems where back-reflection into the cavity
destabilizes or damages the source.

## Lasers and Light Sources

A laser produces coherent, monochromatic, directional light through stimulated emission. An
excited atom stimulated by a photon of matching energy emits a second photon identical in
frequency, phase, direction, and polarization — this cloning is the origin of coherence and
is what distinguishes laser light from thermal emission.

Three ingredients are required. A gain medium provides the atoms or molecules with a usable
transition. A pump supplies energy to create population inversion, meaning more atoms in the
upper state than the lower, which is necessary because otherwise absorption exceeds
stimulated emission. And an optical cavity, typically two mirrors, feeds light back through
the gain medium so it is amplified repeatedly, with one mirror partially transmitting to let
the beam out.

Population inversion cannot be achieved in a two-level system, because pumping the
transition also stimulates the downward transition and the populations equalize at best.
Three-level and four-level schemes solve this by pumping to a higher state that decays
rapidly into the upper laser level, and in the four-level case having the lower laser level
also empty rapidly so it stays nearly unpopulated. Four-level systems have much lower
threshold pump power for this reason and dominate practical designs.

Laser properties and types:
  monochromatic        narrow linewidth; a stabilized laser can reach sub-hertz
  coherent             long coherence length; enables interferometry and holography
  directional          low divergence set by diffraction from the aperture
  brightness           very high power per unit area per unit solid angle; this,
                       not total power, is what distinguishes a laser
  gas (HeNe, CO2)      good beam quality; CO2 at 10.6 um for material processing
  solid state (Nd:YAG) high power, 1064 nm, frequency-doubled to 532 nm green
  fiber                gain in a doped fiber; efficient, excellent beam quality,
                       now dominant for industrial cutting
  semiconductor        compact, efficient, directly modulated; communications and
                       consumer devices; poorer beam quality without correction
  Ti:sapphire          broadly tunable, ultrashort pulses
  mode locking         fixed phase across cavity modes produces femtosecond pulses
  Q-switching          stores energy then releases it in a nanosecond pulse of
                       enormous peak power

The distinction between average and peak power is essential when working with pulsed lasers
and is a frequent source of both underestimated capability and underestimated hazard. A
mode-locked laser emitting one nanojoule pulses of 100 femtoseconds at 80 megahertz has an
average power of 80 milliwatts and a peak power of 10 kilowatts. The peak is what drives
nonlinear processes and what damages optics; the average is what determines thermal load.
Specifying only one of them describes the source incompletely.

[assistant] You are actually at the theoretical limit for this setup — 30% measured against
a 32% maximum. The problem is not alignment; it is that your diode's beam is highly
asymmetric and single-mode fiber accepts only a symmetric mode.

A laser diode emits from a rectangular aperture, so it diverges very differently in the two
axes: your fast axis has an NA of 0.55 and your slow axis 0.12. Your fiber's NA is 0.14, so
most of the fast-axis light is emitted outside the acceptance cone and simply cannot be
captured no matter how well you align. Even the captured light has poor mode overlap because
its spatial profile is elliptical and the fiber mode is circular.

Three ways forward, depending on what you need.

If you need single-mode output, circularize the beam. A fast-axis collimator — a short-focal
cylindrical microlens placed close to the facet — collimates the divergent axis first, after
which an anamorphic prism pair or a pair of crossed cylindrical lenses equalizes the two
axes. Done well this reaches 60-75% coupling. This is standard practice for diode-to-fiber
coupling and the components are commercially available.

If you only need the power delivered and not a single spatial mode, use multimode fiber. A
105 micron core with 0.22 NA will take essentially everything with simple aspheric coupling,
often above 85%. For pumping a fiber amplifier this is the usual answer, since pump light
does not need to be single mode.

If you need both high power and single mode, buy a fiber-pigtailed diode. The manufacturer
does the beam shaping at the wafer level with far better tolerances than bench assembly
achieves, and the specified coupled power is what you get.

One diagnostic worth running first: measure the free-space beam profile at two distances to
confirm the divergence in each axis matches the datasheet. If the fast axis is worse than
specified the diode may be damaged, and no coupling optics will fix that.

## Fiber, Detectors, and Nonlinear Optics

Optical fiber guides light by total internal reflection between a higher-index core and a
lower-index cladding. Single-mode fiber has a core small enough — around nine micrometres —
that only one spatial mode propagates, eliminating modal dispersion and enabling the
bandwidth-distance products that make long-haul communication possible. Multimode fiber has
a larger core, is far easier to couple into and to splice, and suffers modal dispersion that
limits it to short reaches.

Loss in silica fiber has a minimum near 1550 nanometres, around 0.2 decibels per kilometre,
which is why that wavelength band carries essentially all long-haul traffic. At that loss a
signal travels roughly 100 kilometres before needing amplification, and erbium-doped fiber
amplifiers happen to have gain in exactly that band, which is an accident of materials
science that shaped the entire architecture of global communications.

Dispersion limits how fast data can be sent. Chromatic dispersion arises because different
wavelengths travel at different speeds, so a pulse containing a range of wavelengths spreads
as it propagates, eventually overlapping its neighbours. It is compensated by fiber with
opposite dispersion sign or by digital signal processing at the receiver, which in coherent
systems can undo it almost entirely. Polarization mode dispersion, from residual
birefringence, is stochastic and harder to compensate.

Detectors and fiber quantities:
  photodiode           reverse-biased junction; fast, linear, the workhorse
  PIN vs avalanche     APDs provide internal gain at the cost of excess noise and
                       high bias voltage
  photomultiplier      extremely sensitive, single-photon capable, bulky, fragile
  SPAD / SiPM          solid-state single-photon detection; timing resolution in
                       tens of picoseconds
  CCD / CMOS           imaging arrays; CMOS now dominant on speed and integration
  bolometer            thermal detector; broadband, slow, used in the far infrared
  responsivity         amps per watt; wavelength dependent
  dark current         signal with no light; doubles roughly every 7 degrees C
  NEP                  noise equivalent power; the sensitivity figure of merit
  fiber loss minimum   ~0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm
  dispersion           ~17 ps/(nm km) in standard single-mode fiber at 1550 nm

Nonlinear optics becomes relevant at high intensity, where the material's response is no
longer proportional to the field. Second harmonic generation converts two photons into one
of twice the frequency, which is how green laser pointers produce 532 nanometres from a 1064
nanometre source. It requires a material without inversion symmetry and phase matching, so
that the generated waves from different depths add constructively rather than cancelling —
achieved by exploiting birefringence to equalize the phase velocities, or by periodically
poling the material to reset the phase.

Third-order effects occur in any material, including glass, and dominate fiber behaviour at
high power. The Kerr effect makes the refractive index intensity-dependent, causing
self-phase modulation that broadens a pulse's spectrum, self-focusing that can damage optics,
and cross-phase modulation between wavelength channels. Four-wave mixing generates new
frequencies from combinations of existing ones, which is a nuisance in
wavelength-multiplexed systems and a tool for wavelength conversion and parametric
amplification. Supercontinuum generation, where an intense short pulse in a suitable fiber
broadens into a spectrum spanning an octave or more, combines several of these effects and
produces a source that is simultaneously as bright as a laser and as broadband as a lamp —
which is what made frequency comb metrology possible and earned a Nobel Prize for connecting
optical frequencies to microwave standards.
