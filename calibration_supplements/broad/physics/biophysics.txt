## Scale and Physical Constraints

Biophysics applies physical reasoning to biological systems, and the first thing it establishes
is that the physics at cellular scale is genuinely different from the physics of everyday
experience. Quantities scale with different powers of length, so ratios that are negligible for a
large organism dominate for a small one, and intuitions built at human scale mislead
systematically.

Surface area scales as length squared while volume scales as length cubed, so the surface to
volume ratio rises as size falls. A bacterium has enormous surface relative to its volume, which
means diffusive exchange across its membrane is fast enough to supply its entire interior, and it
also means it loses heat and water rapidly relative to its content. This single scaling
relationship explains why small organisms need no circulatory system, why large ones do, why
cells are the size they are, and why heat loss constrains the minimum size of a warm-blooded
animal.

The Reynolds number for a swimming organism is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, and it
falls with size and speed. A human swimming operates at a Reynolds number around a million, where
inertia dominates and coasting works. A bacterium operates around ten to the minus five, where
viscosity dominates absolutely and inertia is irrelevant — a bacterium that stops swimming halts
within a fraction of an atomic diameter. This is why bacterial propulsion uses rotating helical
flagella rather than the reciprocal motion that works at high Reynolds number, since any
reciprocal motion at low Reynolds number returns the organism exactly to where it started.

Q: A bacterium stops rotating its flagella. The distance it coasts before stopping is closest to:
A. Several body lengths
B. Less than an atomic diameter
C. One body length
D. It does not stop
Reasoning: At a Reynolds number around ten to the minus five, viscous drag dominates inertia
completely, and the coasting distance is of order the size times the Reynolds number — far below
an atomic diameter. This is why bacterial motility requires continuous propulsion and why the
mechanisms are rotational or wave-like rather than reciprocal. Answer: B.

## Molecular Forces and Thermal Noise

The forces holding biological structures together are almost all non-covalent and individually
weak, and this is a design feature rather than a limitation. A structure held by covalent bonds
would be permanent and unable to assemble, disassemble, or respond. Structures held by many weak
interactions are stable in aggregate and reversible, so they self-assemble, they can be
disassembled when required, and they respond to conditions.

The energy scale is what makes this work. Thermal energy at room temperature is about four
piconewton-nanometres, and a hydrogen bond is a few times that, so hydrogen bonds break and reform
continuously on nanosecond timescales. A structure requiring ten simultaneous hydrogen bonds is
stable because the probability of all ten breaking at once is negligible, while any individual bond
is transient — giving both stability and the capacity to rearrange.

Brownian motion is the constant thermal agitation that dominates molecular-scale dynamics, and
its magnitude is what makes biological mechanism unintuitive. A protein in water is buffeted
continuously and moves erratically, so a molecular machine does not push its way forward against
resistance; it uses chemical energy to bias which thermally-driven fluctuations are captured. The
Brownian ratchet framing captures this: thermal motion supplies the movement and the energy input
supplies the directionality by preventing backward steps.

Entropic elasticity deserves a note because it explains a property with no mechanical analogue.
A polymer's resistance to being stretched does not come from bond stretching but from
configurational entropy: an extended chain has far fewer possible arrangements than a coiled one,
so extending it reduces entropy and the free energy rises. The resulting force increases with
temperature, which is the opposite of ordinary elastic materials and is directly observable in
rubber, which contracts when heated under load. DNA's mechanical behaviour under tension follows
the same physics and is described by the worm-like chain model, which is how single-molecule
stretching experiments are interpreted.

## Molecular Machines

Molecular motors convert chemical energy into directed motion, and they operate under conditions
where thermal noise is comparable to the forces involved. Their efficiencies are frequently high —
some approach the thermodynamic limit — which is remarkable given that they work by biasing random
fluctuations rather than by applying deterministic force.

Kinesin and dynein walk along microtubules carrying cargo, taking discrete steps of a fixed
length powered by ATP hydrolysis. Myosin moves along actin and is what produces muscle
contraction, with the sliding filament mechanism converting many small myosin steps into
macroscopic shortening. ATP synthase is a rotary motor driven by a proton gradient, and the
rotation drives conformational changes that synthesize ATP — a mechanism proposed before it could
be observed and subsequently visualized directly.

The forces and step sizes involved are the scale at which single-molecule measurement operates.
A motor generates a few piconewtons and steps by a few nanometres, so the work per step is a few
tens of piconewton-nanometres, comparable to the energy released by hydrolysing one ATP molecule.
That correspondence — one chemical event to one mechanical step — is what establishes the coupling
and was demonstrated by directly observing individual steps.

  stall force          where the motor stops against load
  ribosome             translates mRNA; a machine with a decoding and a catalytic

Kinetic proofreading deserves emphasis because it resolves an apparent impossibility. The
discrimination achievable by binding energy alone is limited by the free energy difference between
correct and incorrect substrates, and observed biological error rates are far below that limit.
The resolution is that the process includes an irreversible energy-consuming step between initial
binding and commitment, giving incorrect substrates additional opportunities to dissociate. The
discrimination is applied twice, so the error rate is squared, at the cost of energy expended on
discarding intermediates. The same principle operates in translation, in DNA replication, and in
immune receptor discrimination.

## Membranes, Transport, and Bioelectricity

Membranes are lipid bilayers whose structure follows from amphipathicity, and their physical
properties determine much of cellular function. The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid in which
components diffuse laterally, its thickness is set by the lipid tail length, and its mechanical
properties — bending rigidity and area compressibility — determine what shapes it can adopt and
what deformations cost energy.

Membrane curvature is generated and sensed by several mechanisms, and it is what shapes the
organelles and drives vesicle formation. Lipids with different head and tail cross-sections
prefer curved arrangements. Proteins with wedge shapes insert and impose curvature. Protein
scaffolds bind and hold a curved shape. And cytoskeletal forces pull. The energy required to bend
a membrane is what makes vesicle budding a process requiring machinery rather than occurring
spontaneously.

Transport across membranes divides by whether it requires energy, and the physical framing is
the electrochemical gradient — the combination of the concentration difference and the electrical
potential difference. Passive transport moves substances down that gradient. Active transport
moves them against it and requires energy input, either directly from ATP or by coupling to
another substance's favourable gradient.

Membrane potential arises from the combination of ion concentration gradients and selective
permeability, and understanding it correctly requires recognizing that very few ions are involved.
The membrane is a capacitor, and charging it to a hundred millivolts requires moving a number of
ions that is negligible relative to the cell's total content — which is why the potential can
change rapidly without measurably altering the concentrations, and why the gradients that
establish it persist through many action potentials.

Cable theory deserves a note because it explains why axons are structured as they are. A passive
electrical signal spreading along a process decays exponentially with a length constant determined
by the ratio of membrane resistance to axial resistance. A thin process has high axial resistance
and a short length constant, so a signal dies within a fraction of a millimetre. The two solutions
are regenerating the signal periodically, which is what voltage-gated channels do in an
unmyelinated axon, and raising the length constant by increasing the membrane resistance, which is
what myelin does — permitting the signal to spread passively between nodes and be regenerated only
there.

Your drift of 15 nanometres per minute is comparable to a kinesin step of 8 nanometres, which
means slow drift is indistinguishable from stepping over the timescale of a measurement. Two
remedies: use differential detection with a second trapped bead as a reference, so common-mode
drift cancels, or use a dumbbell geometry with two traps where the measurement is between the two
beads rather than relative to the stage. Active stabilization referenced to a fiducial marker is
the alternative.

Your sampling at 200 hertz cannot resolve individual steps. A kinesin step takes under a

you need at least a few kilohertz to see the step structure rather than an averaged velocity.

standard approach to resolving stepping, and it also lets you separate the mechanical and chemical
parts of the cycle by varying the concentration.

## Cell Mechanics and Techniques

Cells are mechanically active and mechanically responsive, and the recognition that mechanics is
a signalling modality alongside chemistry has reshaped cell biology. The cytoskeleton provides
structure and generates force: actin filaments with myosin motors produce contractility,
microtubules provide compression resistance and transport tracks, and intermediate filaments
provide tensile resilience.

Cells sense mechanical properties of their environment and respond, which is mechanotransduction.
Stem cells differentiate toward different lineages depending on substrate stiffness alone — soft
substrates toward neural, stiff toward bone — with no difference in chemical signals. Cells migrate
up stiffness gradients. Endothelial cells align with flow. The mechanisms include force-sensitive
ion channels, proteins that unfold under tension to expose binding sites, and adhesion complexes
that grow under load.

Techniques for measuring these have developed alongside the understanding. Single-molecule methods
manipulate and measure individual molecules directly. Optical tweezers trap a dielectric bead in a
focused laser and measure forces of piconewtons with nanometre resolution. Magnetic tweezers apply
force and torque to a magnetic bead, which suits twisting DNA. Atomic force microscopy images
surfaces and measures forces on individual molecular interactions.

Single-molecule measurement deserves the closing emphasis for what it revealed rather than for
its precision. Ensemble measurements report averages, and an average conceals whether a population
is homogeneous or is a mixture of distinct behaviours. Single-molecule observation showed that
enzymes have memory — an individual molecule's catalytic rate fluctuates over time on scales far
longer than a single turnover — that molecular motors take discrete steps rather than moving
smoothly, that protein folding proceeds through distinct pathways rather than one, and that
apparently uniform populations frequently contain distinct subpopulations. These are qualitative
findings about mechanism that no amount of precision in an ensemble measurement could have
produced, because the averaging destroyed exactly the information they depend on.
