## Temperature and the Laws of Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics began as an engineering science concerned with converting heat into work
and became one of the deepest frameworks in physics, because its laws constrain every
possible process regardless of the underlying mechanism. That generality is its
distinguishing feature: a thermodynamic argument does not care whether the working
substance is a gas, a magnet, a black hole, or an information-processing system.

The zeroth law establishes that thermal equilibrium is transitive — if A is in
equilibrium with C and B is in equilibrium with C, then A and B are in equilibrium with
each other. This is what makes temperature a well-defined property rather than a
pairwise relation, and it is why a thermometer works at all. It was named the zeroth law
because its necessity was recognized after the first and second had been numbered.

The first law is conservation of energy applied to thermal systems: the change in
internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system. Its content beyond
ordinary energy conservation is the recognition that heat is a form of energy transfer
rather than a substance, which was not obvious and took the better part of a century to
establish. Internal energy is a state function, depending only on the current state,
while heat and work are path functions describing transfers — which is why one writes a
change in U but not a change in Q, and why the same change of state can be accomplished
by different combinations of heat and work.

The second law has many equivalent statements, and their variety reflects how many
different-seeming impossibilities turn out to be the same one. Clausius: heat does not
flow spontaneously from cold to hot. Kelvin-Planck: no cyclic process converts heat
entirely into work with no other effect. Entropy: the entropy of an isolated system never
decreases. The equivalence of these is a genuine theorem, and the second law is the only
fundamental law of physics that distinguishes past from future, which is why the
arrow of time is a thermodynamic rather than a mechanical concept.

The four laws:
  zeroth   thermal equilibrium is transitive; temperature is well defined
  first    dU = Q - W;  energy conservation, U a state function
  second   dS_universe >= 0;  no process decreases total entropy
  third    entropy approaches a constant (zero for a perfect crystal) as T -> 0;
           absolute zero is unattainable in finitely many steps

The third law has a practical consequence beyond its statement: heat capacities must
vanish as temperature approaches zero, since otherwise entropy would diverge. This is
observed, and explaining the low-temperature heat capacity of solids — the Debye T^3 law
— was one of the early successes of quantum theory, because the classical equipartition
prediction of a constant heat capacity fails badly at low temperature.

Q: A gas expands adiabatically and reversibly. Which quantity is unchanged?
A. Temperature   B. Internal energy   C. Entropy   D. Pressure
Reasoning: Adiabatic means no heat exchange, and reversible means no entropy generation,
so the entropy change is zero — the process is isentropic. The gas does work on its
surroundings, so internal energy falls and temperature drops; pressure falls with the
expansion. Answer: C.

## Processes, Cycles, and Efficiency

Thermodynamic processes are classified by what is held constant, and each has a
characteristic relation between the state variables. Isothermal processes hold
temperature fixed, so for an ideal gas the internal energy is unchanged and all heat
added is converted to work. Adiabatic processes exchange no heat, so all work comes at
the expense of internal energy and the temperature changes. Isobaric processes hold
pressure fixed, which is the ordinary condition for chemistry in an open vessel and is
why enthalpy is the natural energy function there. Isochoric processes hold volume fixed,
so no work is done and all heat goes into internal energy.

Reversibility is an idealization worth understanding precisely because it sets the limit
every real process falls short of. A reversible process proceeds through a continuous
sequence of equilibrium states, so it can be reversed by an infinitesimal change in
conditions, and it generates no entropy. Real processes involve finite gradients — finite
temperature differences driving heat flow, finite pressure differences driving expansion,
friction — and each generates entropy. The practical consequence is a universal tradeoff:
approaching reversibility requires proceeding infinitely slowly, so maximum efficiency is
achieved at zero power output, and every real machine trades efficiency for rate.

The Carnot cycle — two isothermal and two adiabatic steps operating between reservoirs at
T_hot and T_cold — is the most efficient possible cycle between two temperatures, and its
efficiency is 1 - T_cold/T_hot. That this depends only on the reservoir temperatures and
not at all on the working substance is a remarkable result and follows directly from the
second law: a more efficient engine could be combined with a reversed Carnot engine to
move heat from cold to hot with no other effect, which is forbidden.

Cycles and their figures of merit:
  Carnot        eta = 1 - T_c/T_h;  the upper bound for any heat engine
  Otto          spark ignition; eta = 1 - r^(1-gamma), r the compression ratio
  Diesel        compression ignition; higher compression ratio, higher efficiency
  Brayton       gas turbine; continuous flow, jet engines and power generation
  Rankine       steam cycle with phase change; most thermal power plants
  Stirling      external combustion, regenerative; theoretically Carnot-efficient
  refrigerator  COP = Q_c / W;  reversed cycle, COP = T_c/(T_h - T_c) at best
  heat pump     COP = Q_h / W = 1 + COP_refrigerator;  always exceeds 1

The heat pump's coefficient of performance exceeding one is not a violation of anything
and is the single most useful practical consequence of thermodynamics. A heat pump does
not create energy; it moves energy from a cold reservoir to a warm one, and the work
required is only the difference. A pump moving heat from 0 C outside to 20 C inside has
an ideal COP around 14, and real units achieve 3 to 5, meaning three to five units of
heat delivered per unit of electricity. Resistive heating has a COP of exactly one, which
is why replacing it with a heat pump is one of the largest available efficiency gains in
building energy use.

Real engine efficiencies fall well below Carnot for identifiable reasons. Heat transfer
across a finite temperature difference is irreversible and unavoidable if the process is
to run at finite rate — the Curzon-Ahlborn analysis gives the efficiency at maximum power
as 1 - sqrt(T_c/T_h), which is much closer to observed values than the Carnot bound.
Friction, turbulence, incomplete combustion, and heat leakage each add losses. Quoting
Carnot efficiency as a target for a real machine is therefore misleading; it is a bound
rather than a goal.

## Entropy and Free Energy

Entropy has two definitions that turn out to be the same thing. The thermodynamic
definition, from Clausius, is that the entropy change equals reversible heat divided by
temperature. The statistical definition, from Boltzmann, is that entropy is k_B times the
logarithm of the number of microstates consistent with the macrostate. The reconciliation
of these — showing that a quantity defined by heat flow measures the multiplicity of
microscopic arrangements — is one of the great achievements of nineteenth-century
physics and is the foundation of statistical mechanics.

The statistical reading dissolves several confusions. Entropy increases not because of a
force driving it up but because there are overwhelmingly more high-entropy microstates
than low-entropy ones, so a system exploring its accessible states will be found in a
high-entropy macrostate with probability approaching certainty. The second law is
therefore statistical rather than absolute — entropy decreases are not forbidden but are
so improbable at macroscopic scale that the distinction is irrelevant. For a mole of gas,
the probability of a spontaneous fluctuation to a noticeably lower-entropy state is
smaller than any number that arises in physics.

"Entropy is disorder" is a serviceable slogan and misleads in specific cases. Entropy
counts accessible microstates, and that need not correspond to visual disorder. Hard
spheres at high density crystallize spontaneously because the ordered packing gives each
sphere more room to rattle, increasing the total number of accessible configurations
despite the apparent order. Similarly, the hydrophobic effect drives protein folding not
because the folded state is disordered but because releasing structured water from around
exposed nonpolar surfaces increases the water's entropy more than the protein's decreases.

Free energy combines energy and entropy into a criterion applicable to a system rather
than the universe. Helmholtz free energy F = U - TS governs processes at constant
temperature and volume; Gibbs free energy G = H - TS governs constant temperature and
pressure, which is the ordinary laboratory and biological condition. A process is
spontaneous when the relevant free energy decreases, and the free energy change also
gives the maximum useful work extractable.

Entropy and free energy relations:
  Clausius            dS = Q_rev / T
  Boltzmann           S = k_B ln(Omega)
  Gibbs               S = -k_B sum p_i ln p_i
  Helmholtz           F = U - TS;  constant T and V
  Gibbs free energy   G = H - TS;  constant T and P
  spontaneity         dG < 0 at constant T, P
  maximum work        -dG is the maximum non-expansion work available
  chemical potential  mu = dG/dn;  the driving force for particle transfer
  equilibrium         dG = 0;  and dG0 = -RT ln K
  entropy of mixing   dS = -nR sum x_i ln x_i;  always positive, drives dissolution

The Gibbs entropy formula, involving a sum over probabilities, is worth noticing because
it is identical in form to Shannon's information entropy. This is not a coincidence or an
analogy: thermodynamic entropy measures the information required to specify the
microstate given the macrostate, and the connection is made quantitative by Landauer's
principle, which states that erasing one bit of information necessarily dissipates at
least k_B T ln 2 of energy as heat. This resolves Maxwell's demon — the demon must
eventually erase its records, and the entropy cost of that erasure exactly compensates
the entropy it appeared to remove — and it establishes a fundamental thermodynamic cost
for computation, though present-day processors dissipate many orders of magnitude more
than the Landauer bound.

## Kinetic Theory and Statistical Ensembles

Kinetic theory derives macroscopic thermodynamics from mechanical assumptions about
molecules: that a gas consists of many small particles in constant random motion, that
collisions are elastic, and that intermolecular forces are negligible except during
collision. From these, pressure emerges as the momentum transfer from molecular impacts
on the walls, and temperature emerges as proportional to the average translational
kinetic energy — three-halves k_B T per molecule, one-half per degree of freedom.

The equipartition theorem generalizes this: each quadratic degree of freedom in the
energy carries an average of one-half k_B T at equilibrium. A monatomic gas has three
translational degrees of freedom and thus a molar heat capacity of three-halves R. A
diatomic gas adds two rotational degrees at ordinary temperature, giving five-halves R,
and would add two vibrational degrees at high temperature. That the vibrational
contribution is absent at room temperature was a classical mystery, resolved by quantum
mechanics: modes whose energy spacing greatly exceeds k_B T are frozen out, unable to be
excited, which is why heat capacities rise in steps as temperature increases.

The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution gives the distribution of molecular speeds, and its
shape matters for chemistry and for atmospheric science. It is asymmetric with a long
high-speed tail, so the mean speed exceeds the most probable speed and the
root-mean-square speed exceeds both. Reaction rates depend on the fraction of molecules
exceeding an activation energy, which lies far out in that tail, and because the tail is
exponential a small temperature rise produces a large fractional increase in that
population — the microscopic origin of the Arrhenius rate law and of the rule of thumb
that reaction rates roughly double per ten degrees.

Kinetic theory results:
  pressure              P = (1/3) n m <v^2>
  temperature           (3/2) k_B T = (1/2) m <v^2> per molecule
  equipartition         (1/2) k_B T per quadratic degree of freedom
  rms speed             v_rms = sqrt(3 k_B T / m)
  mean speed            v_avg = sqrt(8 k_B T / (pi m))
  most probable         v_p = sqrt(2 k_B T / m)
  mean free path        lambda = 1 / (sqrt(2) n sigma)
  effusion (Graham)     rate proportional to 1/sqrt(M)
  heat capacity ratio   gamma = C_p/C_v;  5/3 monatomic, 7/5 diatomic

Statistical mechanics formalizes this with ensembles, each appropriate to different
constraints. The microcanonical ensemble describes an isolated system with fixed energy,
particle number, and volume, and assigns equal probability to every accessible
microstate. The canonical ensemble describes a system in thermal contact with a
reservoir at fixed temperature, and assigns probability proportional to exp(-E/k_B T) —
the Boltzmann factor, the single most important expression in the subject. The grand
canonical ensemble allows particle exchange as well, adding a chemical potential term.

The partition function, the sum of Boltzmann factors over all states, is the central
object: every thermodynamic quantity is obtained from it by differentiation. Helmholtz
free energy is -k_B T times its logarithm; internal energy, entropy, pressure, and heat
capacity all follow. Computing a partition function is therefore equivalent to solving
the thermodynamics of a system completely, which is why so much of statistical mechanics
consists of evaluating or approximating partition functions for particular models.

A natural-convection heat sink of 0.3 C/W does not practically exist in a reasonable
volume — natural convection bottoms out around 0.5 C/W even for large finned extrusions,
because the air movement is driven only by buoyancy and the heat transfer coefficient is
correspondingly low, on the order of 5-10 W/(m^2 K). You would need an impractically
large surface area.

Three ways forward, in order of how much they buy you:

Add forced convection. A modest fan raises the heat transfer coefficient by an order of
magnitude, and 0.3 C/W is routine for a small fan-cooled sink. This is the direct
solution if a fan is acceptable.

Reduce the interface resistance. 0.3 C/W is high for a thermal interface — that suggests
a thick pad. A thin layer of good thermal grease or a phase-change material typically
achieves 0.05-0.1 C/W, which would free up 0.2 C/W and bring the sink requirement to 0.5

devices are rated to 125 C, and relaxing to 105 C would double your budget to 1.44 C/W

If a fan is genuinely impossible, the combination worth pursuing is a low-resistance
interface, a large sink with vertically oriented fins (natural convection is
orientation-sensitive — horizontal fins trap air and perform far worse), unobstructed
airflow above and below, and a heat spreader or heat pipe to move the heat away from the
device before dissipating it over a larger area.

## Phase Transitions and Heat Transfer

A phase transition is a discontinuous change in the state of matter as a parameter is
varied. First-order transitions — melting, boiling, most crystal structure changes —
involve a latent heat, absorbed or released at constant temperature while the phases
coexist. This is why a pot of boiling water stays at 100 C regardless of how much heat is
supplied: the energy goes into the phase change rather than into raising the temperature.
Second-order or continuous transitions have no latent heat and involve a divergence in
some susceptibility — the ferromagnetic Curie point, the superfluid transition, the
liquid-gas critical point.

Phase diagrams map the stable phase as a function of pressure and temperature.
Coexistence curves separate the regions, and the Clausius-Clapeyron relation gives their
slope in terms of the latent heat and volume change. The triple point is where all three
phases coexist, unique to each substance and reproducible enough that the triple point of
water served as a defining fixed point of the temperature scale. The critical point
terminates the liquid-gas coexistence curve, and beyond it the distinction between liquid
and gas disappears entirely — a supercritical fluid can be taken continuously from
liquid-like to gas-like density with no transition, which is exploited in supercritical
CO2 extraction and in supercritical water oxidation.

Water's phase diagram is anomalous in a way with enormous consequences: its solid-liquid
coexistence curve slopes backward, because ice is less dense than liquid water. Applying
pressure to ice therefore melts it, and the underlying cause is the open hydrogen-bonded
tetrahedral structure of ice, which is less densely packed than the liquid. That ice
floats is why bodies of water freeze from the top down, insulating the liquid below and
allowing aquatic life to survive winters — a property so consequential for terrestrial
life that it is regularly cited in discussions of planetary habitability.

Critical phenomena display universality, which is one of the deeper results in physics.
Near a continuous transition, quantities diverge as power laws with exponents that depend
only on the dimensionality of space and the symmetry of the order parameter — not on the
microscopic details. A magnet, a fluid at its critical point, and a binary alloy share
critical exponents despite having nothing in common microscopically. The renormalization
group explains this: as the correlation length diverges, microscopic details become
irrelevant and only symmetry and dimension survive the coarse-graining, an idea that has
since propagated into quantum field theory and into the analysis of complex systems
generally.

Heat transfer occurs by three mechanisms with different scaling:
  conduction     through a material by molecular and electron transport;
                 Q/t = k A dT / L;  metals high k, insulators low
  convection     by bulk fluid motion; Q/t = h A dT;  h depends strongly on
                 whether flow is natural (buoyancy, h ~ 5-25 W/m^2K) or forced
                 (h ~ 25-250 for air, far higher for liquids)
  radiation      by electromagnetic emission; Q/t = eps sigma A (T^4 - T_s^4);
                 dominant at high temperature and the only mechanism in vacuum
  thermal resistance   R = L/(kA) for conduction, 1/(hA) for convection;
                 resistances in series add, which is how thermal budgets work
  time constant  tau = mc/(hA);  how fast a body approaches ambient

The fourth-power dependence in radiation is what makes it negligible at room temperature
and dominant at high temperature. A surface at 300 K radiates about 460 W/m^2; at 600 K
it radiates sixteen times as much. This is why incandescent lamps and furnaces lose heat
predominantly by radiation while an electronics enclosure loses it predominantly by
convection, and why spacecraft thermal control is entirely a radiation problem — with no
surrounding fluid, radiators are the only way to reject heat, which is why they are large
and why their emissivity and view factor to deep space are the governing design
parameters.
