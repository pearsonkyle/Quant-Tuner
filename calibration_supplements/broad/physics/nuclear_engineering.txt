## Nuclear Reactions and Cross Sections

Nuclear energy comes from the difference in binding energy per nucleon across the chart of nuclides.
That curve peaks near iron, which means that combining light nuclei releases energy and splitting
heavy nuclei releases energy, while the reverse of each consumes it. This single curve explains why
fusion powers stars, why fission powers reactors, and why iron is the endpoint of stellar
nucleosynthesis.

The cross section quantifies the probability of a reaction, expressed as an effective area presented
by a target nucleus. It depends strongly on the incident particle's energy, and for neutrons the
dependence has structure that dominates reactor design: cross sections generally rise as neutron
energy falls, with sharp resonances at specific energies where the compound nucleus has an excited
state. Fission cross sections for the common fissile isotopes are much larger for slow neutrons than
for fast ones, by roughly two orders of magnitude.

That energy dependence is why most power reactors are thermal reactors. Fission produces fast
neutrons; slowing them to thermal energies through collisions with a moderator dramatically raises
the probability that they cause further fission, which permits a chain reaction with fuel that is
only slightly enriched. A fast reactor, operating without moderation, needs much higher enrichment
and in exchange can breed fissile material from the abundant fertile isotope, which is the tradeoff
underlying the entire design space.

Q: Delayed neutrons constitute under one percent of fission neutrons. Why do they dominate reactor
control?
A. They have higher energy
B. Reactors are operated so that the prompt neutrons alone are slightly subcritical, making the
   chain reaction depend on the delayed fraction; this stretches the characteristic response time
   from microseconds to seconds, which is what makes mechanical control possible
C. They cause more fissions
D. They are easier to detect
Reasoning: With prompt neutrons alone, the multiplication timescale is the prompt neutron lifetime,
far too short for any control system. Operating in the range where criticality requires the delayed
contribution ties the period to the precursor decay times. Exceeding prompt criticality removes this
margin entirely and is the condition every design and procedure is arranged to prevent. Answer: B.

## Reactor Physics and Control

Criticality is the condition where each generation of neutrons exactly reproduces itself, quantified
by the multiplication factor. Below one the chain reaction dies out; above one it grows; at exactly
one it is steady. A reactor is not held at criticality by a fixed configuration but by continuous
adjustment, because the reactivity changes as fuel depletes, as fission products accumulate, and as
temperature varies.

Reactivity feedback is the mechanism that makes a reactor stable, and it must be negative for a safe
design. When temperature rises, several effects change the reactivity: thermal expansion reduces
density, the moderator becomes less effective, and resonance absorption broadens with temperature so
that more neutrons are captured without fission. A design where the net effect is negative responds
to a power increase by reducing reactivity, which limits the excursion without any control action.
This is inherent safety and it is a design requirement rather than a bonus.

Fission product poisoning is the operational effect that most surprises people new to the field.
Xenon-135 has an enormous absorption cross section and is produced partly by the decay of a
precursor, so after a power reduction its concentration keeps rising for hours before falling. A
reactor shut down or reduced in power may become impossible to restart for many hours until the
xenon decays, and the transient after a return to power runs the other way. Managing this is a
routine part of operation and it constrains how quickly a plant can change output.

## Thermal Hydraulics and Safety

A power reactor is a heat source and the plant around it is a heat engine, so the limits are as much
thermal as nuclear. Heat is generated in the fuel, conducted through the fuel and cladding,
transferred to the coolant, and carried to the steam system. Each step has a limit, and the binding
one is usually the heat flux at which boiling changes character from efficient nucleate boiling to
film boiling, where a vapour layer insulates the surface and the temperature jumps abruptly.

That transition — the critical heat flux — is the governing safety limit in water-cooled reactors,
because exceeding it causes cladding temperature to rise sharply within seconds and can lead to
failure. Operating margins are defined relative to it, and the instrumentation and protection system
exists to keep the plant away from the conditions where it could be approached.

Safety design follows defence in depth: multiple independent barriers between the fuel and the
environment, multiple independent means of achieving each safety function, and a design basis that
assumes failures occur. The fundamental safety functions are controlling reactivity, removing heat,
and containing radioactive material, and every safety system serves one of them. Passive systems
that work by gravity, natural circulation, or stored energy are preferred over active ones because
they do not depend on power or on operator action, and the trend in newer designs is toward
achieving the safety functions passively for an extended period.

## Radiation Interaction, Shielding, and Dosimetry

Radiation interacts with matter differently by type, and shielding design follows directly. Alpha
particles are stopped by paper or the outer skin layer and are hazardous only if the source is
inhaled or ingested. Beta particles penetrate millimetres of tissue and are shielded by low-atomic-
number materials — using lead produces secondary photons through braking radiation, so plastic
first and lead second is the correct order. Gamma rays are attenuated exponentially with no definite
range, requiring dense high-atomic-number material and thickness proportional to the logarithm of
the required reduction.

Neutrons require a different approach entirely because they interact with nuclei rather than
electrons. Shielding them means first slowing them with hydrogen-rich material, then absorbing the
slowed neutrons with a material having a high thermal capture cross section, and finally attenuating
the gamma rays that capture produces. A layered shield of water or polyethylene, boron, and lead
addresses all three, and omitting any layer leaves a hazard the other layers do not cover.

Dose quantities are distinguished carefully because they answer different questions. Absorbed dose
is energy deposited per unit mass. Equivalent dose weights it by radiation type, since alpha
particles cause more damage per unit energy than gammas. Effective dose further weights by the
sensitivity of the tissues irradiated, giving a single number for whole-body risk comparison. Using
the wrong one — comparing an absorbed dose to an effective dose limit, for instance — produces errors
of an order of magnitude.

## Fuel Cycle and Fusion

The nuclear fuel cycle runs from mining through conversion, enrichment, fabrication, irradiation,
storage, and eventual disposal or recycling. Enrichment increases the fraction of the fissile
isotope, and the same technology at higher output produces weapons material, which is why enrichment
capability is the central concern of nonproliferation policy and why the fuel cycle is a political
subject as much as a technical one.

Spent fuel contains unfissioned fuel, fissile material produced during irradiation, and fission
products of widely varying half-life. The short-lived products dominate the heat and radiation for
decades; the long-lived ones dominate the disposal timescale. Reprocessing separates the reusable
material from the waste, reducing the volume requiring long-term disposal and producing separated
fissile material, which is the proliferation concern that makes the choice contested.

Fusion offers a fuel supply that is effectively unlimited and no long-lived fission product waste,
and it has proven far harder than fission. Confining a plasma hot enough for the nuclei to overcome
their electrostatic repulsion, dense enough for collisions to be frequent, and for long enough for
the released energy to exceed the input is the requirement, and the product of those three
quantities must exceed a threshold. Magnetic confinement in a toroidal geometry and inertial
confinement by compressing a fuel pellet are the two main approaches, and both have now demonstrated
energy release exceeding the energy delivered to the fuel while remaining well short of a net-
positive power plant.
