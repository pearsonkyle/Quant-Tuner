## Seismology and the Structure of the Earth

Nearly everything known about the Earth's deep interior comes from seismic waves, because no direct
sample exists from below a few kilometres. An earthquake radiates elastic waves that travel through
the planet, refract and reflect at boundaries where properties change, and arrive at instruments
worldwide with travel times and amplitudes that encode the structure along their paths.

Two body wave types propagate through the volume. Compressional waves travel fastest, involve motion
parallel to propagation, and pass through liquids. Shear waves travel more slowly, involve
perpendicular motion, and cannot propagate in a liquid at all. That last fact is the single most
consequential observation in the history of the field: a shadow zone where shear waves do not arrive
on the far side of the Earth demonstrates a liquid outer core, and the shape of the compressional
wave shadow gives its size.

Surface waves travel along the boundary, decay with depth, and carry most of the destructive energy
in a large earthquake. Their dispersion — the dependence of velocity on period, since longer periods
sample deeper — allows the shallow velocity structure to be inverted from a single record, which is
why surface wave analysis dominates crustal and upper mantle imaging. The layered picture of crust,
mantle, outer core, and inner core, with the discontinuities between them, was built entirely from
these observations before any other technique could address the question.

Q: Shear waves are not detected on the side of the Earth opposite a large earthquake. This shows:
A. The waves were absorbed by rock
B. There is a liquid layer in the interior, because shear waves cannot propagate through a liquid
   and are therefore blocked entirely rather than merely attenuated
C. The instruments failed
D. The earthquake was too small
Reasoning: A liquid has no shear strength, so shear waves cannot exist in it. The complete absence
of shear arrivals across a well-defined shadow zone, rather than a reduction in amplitude, is
diagnostic of a liquid layer and was the discovery of the outer core. Answer: B.

## Gravity, Geodesy, and the Shape of the Earth

The Earth's gravity field varies from place to place because mass is not uniformly distributed, and
measuring those variations maps subsurface density. A gravity survey measures the field with an
instrument sensitive to parts in a billion, and the interpretation requires removing the predictable
components — latitude, elevation, the mass of the intervening topography, and the tidal effect of
the Sun and Moon — before the residual anomaly reflects the geology.

Isostasy is the principle that the crust floats on a denser mantle in approximate buoyant
equilibrium, so mountain ranges have deep low-density roots supporting them. It explains why the
observed gravity over a mountain range is far less than the topography alone would produce: the
excess mass above sea level is compensated by a deficit below. Departures from isostatic
equilibrium indicate that something is actively supporting or depressing the surface, which is how
dynamic support by mantle flow and glacial rebound are identified.

Satellite geodesy transformed the field by making global measurement continuous. Satellite pairs
measuring their separation to micron precision detect mass redistribution, which has produced direct
measurements of ice sheet loss, groundwater depletion, and the seasonal cycling of water between
land and ocean. Precise satellite positioning measures crustal deformation at the millimetre level,
which is how plate motion, strain accumulation on faults, and volcanic inflation are now routinely
monitored.

## Geomagnetism and the Core

The Earth's magnetic field is generated by convection in the liquid iron outer core, a self-
sustaining dynamo in which fluid motion through a magnetic field generates currents that maintain
the field. The energy source is a combination of cooling and the buoyancy released as the inner core
solidifies, and the rotation of the planet organizes the flow into the pattern that produces a
predominantly dipolar field.

The field is not static. It drifts, its intensity varies, and at irregular intervals averaging
hundreds of thousands of years it reverses polarity entirely, with a transition taking a few
thousand years during which the field is weak and complex. The record of these reversals is
preserved in rocks that cooled through their magnetic blocking temperature, locking in the field
direction at that time, and reading that record from the ocean floor provided the decisive evidence
for seafloor spreading.

That evidence deserves emphasis because of how conclusive it was. Magnetic surveys of the ocean floor
revealed stripes of alternating polarity, symmetric about mid-ocean ridges, with the same sequence
on both sides. The only sensible explanation is that new crust forms at the ridge, records the field
direction as it cools, and spreads outward in both directions — which is seafloor spreading, and
which converted continental drift from a disputed hypothesis into the foundation of plate tectonics.

  reversals            irregular, averaging hundreds of thousands of years apart

  blocking temperature  below which magnetization is fixed

  the magnetosphere    the field's interaction with the solar wind; deflects

                       habitability

## Heat Flow, Mantle Dynamics, and Plate Tectonics

The Earth's internal heat drives everything geologically active, and it comes from two sources:
radioactive decay of long-lived isotopes in the mantle and crust, and heat left over from accretion
and core formation. The total output is modest compared with solar input at the surface — a fraction
of a watt per square metre — but it is the entire budget for mantle convection, plate motion,
volcanism, and the dynamo.

Mantle convection is solid-state creep over geological time. The mantle is crystalline rock that
deforms by diffusion and dislocation mechanisms at rates of centimetres per year, which over
hundreds of millions of years is enough for large-scale overturn. Plates are the cold upper thermal
boundary layer of this convection, and the driving forces are dominated by the negative buoyancy of
subducting slabs pulling the plate behind them rather than by drag from below — which is why the
fastest plates are those with the largest subducting margins.

The plate boundary types organize most surface geology. Divergent boundaries create new crust at
ridges. Convergent boundaries destroy it in subduction zones, producing the deepest earthquakes,
explosive volcanism, and mountain building. Transform boundaries slide past each other, producing
shallow earthquakes without volcanism. Recognizing which type is present tells you what hazards to
expect and what rocks to find.

  the age-depth relation  ocean floor subsides predictably as it cools, one of

## Exploration Methods, Hazards, and Inversion

Applied geophysics images the shallow subsurface for resources, engineering, groundwater, and
archaeology, using the same physics at smaller scale. Seismic reflection sends controlled energy
into the ground and records the echoes from layer boundaries; it is the highest-resolution method
and the most expensive. Electrical and electromagnetic methods measure conductivity, which responds
strongly to water content and to certain ores. Ground-penetrating radar images the top few metres in
resistive material and fails entirely in clay.

Hazard assessment turns this understanding into estimates of what may happen and how often.
Earthquake hazard combines the locations and slip rates of faults, the recurrence statistics of
earthquake magnitudes, and models of how ground shaking attenuates with distance and amplifies in
soft sediment. Local site conditions frequently dominate — the same earthquake produces very
different shaking on rock and on soft basin fill, and the amplification factor can exceed a factor
of several.

The unifying methodological problem across all of this is inversion: inferring subsurface properties
from surface measurements. It is inherently non-unique, because different structures can produce
identical observations, and every result therefore depends on the regularization or prior imposed to
select among the possibilities. A geophysical image is a model consistent with the data, not a
photograph, and its resolution varies with depth and position in ways that a rendered image does not
communicate. Reading the resolution analysis alongside the image is what separates competent
interpretation from over-reading.

  magnetics            buried magnetic bodies; fast and cheap reconnaissance

  borehole logging     direct measurement along a well; the ground truth for
