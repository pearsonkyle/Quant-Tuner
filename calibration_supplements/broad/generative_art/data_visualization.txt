## Perceptual Foundations

Data visualization works by mapping data to visual properties that the perceptual system reads
accurately, and the accuracy of that reading varies enormously by property. This is an empirical
matter rather than a stylistic one: controlled experiments established a ranking of how
precisely people judge magnitude from different visual encodings, and the ranking should drive
encoding choices for anything where precise comparison matters.

Position along a common scale is judged most accurately, which is why scatter plots and bar
charts with a shared axis support precise comparison. Length is next, then slope and angle, then
area, then volume, then colour saturation and hue. The gap is large: people judge position to
within a few percent and area with errors that routinely exceed thirty percent, systematically
underestimating large areas relative to small ones. This is the perceptual basis for the standard
criticism of pie charts, which encode by angle and area, and of bubble charts, which encode by
area.

Pre-attentive processing is the other perceptual fact worth designing around. Certain visual
properties are processed in parallel across the visual field before conscious attention, so a
single element differing in colour, size, orientation, or motion is found in constant time
regardless of how many other elements are present. A target differing in two properties
simultaneously loses this advantage and requires serial search. The design implication is that
one strong contrast draws the eye immediately and several competing contrasts draw it nowhere.

Perceptual foundations:
  position on a common scale  most accurate; the default for precise comparison
  position on unaligned scales  less accurate; small multiples pay this cost
  length               accurate; bar charts
  slope and angle      moderate; line charts read trend well and value less well
  area                 poor; errors of thirty percent or more, and large areas
                       are underestimated
  volume               worse; three-dimensional encoding of quantity is almost
                       never justified
  colour saturation    poor for magnitude; adequate for ordering a few levels
  colour hue           categorical only; no natural order
  pre-attentive        a single differing property is found in constant time
  competing contrasts  several simultaneous emphases cancel
  Gestalt grouping     proximity, similarity, enclosure, connection; operates
                       before conscious inspection and overrides labels
  change blindness     large changes across an interruption go unnoticed;
                       animated transitions must be slow enough to track

Gestalt grouping deserves emphasis because it determines what a reader perceives as related
regardless of what the design intends. Elements placed close together are read as a group; a
shared background or enclosure groups more strongly than proximity; a connecting line groups
more strongly still. A legend placed nearer to the wrong series will be associated with it, and
a spacing that separates a label from its bar will detach them perceptually whatever the
alignment implies. Spacing communicates relationship more forcefully than any label does.

Q: A dashboard uses a 3D pie chart to compare five categories. The most fundamental problem is:
A. It is out of fashion
B. It encodes magnitude by angle and area, which people judge poorly, and the perspective
   distorts the areas further so identical values appear different
C. Five categories is too many
D. It uses too much colour
Reasoning: Angle and area are low on the accuracy ranking, so precise comparison is already
difficult. Adding perspective makes the near slices appear larger than equal far ones, which
introduces a systematic distortion unrelated to the data. A bar chart encoding the same values
by position on a common scale supports accurate comparison. Answer: B.

## Encodings and Chart Selection

Chart selection follows from the question being asked rather than from the data's shape, and
stating the question explicitly is the most useful discipline in the field. Comparing values
across categories, showing a distribution, showing change over time, showing the relationship
between two variables, showing composition, and showing geographic pattern are different
questions with different appropriate forms, and a chart chosen without deciding which is being
asked usually answers none of them well.

Bar charts compare values by length along a common baseline, and the baseline must start at
zero because length is the encoding — truncating the axis makes a small difference look large
and is the most common form of misleading chart. Line charts show change over an ordered
variable, and their axis need not start at zero because slope rather than length is the
encoding, so a truncated axis showing meaningful variation is legitimate. Confusing these two
rules produces both unnecessary and misleading axis choices.

Scatter plots show the relationship between two continuous variables and are the most
information-dense standard form, supporting judgments about correlation, clusters, outliers, and
functional form simultaneously. Their limitation is overplotting: at high density, points
overlap and the visual density no longer represents the data density, so the apparent pattern
reflects the plotting order. Transparency, small point size, binning into a heatmap, or
contouring the density each address it.

Chart forms and their questions:
  bar chart            compare across categories; zero baseline required
  grouped bar          compare across two categorical dimensions
  stacked bar          composition within a total; only the bottom series has a
                       common baseline, so the others are hard to compare
  line chart           change over an ordered variable; zero baseline optional
  slope chart          change between two points across many categories
  scatter plot         relationship between two continuous variables
  bubble chart         adds a third variable by area; the area is read poorly
  histogram            distribution of one variable; bin width materially changes
                       the impression and should be examined at several settings
  box plot             distribution summary; hides multimodality entirely
  violin / strip       show the distribution's shape; preferable when n permits
  heatmap              a matrix of values by colour; ordering the rows and columns
                       is what makes structure visible
  small multiples      the same chart repeated across a facet; excellent for
                       comparison, and each panel needs a shared scale
  dual axis            two series on different scales; the relationship implied
                       depends entirely on arbitrary scaling and should almost
                       always be replaced by two aligned panels

The box plot's limitation deserves emphasis because it is widely used and hides exactly the
feature that would change an interpretation. It shows median, quartiles, and outliers, all of
which are identical for a unimodal and a strongly bimodal distribution with the same summary
statistics. A dataset where two subpopulations differ would appear as a single unremarkable box.
Overlaying the individual points, or using a violin plot showing the estimated density, makes the
structure visible at essentially no cost when the sample size permits.

Stacked bars carry a related and less recognized problem. Only the bottom series sits on a
common baseline, so only it can be compared accurately across categories; every series above it
starts at a varying position, and comparing them requires judging length without alignment,
which is substantially less accurate. If the comparison across categories matters more than the
totals, grouped bars or small multiples serve better.

## Scales, Axes, and Colour

Scale choice determines what is visible and what is compressed. A linear scale represents equal
differences equally and is the default. A logarithmic scale represents equal ratios equally,
which is correct when the data spans orders of magnitude or when proportional change is the
meaningful quantity — and it must be labelled clearly, because a reader who assumes linearity
will misread it badly. Square root scales are used for area encodings so that the perceived area
is proportional to the value.

Axis conventions carry meaning that violating them subverts. Time on the horizontal axis
increasing to the right is nearly universal and reversing it will be misread regardless of
labels. Zero on a bar chart's value axis is required because the bar's length encodes the value.
Consistent scales across small multiples are required for the comparison the form exists to
support, and independent scales silently defeat it.

Colour is the most misused encoding channel and its correct use depends on what the data is.
Categorical data with no order takes a qualitative palette of distinct hues, limited to about
eight before discrimination fails. Ordered data takes a sequential palette varying primarily in
lightness, because lightness is the channel that reads as ordered. Data with a meaningful
midpoint takes a diverging palette with two hues meeting at a neutral centre, and the midpoint
must correspond to the meaningful value rather than to the data's median.

Scales, axes, and colour:
  linear               equal differences; the default
  logarithmic          equal ratios; for orders of magnitude, and label it clearly
  square root          for area encodings, so perception is proportional
  zero baseline        required for bars, optional for lines
  consistent scales    across small multiples; independent scales defeat the
                       comparison
  qualitative palette  distinct hues for unordered categories; about eight is the
                       practical limit
  sequential palette   varying lightness; for ordered data
  diverging palette    two hues meeting at neutral; place the midpoint at the
                       meaningful value
  perceptual uniformity  equal steps should look equally different; the rainbow
                       palette fails this badly, creating false boundaries at
                       yellow and cyan and compressing differences elsewhere
  colourblind safe     avoid red-green as the sole distinction; about 8% of men
                       are affected
  redundant encoding   pair colour with shape, position, or label; colour alone
                       fails for colourblind readers and in greyscale
  greyscale check      a visualization should survive being printed in black and
                       white with its structure intact

The rainbow palette deserves specific criticism because it remains common in scientific
figures and is actively misleading. It is not perceptually uniform: the transitions through
yellow and cyan appear abrupt while the greens change slowly, so the palette creates apparent
boundaries where the data is smooth and hides variation where the data changes. It also has no
consistent lightness ordering, so it fails entirely in greyscale and is difficult for
colourblind readers. Perceptually uniform sequential palettes with monotonic lightness solve all
three problems and are available in every plotting library.

A dual-axis chart lets you choose two independent scale ranges, and the apparent relationship
between the two lines depends entirely on that choice. Your second axis spans 2.1 to 2.4, a
range you selected, and a different range would make the lines diverge, converge, or cross at
different points. Any of those choices is equally defensible and they imply different
conclusions, which means the visual relationship carries no information about the data. Your
actual correlation is 0.31, which is weak, and the chart implies something much stronger.

Three alternatives, depending on what you want to show.

Two aligned panels stacked vertically with a shared time axis. Each series keeps its own scale,
the reader can see both trends, and no spurious alignment is implied because the lines are not
superimposed. This is the general-purpose replacement and it is almost always better.

Index both series to a common base — set each to 100 at the starting period and plot percentage
change. This is legitimate on a single axis because both series are now in the same units, and
it directly shows relative movement, which is usually the intended comparison.

A scatter plot of one against the other, with time encoded by point colour or by connecting the
points in order. If the question is whether the two are related, this answers it directly and
shows the strength honestly — a cloud with a weak trend looks like a cloud with a weak trend.

If the dual axis must stay for some reason, at minimum state the correlation in the chart and
be explicit that the axis ranges were chosen. But the standard advice is well founded: dual
axes make a visual claim that is a function of the designer's arbitrary choices rather than of
the data, and there is nearly always a better form.

## Multivariate, Networks, and Interaction

Showing more than two or three variables requires either additional encoding channels or
additional structure, and each has costs. Adding colour, size, and shape to a scatter plot
extends it to five variables and rapidly exceeds what a reader can decode. Small multiples
faceting by a categorical variable preserve the accuracy of the base encoding at the cost of
space and of comparison across unaligned panels. Parallel coordinates place each variable on a
vertical axis and draw a line per observation, which handles many dimensions and requires
practice to read and is highly sensitive to axis ordering.

Dimensionality reduction projects high-dimensional data to two dimensions for viewing, and the
results are routinely over-interpreted. In nonlinear embeddings, cluster sizes are not
meaningful, distances between clusters are largely not meaningful, and the apparent structure
depends strongly on hyperparameters. They are valuable for exploration and for generating
hypotheses, and they do not establish that clusters are separable or that two groups are more
similar than two others.

Network visualization shows relationships, and the layout algorithm determines what is visible.
Force-directed layouts place connected nodes near each other and reveal community structure at
the cost of being non-deterministic and slow at scale. Matrix representations avoid edge
crossings entirely and read densities well while making path-following impossible. Beyond a few
hundred nodes, node-link diagrams become uninterpretable regardless of layout — the hairball —
and the answer is aggregation, filtering, or a different representation rather than a better
layout.

Multivariate, networks, and interaction:
  small multiples      facet by a category; preserves encoding accuracy
  parallel coordinates  many dimensions; axis order matters greatly
  scatterplot matrix   all pairwise relationships; scales poorly beyond ~8
                       variables
  dimensionality reduction  exploration only; cluster size and inter-cluster
                       distance are not meaningful
  force-directed       reveals communities; non-deterministic, slow at scale
  matrix view          no edge crossings, good for density, poor for paths
  hairball             a node-link diagram beyond a few hundred nodes; aggregate
                       or filter instead
  overview then detail  show the whole, then zoom to specifics
  brushing and linking  select in one view, highlight in all; the highest-value
                       interaction for multivariate exploration
  filtering            dynamic queries; immediate feedback is what makes it
                       exploratory
  tooltips             detail on demand; do not put essential information there,
                       since it is invisible in a static export and on touch
                       devices
  animation            effective for showing transitions between states; poor for
                       comparing states, since it requires memory rather than
                       side-by-side comparison

The animation caveat is worth stating because animated charts are visually compelling and
frequently the wrong choice. Comparing two states requires holding one in memory while viewing
the other, which is far less accurate than comparing them side by side. Animation is genuinely
valuable for showing a transition — making clear that this bar became that bar, which preserves
object identity across a change — and it is a poor substitute for small multiples when the
question is how the states differ.

## Narrative and Honesty

A visualization intended to communicate rather than to explore requires different design.
Exploratory visualization serves an analyst who knows the data and is looking for structure, so
density and flexibility matter more than immediate legibility. Explanatory visualization serves a
reader encountering the data once, so it must have a clear point, remove everything not serving
that point, and direct attention to what matters.

Annotation is the most underused tool in explanatory visualization. A chart with the key
finding stated in text on the chart, with the relevant point marked, communicates in seconds what
an unannotated chart requires the reader to find. The title should state the finding rather than
describe the axes — a title reading that costs rose 40% after the policy change conveys more than
one reading costs over time, and it is honest as long as the chart supports it.

Honesty in visualization is a design responsibility, and the common distortions have known
forms. Truncating a bar chart's axis exaggerates differences. Inconsistent axis intervals distort
trends. Cherry-picking the time range shows the trend that suits. Encoding one-dimensional
quantities by area or volume misleads because perception is nonlinear. Omitting uncertainty
presents estimates as facts. Aggregating across a variable can reverse the apparent
relationship — Simpson's paradox — which is a genuine analytical hazard rather than a
presentational one.

Narrative and honesty:
  exploratory vs explanatory  different audiences, different designs
  state the finding    a title that says what the chart shows
  annotate             mark the relevant point; the highest-value addition
  remove non-data ink   gridlines, borders, and decoration that carry no
                       information compete for attention
  direct labelling     label series on the chart rather than in a legend; removes
                       the lookup
  sort meaningfully    by value rather than alphabetically, unless the reader
                       needs to look up a specific category
  show uncertainty     intervals or distributions; omitting them presents
                       estimates as facts
  aggregation          check whether the pattern holds within subgroups; Simpson's
                       paradox reverses aggregate relationships
  axis truncation      exaggerates; legitimate for lines, misleading for bars
  time range selection  choosing the window that shows the desired trend
  area encoding        perception is nonlinear; scale by the square root or use
                       length instead
  the honest test      would someone with the underlying data draw the same
                       conclusion from this chart

Uncertainty deserves the final emphasis because omitting it is the most consequential and most
common failure. A point estimate drawn as a line or a bar reads as a fact, and the same estimate
with an interval reads as an estimate — which is what it is. This matters most where the
intervals overlap and the visual difference is striking, since a reader without the intervals
concludes there is a difference and a reader with them concludes correctly that the data does
not establish one. Presenting uncertainty is harder to design and it is the difference between
communicating what the data shows and communicating more confidence than it supports.
