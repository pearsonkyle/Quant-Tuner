## Colour Perception and Spaces

Colour is a perceptual construction rather than a property of light. The retina contains
three cone types with broad overlapping sensitivities, and colour is the brain's
interpretation of their relative responses — which is why entirely different spectra can look
identical, a phenomenon called metamerism. Two paint samples matching under daylight can
differ visibly under incandescent light, because their spectra differ while their cone
responses happened to coincide under one illuminant. This is a practical problem in printing,
manufacturing, and any situation where colours are matched under one condition and viewed
under another.

Colour constancy is the perceptual system's compensation for illumination. A white sheet
looks white in warm indoor light and in blue shade despite reflecting very different spectra,
because the visual system estimates the illuminant and discounts it. The compensation is
imperfect and context-dependent, which is why identical pixel values can appear as different
colours depending on surrounding content, and why simultaneous contrast effects are so
robust: a grey patch appears lighter against dark surroundings and darker against light ones.

Colour spaces organize colour numerically, and choosing the wrong one for a task is the most
common source of poor colour work. RGB is device-oriented and additive, matching how displays
emit light. CMYK is subtractive and matches how ink absorbs it, which is why the two gamuts
differ and why saturated RGB colours cannot be printed. HSL and HSV are convenient
reparameterizations of RGB and are not perceptually uniform: their lightness axis does not
correspond to perceived brightness, so a fully saturated yellow and a fully saturated blue at
the same nominal lightness differ enormously in how bright they look.

Colour spaces and their proper use:
  RGB             additive, device-dependent; capture and display
  sRGB            the standard consumer space; gamma-encoded, limited gamut
  Display P3      wider gamut, now common on modern screens
  CMYK            subtractive; print, with a smaller and differently shaped gamut
  HSL / HSV       intuitive parameterization of RGB; not perceptually uniform
  CIELAB          designed for perceptual uniformity; the classical choice for
                  colour difference
  OKLab / OKLCH   modern perceptually uniform space; better hue linearity than
                  Lab, and interpolation in it produces predictable gradients
  linear RGB      proportional to light; required for any arithmetic on colour
  gamut           the representable range; conversion requires mapping
                  out-of-gamut colours, which is a lossy decision
  white point     the reference for what counts as neutral; D65 is standard
  delta E         perceptual colour difference; below about 1 is imperceptible

The practical recommendation that follows is to work in a perceptually uniform space for
anything involving interpolation, comparison, or systematic variation. Generating a gradient
in sRGB passes through dark muddy midpoints because the encoding is nonlinear; generating it
in linear RGB avoids that and still produces hue shifts; generating it in OKLCH produces even
lightness steps with stable hue. Similarly, building a palette by varying lightness in HSL
gives swatches that do not look equally spaced, while doing it in OKLCH does.

Q: Interpolating between blue and yellow in sRGB produces a desaturated grey midpoint. The
best fix is:
A. Add more intermediate stops
B. Interpolate in a perceptually uniform space such as OKLCH, which preserves chroma through
   the transition
C. Increase saturation on both endpoints
D. Use CMYK
Reasoning: The straight line between two hues in a device RGB cube passes near the neutral
axis, so chroma collapses at the midpoint. Interpolating in a space with separate lightness,
chroma, and hue axes takes a path around the hue circle at maintained chroma, which is what
the eye expects. Adding stops in the same space merely samples the same bad path more
finely. Answer: B.

## Palette Construction

A palette is a constrained set of colours used consistently, and constraint is what makes a
design read as designed. The most common failure in generative and amateur work is sampling
colours freely, which produces combinations that are individually plausible and collectively
incoherent.

Harmony schemes provide starting structures based on relationships around the hue circle.
Monochromatic uses one hue with varied lightness and chroma, which is always safe and can be
dull. Analogous uses neighbouring hues, giving a coherent and often naturalistic result.
Complementary uses opposite hues for maximum contrast, and works best with one dominant and
one accent rather than equal weight. Split-complementary softens that contrast. Triadic uses
three evenly spaced hues, which is vibrant and difficult to balance.

These schemes are starting points rather than rules, and the more durable principle is
proportion. A palette in which every colour occupies equal area reads as chaotic regardless of
harmony; one with a clear dominant, a secondary, and a small accent reads as deliberate. The
often-cited sixty-thirty-ten proportion is a heuristic expressing this, and the specific
numbers matter less than the presence of a clear hierarchy.

Palette construction:
  monochromatic       one hue, varied lightness and chroma; safe
  analogous           neighbouring hues; coherent, naturalistic
  complementary       opposite hues; maximum contrast, use unequally
  split-complementary softer than complementary
  triadic             three evenly spaced; vibrant, hard to balance
  proportion          a dominant, a secondary, a small accent
  neutrals            most palettes need them; slightly tinted neutrals read as
                      more considered than pure grey
  warm and cool       warm advances, cool recedes; the basis of depth in painting
  chroma control      high chroma everywhere is exhausting; reserve it for focus
  lightness ramp      build a systematic ramp per hue, spaced perceptually
  semantic mapping    in interfaces, colour must carry consistent meaning
  culture             colour associations are not universal; verify assumptions for
                      the intended audience

The systematic lightness ramp is the technique that distinguishes a professional colour
system from an ad hoc collection. Rather than choosing individual colours, define a set of
lightness steps and generate each hue at each step, holding perceived lightness constant
across hues. This yields a grid in which any two colours at the same step have the same
contrast against a background, so substitutions are safe, dark mode is a systematic
transformation rather than a redesign, and accessibility contrast checks pass or fail
predictably by step rather than per pair.

## Composition and Visual Hierarchy

Composition arranges elements to guide attention and communicate structure. The eye does not
scan uniformly; it is drawn to contrast, to faces, to isolated elements, and to the
intersections of implied lines, and composition works by placing what matters where attention
naturally goes.

Visual hierarchy is the ordering of elements by importance, expressed through the tools that
create emphasis. Size is the most direct. Contrast against the surroundings — in lightness,
in chroma, in hue — is the most powerful, since the eye is fundamentally a contrast detector.
Position matters, with the top-left of a left-to-right reading order receiving attention
first. Isolation makes an element prominent regardless of its other properties, because
whitespace around it removes competition. Repetition creates grouping, and breaking a
repetition creates emphasis.

The Gestalt principles describe how the visual system groups elements before conscious
inspection, and they operate whether or not a designer accounts for them. Proximity groups
nearby elements, which means spacing communicates relationship more strongly than any label —
a form field closer to the wrong label will be misread regardless of what the label says.
Similarity groups elements sharing appearance. Closure completes implied shapes. Continuity
follows implied lines. Common region groups elements sharing an enclosure or background.

Composition tools:
  size                the most direct emphasis
  contrast            the most powerful; the eye detects difference
  position            reading order determines default scan path
  isolation           whitespace creates prominence
  repetition          creates grouping and rhythm
  breaking repetition creates emphasis
  alignment           creates invisible structure; misalignment reads as error
  proximity           spacing communicates relationship more strongly than labels
  similarity          shared appearance implies shared kind
  figure-ground       what is object and what is background; ambiguity is
                      uncomfortable unless intended
  rule of thirds      a heuristic for placing interest off-centre
  visual weight       dark, saturated, detailed, and large elements weigh more
  negative space      an active compositional element, not leftover area

Whitespace deserves its own note because it is consistently undervalued by non-designers,
who read it as waste. It is what creates grouping, what establishes hierarchy through
isolation, and what gives the eye rest. Reducing whitespace to fit more content reliably
reduces how much of that content is actually read, because the structure that made it
scannable is gone. The instruction to fill empty space is almost always a request to make a
design worse.

The diagnostic numbers: 19 colours, 11 type sizes, 34 elements, and 12% whitespace, with no
designated primary metric. When every element is emphasized, none is.

Three changes, in this order, because each one makes the next easier to judge.

Establish a hierarchy. Decide what the single most important number on this dashboard is —
the one a person glancing for two seconds should leave with — and make it substantially larger
than everything else. Then a second tier of three to five supporting metrics, then everything
else. This is the change that will produce the biggest perceived improvement, and it requires
a decision about priority rather than a design skill.

Reduce the palette to about five colours plus neutrals. Use colour to carry meaning
consistently — one colour for positive, one for negative, one accent for the focal element,
and neutral grey for everything that is context rather than signal. Most of your 19 colours
are probably decorating chart series that could be neutral, since a chart with one highlighted
series and the rest in grey communicates far more than a chart where every series is a
different bright colour.

Add whitespace by grouping. At 12% whitespace the elements have no perceptual grouping, so
the viewer sees 34 things rather than 5 groups. Increase spacing between groups substantially
more than within them — the ratio matters more than the absolute amount — and the structure
will become visible without removing any content.

Two smaller things: reduce to three or four type sizes with clear roles, and align everything
to a grid. Misalignment does not register consciously as misalignment; it registers as the
design being sloppy, which reduces trust in the data.

Eight chart types is also worth revisiting. Each type a viewer must interpret costs attention.
Using bar and line for nearly everything, with a deliberate exception where a different form
genuinely communicates better, will read as more coherent and be faster to scan.

## Typography

Typography carries a large share of a design's character and readability, and most of the
craft is in a small number of decisions.

Typeface classification provides the vocabulary. Serifs have small terminal strokes and carry
associations of tradition and formality; their utility for long-form reading on screen is a
matter of the specific face and rendering rather than a general rule. Sans-serifs are cleaner
at small sizes and on low-resolution displays. Slab serifs have heavy rectangular serifs and
read as sturdy. Monospaced faces give every character the same width, which is essential for
code where alignment carries meaning, and they are poor for prose because the spacing is
uneven.

Readability is governed by measurable properties. Line length in the range of 45 to 75
characters is the well-established comfortable range, because much longer lines make the
return sweep to the next line error-prone and much shorter ones break reading rhythm. Line
height should increase with line length, since a longer line needs more vertical separation
to keep the return sweep accurate. Contrast must be sufficient, and light grey text on white
is the most common readability failure in contemporary interfaces, chosen for aesthetic
lightness at the cost of legibility for anyone with less than excellent vision.

Typography practice:
  measure             45-75 characters per line for body text
  line height         1.4-1.6 for body; more for longer lines, less for headings
  type scale          a systematic ratio between sizes; 1.2 to 1.33 are common
  hierarchy           three or four sizes with clear roles, not eleven
  weight              a more efficient hierarchy tool than size for small
                      differences
  tracking            tighten for large display sizes, loosen for small caps
  kerning             pair-specific spacing; matters at display sizes
  alignment           left-aligned ragged right for most text; justified text needs
                      hyphenation to avoid rivers of whitespace
  widows and orphans  single lines separated from their paragraph; avoid
  font pairing        contrast the pair clearly, or use one family's full range;
                      two similar faces read as a mistake
  loading             web fonts cause layout shift; match fallback metrics or use
                      font-display settings
  contrast            4.5:1 minimum for body text; light grey on white is the most
                      common accessibility failure

## Accessibility and Systematic Practice

Accessible design is not a separate mode but a set of constraints that improve the design
generally. Sufficient contrast helps everyone in bright sunlight. Clear hierarchy helps
everyone scanning. Not relying on colour alone helps colourblind users and also helps in
greyscale printing and on poor displays.

Colour vision deficiency affects roughly eight percent of men and half a percent of women in
populations of European descent, with red-green confusion being by far the most common. A
design distinguishing states by red and green alone is unreadable for those users, and the
remedy is redundant encoding: pair colour with shape, position, label, or pattern so that
colour is confirming rather than carrying the information. This is a general principle worth
applying regardless — colour is a weak channel for precise discrimination even for viewers
with typical vision.

Contrast ratios are specified numerically and are worth checking rather than judging by eye,
since perceived contrast is affected by size, weight, and surroundings. The standard
thresholds are 4.5 to 1 for body text, 3 to 1 for large text and for interface components and
graphical objects. Meeting them is a floor rather than a target, and text at exactly the
minimum is legible rather than comfortable.

Accessibility and systems:
  contrast ratios     4.5:1 body text, 3:1 large text and UI components
  never colour alone  pair with shape, label, position, or pattern
  colour blindness    ~8% of men; red-green most common; simulate to check
  focus indicators    visible and never removed without replacement
  motion              respect reduced-motion preferences; vestibular disorders
                      make parallax and large transitions genuinely harmful
  text size           must scale with user settings; avoid fixed pixel sizing
  touch targets       at least 44 by 44 points
  design tokens       named values for colour, spacing, and type; the mechanism by
                      which a system stays consistent
  spacing scale       a consistent set of values, often a geometric progression;
                      arbitrary spacing is what makes layouts feel unresolved
  component library   consistent implementations of recurring patterns
  documentation       when to use each component and why; a library without it
                      gets used inconsistently

The systematic approach is what separates a design that scales from one that does not.
Defining tokens — a spacing scale, a type scale, a colour ramp, a set of radii and shadows —
and building from them means that consistency is structural rather than maintained by
vigilance. It also makes global changes tractable: a shift in brand colour or a dark mode
becomes a token remapping rather than a review of every screen. The constraint of choosing
from a defined scale rather than from a continuum feels limiting initially and reliably
produces more coherent work, for the same reason that a constrained palette does — the
constraint is where the coherence comes from.
