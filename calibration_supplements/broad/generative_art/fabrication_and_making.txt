## Additive Processes

Material extrusion is the most accessible additive process: a filament is melted and deposited layer
by layer along a path computed from a sliced model. Its characteristics follow from that mechanism.
Layer lines are visible and the surface finish is anisotropic. Strength is substantially lower across
layers than within them, because the bond between layers is a partial weld rather than continuous
material. Overhangs beyond a certain angle require support structures, which leave marks where they
attach.

The orientation decision is the most consequential choice in preparing a part and it is frequently
made carelessly. Orientation determines where the weak interlayer direction lies relative to the
load, how much support is needed, how the surfaces that matter are finished, and how long the print
takes. A part that fails in use has usually been oriented so that its load path crosses layers, and
reorienting it costs nothing and can multiply its strength.

Resin processes cure a photopolymer layer by layer with light, producing much finer feature
resolution and smoother surfaces with essentially isotropic properties. The costs are handling —
uncured resin is messy and requires care — a post-processing sequence of washing and additional
curing, and mechanical properties that are generally more brittle and less stable over time,
particularly under ultraviolet exposure. Powder-based processes fuse a bed of powder and need no
support structures because the surrounding powder supports the part, which permits geometries the
other processes cannot make.

Q: A printed bracket snaps cleanly along a horizontal plane under load. The most effective fix is:
A. Increase the infill density
B. Reorient the part so the load runs within layers rather than across them, since interlayer bonds
   are the weak direction and orientation costs nothing to change
C. Use a stronger material
D. Print more slowly
Reasoning: A clean break along a layer plane is the signature of interlayer failure. Infill density
adds material in the wrong direction to help, and a stronger filament still has weak interlayer
bonds. Orientation directly addresses the mechanism and is free. Answer: B.

## Subtractive and Cutting Processes

Computer-controlled milling removes material with a rotating cutter, and its constraints are
geometric and physical. The tool has a diameter, so internal corners have that radius and cannot be
sharp. The tool must reach the feature, so undercuts and deep narrow pockets require special tooling
or additional setups. Material is removed at a rate limited by the tool, the material, and the
machine's rigidity, and exceeding it produces poor finish, tool wear, or breakage.

Feeds and speeds are the parameters governing this, and their relationship is worth understanding
rather than looking up. Spindle speed sets the surface velocity at the cutting edge, which
determines heat generation and is bounded by the tool and material combination. Feed rate sets the
thickness of material each tooth removes, and the counterintuitive point is that too slow a feed is
harmful — the tool rubs rather than cuts, generating heat instead of chips and dulling rapidly.
Chips carry heat away, so producing proper chips is the goal rather than a side effect.

Laser cutting vaporizes or burns through material along a path and excels at flat parts from sheet
stock. Its geometry constraints are minimal in two dimensions and absolute in the third: it makes
flat parts, and three-dimensional objects are built by assembling them. The kerf — the width the beam
removes — must be accounted for in fitted joints, and the cut edge is tapered slightly and darkened on
many materials. Material compatibility is a genuine safety matter rather than a quality one, since
some plastics release corrosive or toxic gases when cut.

## CAD, CAM, and the Digital Chain

Parametric modelling defines geometry by dimensions and relationships rather than by fixed
coordinates, so changing a dimension updates everything derived from it. The practical value is that
a design is modifiable rather than remade, and the practical difficulty is that a model built
carelessly breaks when modified — features referencing faces that move, sketches constrained
ambiguously, and dependency chains that were never intended.

Design intent is the discipline that prevents this. Constraining a sketch fully, referencing stable
geometry rather than incidental faces, and naming and organizing features so their purpose is
evident are what make a model survive revision. A model that regenerates correctly after a dimension
change is worth several that require rebuilding, and the difference is entirely in how it was
constructed rather than in what it looks like.

The path from model to machine passes through a toolpath generator that computes the actual motion.
Its inputs are the geometry, the tool, the material, and the strategy — roughing to remove bulk,
finishing to produce the surface, and specific operations for holes and pockets. Simulation before
cutting catches collisions and errors that are expensive to discover on the machine, and the
practical rule is that the first run of any new program is watched with a hand on the stop.

CAD and CAM:

  the value            modifiable rather than remade
  the fragility        careless models break on modification
  design intent        constraining and referencing so the model survives
                       revision
  full constraint      a sketch with no undefined degrees of freedom
  stable references    avoid referencing faces that move

  assemblies           parts positioned by relationships; interference checked

  the profile approach  a tuned profile per material rather than adjusting

                       settings are strong and per-print fiddling loses the
                       knowledge

## Materials and Finishing

Material selection for making balances mechanical requirements against processability, cost, and
availability. Common printing plastics span a range: an easy-to-print material that is stiff and
brittle and softens at low temperature; a tougher one that is harder to print and more temperature
resistant; a flexible one requiring a specific extruder arrangement; and engineering materials
requiring enclosed chambers and dried filament. Choosing the easiest material that meets the
requirement is usually correct, since a failed print in a demanding material is worse than a
successful one in an adequate material.

Wood, acrylic, and metal sheet each behave differently under the same process. Plywood cuts and
machines readily and varies between sheets. Acrylic cuts beautifully with a laser and cracks under
stress concentrations, so drilled holes need generous clearance and no pinching. Aluminium machines
well and gums up tooling if the chip clearance is inadequate. Knowing a handful of materials
thoroughly is more useful than knowing many superficially.

Finishing determines how a piece is perceived far more than its dimensional accuracy does. Sanding
progressively through grits, filling and priming, and applying a finish transform a printed or
machined surface, and the labour involved usually exceeds the fabrication time by a wide margin.
Planning which surfaces are visible and orienting the fabrication so those surfaces need the least
work is the efficient approach, and it is a decision made before making rather than after.

Materials and finishing:
  the selection balance  requirements against processability and cost
  the easy-material principle  the least demanding material that meets the need
  the failed print cost  worse than an adequate material succeeding
  plywood              readily cut and machined; varies between sheets
  acrylic              cuts beautifully, cracks at stress concentrations
  the acrylic detail   generous clearance at holes and no pinching
  aluminium            machines well with adequate chip clearance
  the depth principle  a few materials known thoroughly beats many known
                       superficially
  sanding              progressive grits; skipping grits wastes time overall
  filling and priming  what makes layer lines disappear
  the labour ratio     finishing usually exceeds fabrication time
  visible surface planning  orient so the seen surfaces need least work
  adhesives            matched to the material pair; the joint design matters as
                       much as the adhesive
  fasteners into plastic  heat-set inserts rather than threading directly, for
                       anything disassembled more than once
  post-processing for resin  wash and cure; the cure step determines final
                       properties and is frequently rushed

## Prototyping Practice

The purpose of a prototype is to answer a question, and being explicit about which question
determines what to build. A fit prototype tests whether parts assemble and needs only dimensional
accuracy at the interfaces. A form prototype tests appearance and ergonomics and needs surface
quality and correct proportions. A function prototype tests whether a mechanism works and needs the
right materials in the loaded regions and nowhere else. Building a prototype that answers all three
costs several times more than building three that each answer one.

Iteration speed matters more than iteration quality in early stages. A rough part in hand tomorrow
teaches more than a refined part next week, because the questions it answers redirect the design
before more effort is invested in the wrong direction. The practical corollary is that the
fabrication method for early prototypes should be chosen for turnaround rather than for fidelity, and
cardboard, tape, and hand-cut parts remain genuinely useful at this stage.

Documentation is what makes a project repeatable and transferable, and it is what almost every
project lacks. Source files with the parameters that matter, the machine settings used, the material
specifications, an assembly sequence, and a note about what did not work are enough. The last item is
the most valuable and the least often recorded — the failed approaches are what prevent the next
person, frequently oneself six months later, from repeating them.

Prototyping practice:
  the question first   what is this prototype for
  fit prototype        assembly; accuracy at the interfaces only
  form prototype       appearance and ergonomics; surface and proportion
  function prototype   mechanism; correct material where loaded
  the separation economy  three focused prototypes cost less than one that does
                       everything
  iteration speed      more valuable than iteration quality early
  the redirect value   rough parts answer questions before effort is committed
  low-fidelity methods  cardboard, tape, and hand-cut parts remain useful
  tolerance discovery  print or cut a test piece with a range of clearances and
                       measure which fits; faster than calculating
  the test piece habit  a small calibration part before a long job
  documentation        source files, settings, materials, assembly, and failures
  the parameters that matter  recorded explicitly rather than embedded implicitly
  the failure record   the most valuable and least often kept
  version discipline   a file naming or version control scheme, because a folder
                       of similarly named files is how a working design gets lost
  sharing              a documented project that others can reproduce is a
                       different artifact from a finished object, and deciding
                       which one is being made changes what work is worth doing
