## Grammars and Rewriting Systems

Procedural generation creates content algorithmically rather than by hand, and the oldest
family of techniques is rewriting: start from a symbol and repeatedly replace symbols
according to rules until a terminal string remains, then interpret that string as structure.

L-systems, developed to model plant growth, rewrite all symbols in parallel at each step,
which is what distinguishes them from ordinary formal grammars and what makes them model
biological growth well — every cell divides simultaneously rather than one at a time. The
resulting string is interpreted by a turtle: symbols command forward movement, rotation,
and the pushing and popping of state that produces branching. A handful of rules and a few
iterations produce convincing trees, ferns, and space-filling curves.

The extensions are what make L-systems practical rather than merely elegant. Stochastic
rules select among alternatives by probability, so repeated generation gives variety rather
than identical copies. Context-sensitive rules let a symbol's rewriting depend on its
neighbours, which models signal propagation through a growing structure. Parametric systems
attach numeric parameters to symbols — length, width, age — so that rules can express
continuous change and conditional behaviour, which is how realistic tapering and
age-dependent branching are obtained.

Shape grammars apply the same idea to geometry directly rather than through a string,
rewriting shapes into arrangements of shapes. Split grammars, their architectural
specialization, repeatedly subdivide a mass into floors, then into bays, then into window
and wall elements, which is how procedural building generation works and why it produces
plausible facades: real buildings genuinely are organized by hierarchical subdivision.

Rewriting system components:
  alphabet             the symbols
  axiom                the starting string
  production rules     symbol to string replacements
  parallel rewriting   all symbols rewritten simultaneously; the L-system defining
                       property
  turtle interpretation F forward, +/- rotate, [ ] push/pop state
  stochastic rules     multiple productions with probabilities; variety
  context sensitive    rewriting depends on neighbours
  parametric           symbols carry numeric parameters
  shape grammar        rewriting on geometry rather than symbols
  split grammar        hierarchical subdivision; facades and interiors
  wave function collapse  constraint propagation over local adjacency rules

The characteristic failure of grammar-based generation is that local rules do not guarantee
global properties. An L-system tree can grow branches that intersect, because no rule
considers what other branches are doing. A split grammar can produce a building whose
windows do not align between floors. The remedies are post-processing to detect and repair
violations, adding environmental sensitivity so that rules query the surrounding geometry
before firing, or moving to a formulation where global constraints are expressed directly —
which is what constraint-based methods do.

Q: An L-system produces identical output on every run. The most direct way to get variety
while preserving the same structural character is:
A. Increase the iteration count
B. Add stochastic productions with weighted alternatives
C. Change the axiom each run
D. Add more symbols to the alphabet
Reasoning: Stochastic productions replace a symbol with one of several alternatives chosen
by probability, so the structural grammar is preserved while the specific realization varies.
More iterations produce a larger structure of the same character; changing the axiom changes
the starting point rather than the variation; more symbols changes the grammar itself.
Answer: B.

## Constraint-Based Generation

Constraint-based methods invert the grammar approach: rather than specifying how content is
built, specify what must be true of it and let a solver find something satisfying the
requirements. This is attractive because designers naturally think in constraints — every
room must be reachable, the key must be findable before the door, no two adjacent tiles may
be incompatible — and because it guarantees the constraints rather than hoping the generative
process happens to satisfy them.

Wave function collapse is the technique that popularized this framing in generative art. It
takes an example input, extracts the set of local patterns and their adjacency
compatibilities, then fills an output grid by repeatedly choosing the cell with fewest
remaining possibilities, collapsing it to one option, and propagating the consequences to
neighbours. The result resembles the input locally everywhere while being globally novel.
Its appeal is that the designer specifies the style by drawing an example rather than by
writing rules.

The technique's characteristic problem is contradiction: propagation can reach a state where
some cell has no valid options, and the generation fails. The standard responses are
backtracking, which is expensive; restarting, which is simple and wasteful; and constraint
relaxation, which permits an imperfect result rather than none. In practice, careful design
of the tile set to avoid over-constraining, plus restart-on-failure, is what most
implementations do.

Answer set programming and SAT-based approaches generalize this. Content requirements are
expressed as logical constraints and a general-purpose solver finds satisfying assignments,
with the ability to enumerate distinct solutions for variety. This handles global constraints
that local propagation cannot — that a level must be completable, that a puzzle must have
exactly one solution, that a quest chain must be acyclic — at the cost of much heavier
computation and the difficulty of expressing aesthetic preferences as logic.

Constraint approaches:
  wave function collapse  local adjacency from an example; fast, style-driven
  model synthesis         the predecessor; similar idea with different propagation
  answer set programming  full logical constraints; global guarantees, slower
  SAT / SMT               constraint satisfaction with rich theories
  constraint propagation  arc consistency; prune impossible options early
  backtracking            recover from contradictions at some cost
  soft constraints        preferences with weights rather than hard requirements
  generate and test       simple, effective when the acceptance rate is decent,
                          hopeless when constraints are tight
  repair-based            generate freely, then fix violations; often the most
                          practical hybrid

The choice between generate-and-test and constraint solving comes down to the acceptance
rate. If a naive generator produces a valid artifact most of the time, testing and rejecting
is far simpler than building a solver. If valid artifacts are rare — a level that must be
completable, a puzzle with a unique solution — rejection sampling never terminates and a
constructive or constraint-based method is required. Estimating that acceptance rate early is
worth doing before committing to an architecture.

## Terrain, Worlds, and Continuous Content

Terrain generation is the most visible application and rests on noise. A heightmap generated
from fractal Brownian motion — layered noise octaves at increasing frequency and decreasing
amplitude — produces plausible rolling landscape immediately. The refinements are what turn
that into something with character.

Ridged noise, taking one minus the absolute value of noise and squaring, produces sharp
ridgelines resembling mountain ranges rather than rounded hills. Domain warping, evaluating
noise at coordinates displaced by another noise field, produces the flowing, folded
structure of real geology rather than isotropic bumps. Erosion simulation — either
hydraulic, moving material downhill with simulated water flow, or thermal, relaxing slopes
beyond an angle of repose — produces the drainage networks, valleys, and talus slopes that
make terrain read as having a history rather than as noise.

Erosion deserves emphasis because it is the single highest-impact addition. Pure fractal
noise produces terrain that is statistically self-similar and geologically impossible: no
drainage, no valleys converging, no sediment deposition in basins. Even a crude hydraulic
erosion pass — drop particles, let them pick up and deposit material according to slope and
capacity, repeat — transforms the result, because real terrain is shaped overwhelmingly by
water and the eye recognizes drainage structure even without knowing why.

Terrain and world generation:
  fractal noise         layered octaves; the base heightmap
  ridged noise          sharp crests; mountain ranges
  domain warping        folded, flowing structure; the highest-value cheap addition
  hydraulic erosion     drainage networks, valleys, sediment; transformative
  thermal erosion       talus slopes at the angle of repose
  Voronoi regions       tectonic plates, biomes, political territories
  Whittaker biomes      assign biome from temperature and precipitation fields
  rivers                trace downhill from high points; carve and widen
  masks and gradients   island shapes, continental outlines, playable-area limits
  chunked generation    generate on demand from position-seeded noise; enables
                        effectively infinite worlds with no storage
  level of detail       resolution scaled by distance; essential at world scale

Infinite world generation requires that content be a pure function of position and seed,
computable independently of any other chunk. This is why noise functions rather than
simulation are the base layer: noise is evaluable at any coordinate in isolation, while
erosion is inherently global. The standard compromise is a noise base that is
position-independent, plus locally-simulated detail within a chunk, plus careful handling of
chunk boundaries so that features crossing them remain continuous — which usually means
generating with overlap and blending, or restricting cross-boundary features to the base
layer.

Domain warping costs almost nothing and changes the character immediately. Instead of
sampling noise at position p, sample it at p plus a vector derived from another noise field.
That converts isotropic bumpiness into folded, flowing structure with directionality, which
is what real geology has and pure fractal noise does not. Two lines of code and a warp
strength parameter to tune.

Hydraulic erosion is the bigger win and costs more. Your terrain has no drainage — no
valleys converging into larger valleys, no ridgelines separating watersheds, no sediment
deposited in flat basins. The eye recognizes drainage structure even without knowing what it
is looking at, and its absence is the main reason fractal terrain reads as noise. A
particle-based erosion pass is straightforward: drop a droplet at a random point, let it flow
downhill accumulating sediment proportional to speed and slope, deposit when it slows or
pools, and run a few hundred thousand droplets. Even a crude implementation transforms the
result.

Two smaller things while you are in there. Add ridged noise to at least the high-frequency
octaves — one minus the absolute value of noise, squared — which gives sharp crests instead
of rounded lumps. And your biomes are assigned from height alone, which produces the
characteristic horizontal banding that immediately reads as generated. Generate separate
temperature and moisture fields (temperature falling with height and latitude, moisture
from a noise field modified by prevailing wind and rain shadow) and assign biomes from that
two-dimensional space. Rain shadow especially — drier on the lee side of a range — is a
strong realism cue that is nearly free once you have a wind direction.

Order: domain warp, then ridged octaves, then biome fields, then erosion. Each is visible on
its own so you learn what each contributes.

## Graphs, Dungeons, and Discrete Structure

Discrete content — level layouts, dungeon maps, quest structures, skill trees — is naturally
represented as a graph, and generating it well means generating the graph first and the
geometry afterward. The mistake that produces incoherent levels is generating rooms and
corridors geometrically and hoping the connectivity turns out interesting.

The graph-first approach generates the intended structure — a critical path with branches,
optional loops, gated sections — as an abstract graph, verifies its properties, and only then
embeds it in space. This guarantees that the level is completable, that keys precede their
doors, that optional content is genuinely optional, and that the difficulty progression is
what was intended, because all of those are graph properties checked before any geometry
exists.

Space partitioning generates layouts by recursive subdivision. Binary space partitioning
splits a rectangle repeatedly, placing a room in each leaf and connecting siblings, which
produces the characteristic rectangular dungeon efficiently and with guaranteed
connectivity. Cellular automata smoothing produces organic cave systems from random noise by
repeatedly applying a rule that turns a cell into whatever most of its neighbours are, and
requires a connectivity check afterward since it readily produces isolated pockets.

Discrete generation techniques:
  BSP subdivision       rectangular rooms with guaranteed connectivity
  cellular automata     organic caves; needs a flood fill to verify connectivity
  random walk / drunkard  simple tunnelling; produces winding organic paths
  Delaunay + MST        connect rooms by minimum spanning tree, then add a few
                        extra edges for loops; produces good connectivity structure
  graph grammar         rewrite an abstract mission graph, then embed
  lock and key          gating structure; verify solvability on the graph
  Poisson disc          room or object placement with minimum spacing
  flood fill            connectivity verification; run it always
  A-star / pathfinding  verify traversability and measure path length as a
                        difficulty proxy

The minimum spanning tree plus extra edges pattern is worth knowing because it produces
good level connectivity with two lines of reasoning. A minimum spanning tree over room
centres guarantees every room is reachable with no redundant connections, which is a
tree — and a pure tree level is frustrating, because every dead end must be backtracked. Adding
back a small fraction of the discarded edges, chosen from the shortest, introduces loops that
make navigation pleasant without making the space incomprehensible. The fraction of extra
edges is a single tunable parameter controlling how maze-like versus how open the level
feels.

## Controllability and Evaluation

The central tension in procedural generation is between variety and control. A system with
no constraints produces varied and mostly bad output; a heavily constrained system produces
reliably acceptable and boringly similar output. The design work is finding the parameterization
where the achievable outputs are all acceptable and meaningfully different.

Controllability techniques give a designer authority over the result without abandoning
generation. Seeded determinism allows a specific output to be recovered and refined.
Exposed parameters with meaningful names let a designer steer at a high level. Authored
constraints let specific content be pinned while the rest generates around it — a hand-built
boss room embedded in a generated dungeon. Mixed-initiative tools let a designer edit a
generated result and regenerate the remainder consistently with the edit, which is the
interaction model that has proven most usable in practice.

Evaluation is where procedural generation is weakest, because the quality criteria are
usually aesthetic and the output space is too large to inspect. Automated metrics can
capture some properties objectively: connectivity, path length, symmetry, density,
compressibility as a proxy for structure, and expressive range — the region of a metric space
the generator actually covers, which reveals whether the variety is real or nominal.

Evaluation approaches:
  hard constraints       completability, connectivity, reachability; verify always
  expressive range       plot outputs in a two-dimensional metric space; a
                         generator clustering tightly has less variety than it
                         appears to
  diversity metrics      pairwise distance between outputs under some
                         representation
  playtesting            for interactive content, the ground truth
  contact sheet          judge a hundred outputs at small size, never one at full
  worst-case review      inspect the least good outputs; a system is only as good
                         as its floor if outputs cannot be curated
  A-star difficulty      path length and branching as a difficulty proxy
  designer review        a domain expert rating a sample; expensive and honest

Expressive range analysis is the most useful evaluation tool and the least used. Generate a
few hundred artifacts, compute two or three meaningful metrics on each, and plot them.
Generators that feel varied frequently occupy a small tight cluster, which explains why their
output starts to feel repetitive after a while even though no two artifacts are identical.
Seeing the covered region also shows which parameter changes move the generator into new
territory and which merely jiggle it within the same cluster, which is far more actionable
than a general impression that the output needs more variety.

The final practical principle is to design for the worst case rather than the best. A
generator producing an occasional spectacular result alongside frequent failures is only
usable if a human curates the output, and that curation does not scale and does not work at
all for content generated at runtime. A generator whose worst output is acceptable can run
unattended, and the accumulated experience of the field is that constraining the system until
its floor is acceptable — even at the cost of lowering its ceiling — produces more usable work
than the reverse.
