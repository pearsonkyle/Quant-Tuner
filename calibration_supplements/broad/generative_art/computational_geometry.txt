## Primitives and Geometric Predicates

Computational geometry builds everything from a small set of primitives, and the most
important is the orientation predicate: given three points, does the third lie to the left of,
to the right of, or on the directed line through the first two. It is computed as the sign of a
determinant, equivalently the sign of a two-dimensional cross product, and an enormous fraction
of the field reduces to evaluating it.

From orientation follow most of the tests that algorithms need. Whether two segments intersect
is determined by the orientations of each segment's endpoints relative to the other segment,
with special handling for collinear cases. Whether a point lies inside a convex polygon is
determined by having consistent orientation with respect to every edge. Whether a polygon's
vertices are listed clockwise or counterclockwise is determined by the sign of its signed area,
which is the shoelace formula and is itself a sum of orientation determinants.

The signed area is worth knowing in its own right because it does several jobs at once. Its
magnitude gives the polygon's area for any simple polygon, convex or not. Its sign gives the
winding direction, which matters for consistent normals, for fill rules, and for distinguishing
holes from outer boundaries. And the same formula generalizes to give the centroid, which is
needed constantly for placement and for physics.

Primitives and predicates:
  orientation           sign of a 2D cross product; left, right, or collinear
  signed area           shoelace formula; magnitude is area, sign is winding
  centroid              area-weighted; not the average of the vertices except for
                        a triangle
  segment intersection  from orientations of endpoint pairs; collinear overlap is
                        the special case
  point in convex poly  consistent orientation against every edge; O(n), or
                        O(log n) with binary search on a fan
  point in simple poly  ray casting parity, or the winding number; ray casting
                        needs care when the ray passes exactly through a vertex
  in-circle test        does a point lie inside the circle through three others;
                        the predicate defining Delaunay triangulation
  convexity test        all consecutive orientations agree
  distance point-line   the cross product magnitude divided by the segment length
  closest point on seg  project and clamp the parameter to [0, 1]
  barycentric coords    a point as a weighted combination of triangle vertices;
                        inside when all weights are non-negative

Barycentric coordinates deserve emphasis because they solve several problems at once and are
underused by people who have not encountered them. Expressing a point as a weighted combination
of a triangle's vertices, with weights summing to one, simultaneously tests containment — all
weights non-negative means inside — and provides the interpolation weights for any attribute
stored at the vertices. This is exactly what a graphics rasterizer computes per pixel, and it
is the natural way to interpolate colour, texture coordinates, or any other per-vertex data
across a triangle.

Q: The shoelace formula applied to a polygon's vertices returns a negative value. This
indicates:
A. The polygon is invalid
B. The vertices are ordered clockwise under the convention where counterclockwise is positive
C. The polygon is concave
D. A computational error
Reasoning: The signed area's sign encodes winding direction. A negative value under the
standard convention means clockwise ordering, which is entirely valid and is frequently used
deliberately to mark interior holes in a polygon with holes. The magnitude is the area
regardless of sign, and concavity does not affect the sign. Answer: B.

## Convex Hulls, Triangulation, and Voronoi

The convex hull is the smallest convex set containing a point set, and it is one of the most
frequently computed structures because so many problems reduce to it. Graham's scan sorts
points by angle from a reference and builds the hull by maintaining a stack, discarding points
that create a non-convex turn. Andrew's monotone chain sorts by coordinate and builds upper and
lower hulls separately, which is simpler to implement correctly and equally efficient at
O(n log n) dominated by the sort. Gift wrapping is O(nh) in the output size h, which is better
than O(n log n) when the hull has very few vertices.

Triangulation decomposes a region into triangles, which is required because most downstream
algorithms and all graphics hardware operate on triangles. Any simple polygon can be
triangulated, and ear clipping is the straightforward method: repeatedly find a vertex whose
adjacent vertices can be joined without leaving the polygon or containing another vertex, and
remove it. It is O(n^2) naively and adequate for most practical polygon sizes.

Delaunay triangulation is the canonical triangulation of a point set, defined by the property
that no point lies inside the circumcircle of any triangle. Among all triangulations of the
same points it maximizes the minimum angle, which means it avoids the thin sliver triangles that
cause numerical problems in interpolation and in finite element analysis. This is why it is the
default for mesh generation and terrain modelling from scattered samples.

The Voronoi diagram partitions space into regions closest to each of a set of sites, and it is
the dual of the Delaunay triangulation — connecting sites whose Voronoi regions share an edge
produces exactly the Delaunay edges. Computing one gives the other, which is convenient because
the two answer different questions: Voronoi answers nearest-site queries and describes regions
of influence, while Delaunay provides a mesh.

Hulls, triangulation, and Voronoi:
  convex hull           smallest containing convex set
  Graham scan           angular sort plus a stack; O(n log n)
  monotone chain        coordinate sort, upper and lower hulls; simpler to
                        implement
  gift wrapping         O(nh); better when the hull is small
  quickhull             divide and conquer; fast in practice
  ear clipping          simple polygon triangulation; O(n^2) naive
  Delaunay              empty circumcircle property; maximizes the minimum angle
  Lawson flipping       incremental Delaunay by flipping non-Delaunay edges
  constrained Delaunay  forces specified edges into the triangulation
  Voronoi diagram       nearest-site regions; the dual of Delaunay
  Fortune's algorithm   sweepline Voronoi construction in O(n log n)
  Lloyd relaxation      move sites to their region centroids and repeat; produces
                        an even, organic-looking distribution
  power diagram         weighted Voronoi; regions vary with site weight

Lloyd relaxation deserves mention because it is the standard route to a distribution that
looks deliberately even without being regular. Starting from random points, computing the
Voronoi diagram, and moving each site to its cell's centroid, repeated a few times, converges
toward a centroidal Voronoi tessellation in which cells are similar in size and roughly
hexagonal. This is the blue-noise distribution that reads as natural in stippling, in point
placement, and in sampling, and it is why a handful of relaxation iterations transforms a
clumpy random scatter into something that looks placed by hand.

## Intersection, Clipping, and Curves

Intersection tests are the workhorse of interactive geometry and their cost hierarchy should
guide how they are ordered. Bounding volume tests are cheap and conservative — an
axis-aligned box overlap test is a handful of comparisons — so they serve as early rejection
before an exact test. Sphere tests are similarly cheap and rotation-invariant. Exact tests
against the actual geometry are expensive and are performed only for candidates that survived
the cheap filters.

Polygon clipping computes the intersection, union, or difference of polygonal regions, and it
is the operation underlying constructive geometry, occlusion, and much of vector graphics.
Sutherland-Hodgman clips a polygon against a convex region by clipping successively against
each edge, which is simple and produces degenerate connecting edges for concave subjects.
Weiler-Atherton handles the general case including holes. Robust general polygon boolean
operations are substantially harder than they appear, and using a well-tested library rather
than implementing them is the pragmatic choice for anything beyond convex clipping.

Curves are represented parametrically, and Bezier curves are the standard because their
control polygon provides intuitive editing and their mathematical properties are convenient.
A cubic Bezier passes through its first and last control points and is pulled toward the middle
two, lies within the convex hull of its control points, and can be subdivided exactly at any
parameter into two Bezier curves — which is what makes adaptive rendering and intersection by
recursive subdivision practical.

Intersection and curves:
  AABB overlap          cheapest broad-phase test; separating axis on each axis
  bounding sphere       rotation invariant; cheap
  separating axis       for convex shapes; if any axis separates them, they do not
                        intersect
  ray-triangle          Moller-Trumbore; the standard fast method
  ray-sphere            solve a quadratic
  polygon clipping      Sutherland-Hodgman for convex clip regions;
                        Weiler-Atherton for the general case
  Bezier curve          control polygon, convex hull property, exact subdivision
  de Casteljau          numerically stable evaluation by repeated interpolation
  continuity            C0 position, C1 tangent, C2 curvature; G1 and G2 are the
                        weaker geometric versions sufficient for visual smoothness
  B-spline              local control; moving one control point affects a bounded
                        region
  NURBS                 rational B-splines; represent conics exactly
  Catmull-Rom           interpolating spline; passes through its control points,
                        which Bezier control points do not
  arc length            not available in closed form for cubics; parameterize by
                        arc length numerically when even spacing matters

The distinction between parameter spacing and arc length spacing is a practical trap. Sampling
a Bezier curve at evenly spaced parameter values does not produce evenly spaced points along the
curve — the spacing varies with the curve's speed, so points bunch where the curve is slow and
spread where it is fast. For rendering this rarely matters; for placing objects along a path,
animating at constant speed, or generating a plotter toolpath, it matters a great deal, and the
remedy is building a lookup table mapping arc length to parameter and inverting it.

[user] I'm placing points on a Bezier path for a generative piece and the spacing looks
uneven even though I'm stepping t uniformly.
[assistant] tool_call geom.curve_sampling({"curve": "cubic_bezier",
"sampling": "uniform_parameter", "control_spread": "asymmetric",
"observed": "clustering_near_endpoints"})
[tool] {"cause": "parameter_is_not_arc_length", "speed_variation_ratio": 3.4,
"fix": "arclength_reparameterization", "method": "cumulative_length_lut_with_inverse
_lookup", "samples_for_lut": 200}
[assistant] Uniform steps in the parameter are not uniform steps along the curve. The
parameter is just an index into the polynomial, and the curve's speed — the magnitude of its
derivative — varies along it, in your case by a factor of 3.4 between the fastest and slowest
regions. Points bunch where the curve moves slowly.

The standard fix is arc-length reparameterization. Sample the curve densely, say 200 points at
uniform parameter values, compute the cumulative distance along that polyline, and store it as
a lookup table mapping parameter to arc length. To place a point at a fraction of the total
length, binary search the table for that length and interpolate between the bracketing entries
to get the parameter, then evaluate the curve there.

Two hundred samples is plenty for a single cubic; the error from the polyline approximation is
well below what is visible. If you need higher accuracy — a plotter toolpath at constant feed
rate, for instance — increase the sample count or use adaptive subdivision that refines where
the curvature is high.

Two related things worth knowing. If your path is several Bezier segments joined, build the
table across the whole path rather than per segment, or you will get even spacing within each
segment and a discontinuity in density at the joins — which is often more visually obvious than
the original problem. And if you want the points to be evenly spaced *and* to fall at particular
features, sample by arc length and then snap the nearest sample to each feature rather than
inserting extra points, which preserves the even appearance.

If constant spacing is not actually what you want aesthetically, the speed variation is a
usable signal in itself: placing more elements where the curve is slow emphasizes the tight
turns, which reads as the path having weight.

## Spatial Data Structures and Meshes

Spatial data structures accelerate queries by organizing geometry so that most of it can be
excluded without examination. The general principle is a hierarchy: test a bounding volume, and
if it fails, skip everything inside it. The structures differ in how they partition and in
whether they adapt to the data.

A uniform grid divides space into equal cells and is trivially simple, with constant-time
insertion and neighbourhood queries, and it degrades badly when the data is unevenly
distributed, since dense regions produce overfull cells. A quadtree or octree subdivides
recursively where density is high, adapting to the data at the cost of a deeper traversal. A
k-d tree splits alternately along each axis at the median, giving a balanced tree well suited to
nearest-neighbour queries. A bounding volume hierarchy groups objects rather than partitioning
space, which handles objects of varying size gracefully and permits overlapping volumes, and is
the standard for ray tracing.

Mesh representations store connectivity as well as geometry, and the choice determines which
operations are efficient. An indexed triangle list stores vertices once and triangles as index
triples, which is compact and is what graphics hardware consumes, and it makes neighbour queries
expensive since finding a triangle's neighbours requires a search. A half-edge structure stores
directed edges with pointers to their twin, next, and incident face, which makes every local
traversal constant time and is what mesh processing algorithms require.

Structures and meshes:
  uniform grid          simple, fast, poor with uneven density
  quadtree / octree     adaptive subdivision; good for varying density
  k-d tree              median splits alternating axes; nearest neighbour queries
  BVH                   bounding volumes around objects; ray tracing standard
  R-tree                for storage and databases; handles extents rather than
                        points
  spatial hash          hash cell coordinates; simple, no hierarchy, good for
                        uniform-size dynamic objects
  indexed triangles     compact, hardware-friendly, poor connectivity queries
  half-edge             constant-time local traversal; the mesh processing standard
  winged-edge           an older equivalent
  manifold              every edge has exactly two incident faces; required by
                        many algorithms and by 3D printing
  watertight            no boundary edges; encloses a volume
  normals               per-face is flat, per-vertex is smooth; crease angle
                        thresholds decide which
  simplification        edge collapse driven by a quadric error metric; the
                        standard method for level of detail
  subdivision           Catmull-Clark for quads, Loop for triangles; smooth
                        refinement

Manifoldness deserves emphasis because it is the property most often violated in
generatively-produced geometry and the one that breaks downstream tools. A mesh with an edge
shared by three faces, with duplicate vertices at the same position that leave the surface
topologically disconnected, with inconsistent face winding, or with self-intersections may
render acceptably and will fail in boolean operations, in simplification, in physics
simulation, and in 3D printing slicers. Checking manifoldness and watertightness as part of the
generation pipeline, rather than discovering the failure at the printer, is worth the small
effort.

## Robustness and Numerical Issues

Geometric algorithms are unusually sensitive to floating-point error because they make
discrete decisions from continuous computations. An orientation predicate returning the wrong
sign for three nearly collinear points does not produce a slightly wrong answer; it produces a
combinatorially inconsistent one, and algorithms that assume consistency then crash, loop
forever, or produce invalid output. This is qualitatively different from numerical error in,
say, a physics simulation, where a small error produces a slightly different trajectory.

Degenerate configurations are where this concentrates: collinear points, coincident points,
concurrent lines, points exactly on a boundary, zero-area triangles, and parallel segments. They
are rare in random data and common in real data, because real data comes from grids, from
architectural drawings, from repeated structures, and from prior computations that produced
exact alignments. An algorithm tested on random points and deployed on real ones frequently
fails immediately.

The remedies form a hierarchy of cost and reliability. Epsilon comparisons — treating values
within a tolerance as equal — are simple, and they are not transitive, so three points can be
pairwise within tolerance while the relation they define is inconsistent, which reintroduces
the original problem. Exact arithmetic using integers or rationals is fully reliable and is
possible when coordinates can be represented exactly. Adaptive precision predicates compute a
fast floating-point estimate with an error bound and fall back to higher precision only when the
estimate is not provably correct, which gives exactness at nearly floating-point speed and is
what robust libraries use.

Robustness practice:
  degeneracy            collinear, coincident, concurrent, zero-area; common in
                        real data and rare in random data
  sign errors           produce combinatorial inconsistency, not small error
  epsilon comparison    simple and not transitive; a partial remedy
  exact arithmetic      integers or rationals; fully reliable, slower
  adaptive predicates   fast estimate with an error bound, escalating only when
                        needed; exactness at near-float speed
  symbolic perturbation  consistently break ties as if points were slightly moved;
                        removes degenerate cases by definition
  snap rounding         round coordinates to a grid to make degeneracies explicit
                        rather than near-degenerate
  input validation      check for duplicates, near-duplicates, self-intersections,
                        and winding consistency before running anything
  use a library         CGAL, Clipper, and similar have solved this; reimplementing
                        robust boolean operations is a multi-year project
  test with real data   random point tests hide exactly the failures that matter
  visualize failures    geometric bugs are far easier to see than to read in a
                        stack trace

The final practical recommendation is to visualize aggressively. A geometric algorithm's
intermediate state is a picture, and rendering it — the hull as it is being built, the
triangulation before and after each flip, the clipped polygon with its winding indicated — makes
bugs immediately apparent that are nearly impossible to find by reading values in a debugger.
Building that visualization early costs an hour and repays it many times over, and for
generative work it frequently becomes part of the piece itself.
