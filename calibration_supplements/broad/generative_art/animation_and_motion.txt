## Principles of Motion

Animation communicates through change over time, and the principles established by traditional
animators transfer directly to computational work because they describe perception rather than
technique. The most consequential is that motion carries meaning independent of what is moving:
the same shape moving in different ways reads as heavy or light, alive or mechanical, deliberate
or accidental.

Timing and spacing are the fundamentals. Timing is how long a motion takes, and spacing is how
the object is distributed through the intervening positions. Equal spacing reads as mechanical
because nothing physical starts and stops instantaneously; accumulating spacing at the start and
end — slow in and slow out — reads as physical because it corresponds to acceleration and
deceleration. Almost every animation that feels wrong feels wrong because of spacing rather than
because of the path.

Anticipation, follow-through, and overlapping action all derive from physical bodies having mass
and connected parts. A movement is preceded by a small opposite motion, because momentum must be
built. Parts continue past the stopping point and settle back, because they have inertia. Loosely
connected parts lag behind the driving part, arriving at different times. Applying these to
abstract objects makes them read as physical, which is why an interface element that overshoots
slightly and settles feels responsive while one that stops instantly feels brittle.

Motion principles:
  timing               how long; sets weight and urgency
  spacing              distribution through the motion; sets acceleration
  slow in and out      accumulating spacing at the ends; reads as physical
  anticipation         a small opposite motion first; prepares the viewer and
                       implies momentum
  follow-through       parts continue past the stop and settle
  overlapping action   connected parts arrive at different times
  arcs                 natural motion follows curved paths, not straight lines
  squash and stretch   deformation implying elasticity and impact; preserve volume
                       or the object reads as inflating
  secondary action     supporting motion that reinforces the primary without
                       competing
  exaggeration         readable motion is usually larger than literal
  staging              direct attention to what matters; one thing at a time
  appeal               clarity and interest in the design of the motion itself
  weight               conveyed by timing and by how motion begins and ends;
                       heavy things accelerate slowly and stop slowly

Arcs deserve emphasis because straight-line motion is the default in any keyframe system and is
almost never what physical motion looks like. A limb rotating about a joint traces an arc, a
thrown object follows a parabola, a head turning moves along a curve. Interpolating a position
linearly between two keyframes produces a straight path that reads as mechanical, and adding a
slight arc — even a small one — makes the same motion read as organic. This is the single
highest-value correction to computer-generated motion.

Q: An interface element animates from one position to another with constant velocity and feels
mechanical. The most effective single change is:
A. Make it faster
B. Apply easing so it accelerates from rest and decelerates into position
C. Add a colour change
D. Make it slower
Reasoning: Constant velocity means instantaneous acceleration at the start and stop, which does
not occur physically and which the perceptual system reads as mechanical. Easing distributes the
motion so it begins and ends gradually, matching how objects with mass move. Duration affects
urgency and does not address the underlying issue. Answer: B.

## Timing, Easing, and Interpolation

Easing functions map normalized time to normalized progress, and the choice of function is the
main expressive control in most animation systems. Linear maps time to progress directly and
reads as mechanical. Ease-out starts fast and decelerates, which suits an element entering or
responding to input because the motion begins immediately and settles gently. Ease-in starts slow
and accelerates, which suits an element leaving. Ease-in-out does both and suits motion between
two resting states.

The asymmetry between ease-in and ease-out is worth stating because it is frequently applied
backwards. An element responding to a user action should ease out — begin immediately so the
response feels instant, then decelerate into place. An element easing in appears to hesitate
before moving, which reads as latency even when the total duration is identical. This is why
interface motion overwhelmingly uses ease-out or a spring rather than symmetric easing.

Duration carries meaning and is bounded by perception. Motion below about a hundred milliseconds
is not perceived as motion, appearing instead as an instantaneous change; motion above about five
hundred milliseconds begins to feel slow in an interface context. The useful range for interface
transitions is roughly 150 to 400 milliseconds, with larger distances and larger elements
warranting longer durations because the perceived speed depends on distance covered rather than
on duration alone.

Timing and interpolation:
  linear               constant velocity; mechanical
  ease-out             fast start, gentle stop; entering, responding to input
  ease-in              gentle start, fast end; leaving
  ease-in-out          both; motion between resting states
  cubic Bezier easing  the standard parameterization; two control points define
                       the curve
  spring               physically parameterized by stiffness and damping;
                       naturally interruptible and produces overshoot when
                       underdamped
  duration bounds      below ~100 ms reads as instantaneous; above ~500 ms reads
                       as slow in an interface
  distance and duration  larger travel warrants longer duration; constant duration
                       across distances produces inconsistent perceived speed
  stagger              offset the start times of a group; reads as coordinated
                       rather than simultaneous, and directs the eye
  keyframe             a specified state at a time; interpolation fills between
  interpolation        linear, cubic, spline; the curve through the keyframes
  slerp                spherical interpolation for rotations; linear interpolation
                       of quaternions changes angular velocity and can take the
                       long path
  gimbal lock          Euler angles lose a degree of freedom at certain
                       orientations; quaternions avoid it

Rotation interpolation deserves specific mention because it fails in ways position
interpolation does not. Interpolating Euler angles independently produces motion that does not
follow the shortest rotational path and that can pass through gimbal lock, where two axes align
and a degree of freedom is lost. Quaternions represent rotation without that degeneracy, and
spherical linear interpolation between them follows the shortest arc at constant angular velocity
— which is what rotation should do and what naive interpolation does not provide.

Springs deserve mention as an alternative to duration-based easing because their properties suit
interactive motion. A spring is parameterized by stiffness and damping rather than by duration,
so the motion emerges from the physical parameters, and it is naturally interruptible — a new
target simply changes the spring's rest position and the motion continues from the current
position and velocity rather than restarting. Duration-based animation interrupted mid-flight
must either snap or blend, both of which are visible.

## Physics-Based and Procedural Motion

Physics simulation generates motion from forces and constraints rather than from specified
keyframes, which produces plausible interaction automatically and gives up direct control. It is
the right approach where the motion should respond to unpredictable conditions — a cloth
reacting to a character's movement, debris from an impact, a chain swinging — and the wrong
approach where a specific result is required, since achieving a particular outcome from a
simulation means tuning inputs indirectly.

Verlet integration is the workhorse for particle and constraint systems in creative work. It
stores positions rather than velocities, deriving velocity implicitly from the difference between
current and previous positions, which makes constraint satisfaction straightforward: move a
particle to satisfy a constraint and its velocity adjusts automatically. Cloth, rope, and soft
body simulation are built from particles with distance constraints solved iteratively, and the
iteration count trades stiffness against cost.

Procedural animation generates motion algorithmically, which scales to many elements and to
situations that cannot be authored individually. Noise-driven motion produces organic drift and
is the standard technique for idle motion, wind, and secondary sway. Flocking rules produce
coordinated group movement from three local rules — separation, alignment, and cohesion — with no
central coordination, which is the canonical demonstration that complex collective behaviour
emerges from simple local interaction.

Physics and procedural motion:
  Verlet integration   position-based; constraints are easy, velocity implicit
  constraint solving   iterate to satisfy; more iterations means stiffer
  cloth and rope       particles with distance constraints
  soft body            volume and shape constraints
  rigid body           collision detection, response, friction, and resting
                       contact; resting contact is the hard part
  collision detection  broad phase spatial partitioning, then narrow phase exact
                       tests
  restitution          bounciness; the ratio of separation to approach speed
  damping              removes energy; without it, numerical error accumulates as
                       energy gain and simulations explode
  substepping          smaller timesteps for stability; the standard fix for a
                       simulation that jitters or explodes
  noise-driven motion  organic drift, idle motion, wind
  flocking             separation, alignment, cohesion; emergent coordination
  inverse kinematics   solve joint angles to place an end effector; the basis of
                       foot placement and reaching
  forward kinematics   joint angles determine the pose; simpler and less useful
                       for targeting
  procedural walk      generate a gait from parameters; adapts to terrain in a way
                       a recorded cycle does not
  motion capture       records real motion; realistic and difficult to retarget to
                       different proportions

Inverse kinematics deserves attention because it solves the problem that makes character motion
adapt to its environment. Forward kinematics computes where a hand ends up from the joint angles,
which is straightforward and requires the animator to find angles producing the desired position
by trial. Inverse kinematics computes the joint angles placing the hand at a specified position,
which is what allows a foot to plant on uneven terrain, a hand to reach a moving target, or a
character to look at something. It is underdetermined for a chain with more joints than
constraints, so the solver needs additional preferences — a rest pose to stay near, joint limits,
and a preferred bend direction.

The stiffness appearance and the instability come from the same place: with position-based
constraint solving, stiffness is a function of the iteration count rather than of a stiffness
parameter. Three iterations cannot satisfy the constraints, so the cloth stretches visibly under
load, and cranking the stiffness parameter to 1.0 with too few iterations produces overcorrection
that manifests as jitter. Raise the iteration count to around 8 and lower the per-iteration
stiffness — the cloth will be both stiffer in appearance and more stable, which sounds
contradictory and follows directly from how the solver converges.

The explosion under fast movement is a timestep problem. At one substep per frame, a fast-moving
attachment point moves a large distance in one step, which stretches constraints far beyond what
the solver can correct in one frame, and the correction overshoots. Substepping — subdividing the
frame into four smaller steps and running the full solve on each — keeps the per-step
displacement small. This costs four times the solve but is the standard and reliable fix.

Add damping. You have none, and position-based solvers accumulate energy from numerical error
because the constraint projections do work. A small global damping of one to three percent per
step removes it and is imperceptible. Without damping, a simulation left running will gain energy
indefinitely, which is why it eventually explodes even without fast motion.

Two further things worth adding once it is stable. Velocity clamping caps how fast any particle
can move in one step, which prevents a single anomalous frame from destroying the simulation and
is cheap insurance. And a maximum stretch ratio — projecting any constraint stretched beyond,
say, 10% back toward its rest length regardless of the iteration budget — prevents the visually
obvious failure where cloth stretches like rubber under load.

## Performance and Interface Motion

Animation performance is governed by what the browser or engine must do per frame, and the
distinction between properties that trigger layout and those that do not is the dominant factor
in web animation. Changing geometry — width, height, position offsets — forces the layout to be
recomputed for the element and everything affected by it, every frame. Changing transform or
opacity can be applied by the compositor to an already-rendered layer, without layout or paint,
and frequently off the main thread entirely.

The consequence is a rule with few exceptions: animate transform and opacity, and express other
effects in terms of them where possible. Moving an element uses translate rather than position
offsets. Resizing uses scale rather than width and height, accepting that scale distorts
non-uniformly scalable content such as text and borders. Where an effect genuinely requires
animating a layout property, the alternatives are the FLIP technique — measure the first and last
positions, apply an inverting transform, and animate it away — or accepting the cost for a short
transition.

Frame budget is the constraint everything must fit within. At sixty frames per second the budget
is 16.7 milliseconds per frame including everything the system does, so the application's share
is well under that. Exceeding it drops a frame, which is perceptible as a stutter, and an
animation dropping frames intermittently reads as worse than one running at a consistently lower
rate — consistency matters more than the absolute rate above a threshold.

Performance and interface motion:
  transform and opacity  compositor-only; no layout or paint
  layout-triggering     width, height, top, left; recomputed every frame
  FLIP                 first, last, invert, play; animate a transform that
                       replicates a layout change
  frame budget         16.7 ms at 60 fps, including everything
  consistency          steady frame rate matters more than peak rate
  layer promotion      will-change or a 3D transform; each layer costs memory,
                       so promoting everything is counterproductive
  requestAnimationFrame  synchronizes with the display refresh
  reduced motion       respect the user preference; vestibular disorders make
                       large motion and parallax genuinely harmful rather than
                       merely unwanted
  purpose              interface motion should communicate — where something came
                       from, what changed, what is loading; decorative motion that
                       communicates nothing costs attention
  continuity           an element moving from one position to another maintains
                       identity; appearing and disappearing does not
  duration by scale    small elements move quickly, large ones more slowly;
                       constant duration across scales looks inconsistent
  interruptibility     a user action mid-animation should redirect rather than
                       queue or snap

The purpose criterion deserves the closing emphasis because it is what separates motion that
improves an interface from motion that degrades it. Motion communicating spatial relationship —
showing that a panel came from the button that opened it, that a deleted item was removed from
this position, that a list reordered rather than replaced — helps the user build a model of the
interface and reduces the effort of following changes. Motion that merely decorates costs
attention and time on every occurrence, and its cost compounds because interface animation is
seen thousands of times by a regular user. The test worth applying is whether the motion answers a
question the user would otherwise have to work out, and if it does not, the interface is better
without it.
