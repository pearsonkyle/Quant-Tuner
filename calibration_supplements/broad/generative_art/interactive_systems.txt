## Interaction Design for Responsive Work

An interactive work differs from a generative one in that the audience's action is part of the
system, and the central design question is what relationship between action and response the
piece establishes. That relationship is the work as much as any visual or sonic output, and the
options span from direct manipulation, where the response is immediate and proportional, to
mediated influence, where the audience shifts a system's behaviour without controlling it.

Discoverability determines whether an interaction happens at all. An audience encountering a
piece has no manual, no expectation of what it responds to, and typically less than a minute
before they move on. Affordances — visual and physical cues suggesting what can be done — are what
bridge this, and the reliable finding from installation practice is that people do what the piece
appears to invite rather than what a label instructs.

The mapping between input and output carries meaning. A direct mapping, where a gesture produces
an obviously corresponding change, is immediately legible and quickly exhausted. An indirect or
delayed mapping is more interesting to explore and risks the audience never establishing that
they are influencing anything at all. The productive middle gives immediate confirmation that
something responded, with the deeper structure revealed through continued engagement.

Interaction design:
  the relationship     what the audience's action does; the substance of the work
  direct manipulation  immediate proportional response; legible, quickly exhausted
  mediated influence   shifts behaviour without control; richer, harder to
                       discover
  discoverability      whether the interaction is found at all
  affordance           a cue suggesting what can be done; stronger than any label
  immediate feedback   confirm that something responded, even if the deeper effect
                       is slow
  legibility           the audience should be able to form a model of what they
                       are doing
  the first thirty seconds  where an installation is won or lost
  progressive depth    a surface that is immediately legible over a structure that
                       rewards longer engagement
  multi-user           people arriving together; whether they cooperate, compete,
                       or interfere is a design decision rather than an emergent
                       accident
  graceful degradation  behaviour with no audience present; a piece that is inert
                       when unobserved attracts nobody
  the attract state    what the piece does to invite approach

The attract state deserves emphasis because installations frequently fail on it. A piece that
does nothing until someone interacts is never discovered, because a passerby has no reason to
approach an inert object. An attract behaviour — motion, sound, a visible response to distant
presence — invites approach, and the transition from attract to engaged should be legible so the
audience knows their arrival was registered. Designing that transition is as consequential as
designing the interaction itself.

Q: An installation responds richly to gesture but visitors walk past without engaging. The most
likely problem is:
A. The interaction is too complex
B. There is no attract state or affordance signalling that the piece responds, so nobody
   discovers there is an interaction
C. The response is too slow
D. The space is too small
Reasoning: Discovery precedes interaction. A piece that is inert until engaged gives a passerby
no reason to approach or to try anything, so the quality of the interaction is never encountered.
An attract behaviour and a visible affordance signalling responsiveness are what convert
passersby into participants. Answer: B.

## Latency, Feedback, and Perception

Latency between action and response determines whether an interaction feels connected, and the
thresholds are perceptual rather than arbitrary. Below roughly 20 milliseconds a response feels
simultaneous with the action. Between 20 and 100 milliseconds it feels responsive but separable.
Above 100 milliseconds the causal link weakens, and above roughly 200 milliseconds the audience
stops attributing the response to their action at all — which is fatal, since attribution is what
makes an interaction an interaction.

The perceptual requirement differs by modality and by the directness of the coupling. Audio
triggered by a physical action is the most demanding, since the auditory system resolves timing to
a few milliseconds and a delayed sound reads as a separate event rather than as the action's
consequence. Visual response tolerates more. A slow ambient change tolerates a great deal, because
no simultaneity is expected.

Jitter is more damaging than latency, and this is frequently missed. A consistent 50 millisecond
delay is quickly adapted to and becomes imperceptible; a delay varying between 20 and 120
milliseconds cannot be adapted to and feels unreliable regardless of its average. Systems should
therefore be designed for consistency, and where a pipeline has variable latency, deliberately
buffering to a fixed worst-case delay frequently produces a better experience than delivering as
fast as possible.

Latency and feedback:
  under 20 ms          feels simultaneous
  20-100 ms            responsive, separable
  100-200 ms           the causal link weakens
  over 200 ms          attribution to the action is lost
  audio                the most demanding modality; a few milliseconds matter
  jitter               worse than latency; variability cannot be adapted to
  fixed buffering      a consistent worst-case delay beats a variable minimum
  latency sources      sensor sampling, filtering, processing, rendering, display
                       refresh, and output; each contributes and the total is what
                       matters
  filtering tradeoff   smoothing sensor noise adds delay; a filter aggressive
                       enough to remove jitter adds latency that is worse
  prediction           extrapolate the input forward to hide latency; effective
                       for continuous motion and produces overshoot on direction
                       changes
  multimodal feedback  visual, auditory, and haptic together; each confirms
                       differently and together they are far more convincing
  haptic               the most underused; a physical click or vibration confirms
                       an action more definitively than any visual change
  false feedback       immediate acknowledgement of a slow operation; honest when
                       the operation will complete, and corrosive when it may not

The filtering and latency tradeoff deserves specific attention because it is where interactive
sensor pipelines are most often mistuned. A noisy sensor produces jittery output, and the obvious
remedy is a low-pass filter — which introduces delay proportional to how much smoothing it
provides. A filter aggressive enough to eliminate visible jitter frequently adds enough latency to
break the sense of connection, so the piece trades one problem for a worse one. The better
approaches are adaptive filtering that smooths heavily when the input is still and lightly when it
is moving, which is what a one-euro filter does, or improving the sensor rather than the
processing.

## Physical Computing

Physical computing connects code to the physical world through sensors and actuators, and the
practical difficulty is that physical systems have properties software does not — they wear, they
drift, they respond slowly, they fail partially, and they must be safe.

Sensor selection follows from what is actually being detected, which is frequently not what it
seems. Detecting a person's presence might use a passive infrared sensor detecting movement of a
warm body, an ultrasonic or time-of-flight sensor detecting distance, a camera with vision
processing, a pressure mat, a capacitive field, or a break beam. Each detects something different:
passive infrared misses a stationary person entirely, distance sensors cannot distinguish a person
from an object, and a pressure mat requires them to be in a specific place. The failure mode is
choosing by convenience and discovering that the sensor answers a different question than the
piece asks.

Actuator selection has the same character. Motion can be produced by a servo with position
feedback and limited range, a stepper with open-loop positioning and no feedback, a DC motor with
speed rather than position control, a solenoid for discrete actuation, or a shape memory alloy
for slow silent motion. Noise, heat, power draw, and duty cycle are the properties that determine
whether an actuator works in an installation running eight hours a day for three months, and they
are rarely the properties considered first.

Physical computing:
  passive infrared     detects movement of a warm body; misses stationary people
  ultrasonic           distance; wide beam, affected by soft surfaces
  time of flight       distance; narrow, fast, more expensive
  capacitive           proximity and touch through a surface; sensitive to
                       humidity and to grounding
  pressure and force   requires the person to be in a place
  break beam           reliable and binary; alignment sensitive
  camera plus vision   the most flexible and the most failure-prone in variable
                       lighting
  servo                position feedback, limited range, audible, duty cycle
                       limited
  stepper              open loop positioning; loses steps silently under overload
  DC motor             speed control; needs an encoder for position
  solenoid             discrete actuation; heats rapidly and must be duty limited
  the duty cycle question  what happens after eight hours a day for three months
  debouncing           mechanical contacts bounce; required in hardware or
                       software
  power budget         actuators dominate; size the supply for the simultaneous
                       worst case rather than the average
  isolation            protect the controller from the actuator's electrical noise
                       and from mains voltage
  fail safe            what happens on power loss, on a crash, and when a sensor
                       disconnects; define it rather than discovering it

The duty cycle question is worth asking early because it eliminates otherwise attractive
components. A hobby servo holding a position under load draws current continuously and overheats
within hours. A solenoid energized for more than a short fraction of the time burns out. A bright
LED at full current without adequate heatsinking degrades visibly over weeks. An installation runs
far longer than a prototype, and components adequate for a demonstration frequently are not
adequate for a run, which is discovered mid-exhibition when the piece stops working and nobody is
on site.

[assistant] Structured light depth cameras project an infrared pattern and read its deformation,
and the gallery is flooding that with ambient infrared. Two sources compound.

Daylight contains a great deal of infrared, and halogen lamps emit far more infrared than visible
light — most of their power is infrared. Both raise the noise floor relative to the projected
pattern, so the sensor cannot resolve it reliably. Your studio's controlled indoor lighting has
comparatively little.

Your polished concrete floor is the second problem and is independent. Structured light needs a
diffuse surface to scatter the pattern back, and a specular surface reflects it away from the
sensor, producing depth dropouts that look like erratic tracking rather than like missing data.

Four things, roughly in order of practicality.

Change the sensor technology if you can. Time-of-flight cameras handle ambient infrared far
better because they modulate the emission and demodulate the return, which rejects uncorrelated
light. Stereo depth with an active pattern degrades more gracefully than structured light because
it can fall back on natural texture. This is the durable fix.

Control the light. Shading the tracked area from daylight, and replacing halogen with LED sources
which emit far less infrared, both raise the signal-to-noise ratio substantially. Gallery lighting
is usually negotiable if you ask early, and it is not negotiable the day before opening.

Treat the floor. A matte finish — a temporary vinyl, a matte sealant, or a rug within the tracked
area — restores diffuse reflection. This is cheap and it often resolves half the problem on its
own.

And recalibrate on site rather than relying on studio calibration. Depth cameras drift with
temperature and the geometry differs, and the tracking thresholds tuned in one environment are
rarely right in another. Build the calibration into the piece as an accessible mode rather than
as a rebuild, because you will need it again.

One structural recommendation: assume the sensing will be worse than in the studio and design the
interaction to degrade gracefully. A piece that requires precise tracking fails visibly when
tracking degrades; a piece that uses coarse presence and approximate position remains coherent.
Building tolerance into the interaction design is more reliable than achieving reliability in the
sensing.

## Vision, Sound, and Space

Computer vision for interaction has different requirements from vision for analysis: it must run
in real time, must be robust to lighting the artist does not control, and must degrade
predictably rather than failing outright. This favours simpler and more robust techniques over
more accurate ones, and background subtraction with adaptive updating remains a workhorse
precisely because it is fast and its failure modes are legible.

The lighting is the dominant variable and the one most often underestimated. Changing daylight
through a gallery window alters the scene continuously; a visitor's shadow is detected as an
object; a reflective floor produces phantom detections; and infrared illumination gives
independence from visible lighting at the cost of requiring an infrared-sensitive camera and
appropriate illuminators. Controlling the lighting is nearly always cheaper than making the
vision robust to arbitrary lighting.

Spatial audio in installation is a different problem from spatial audio in a fixed listening
position, because the audience moves. A stereo image collapses as soon as a listener leaves the
sweet spot, so installation sound uses either many speakers distributed through the space, with
the localization arising from proximity, or wave field synthesis and ambisonics rendered to a
speaker array, or directional speakers producing a beam audible only within it.

Vision, sound, and space:
  background subtraction  fast, robust, and requires adaptive updating for
                       lighting change
  frame differencing   detects motion; ignores stationary people
  blob tracking        simple and adequate for many interactions
  optical flow         motion field; more information, more computation
  pose estimation      skeleton tracking; expressive and dependent on training
                       distribution
  infrared illumination  independence from visible lighting; requires an IR-capable
                       camera and illuminators
  control the lighting  cheaper than making vision robust to arbitrary lighting
  shadows              detected as objects; a persistent source of false positives
  the sweet spot problem  stereo collapses away from the listening position
  distributed speakers  localization by proximity; robust in a moving audience
  ambisonics           a scene representation decoded to any array; rotatable
  directional speakers  an audible beam; creates private zones in a shared space
  acoustic treatment   a reverberant gallery destroys detail and intelligibility;
                       absorption is frequently the highest-value intervention
  sound spill          adjacent works interfering; the most common installation
                       conflict, and best resolved at the layout stage

Sound spill deserves the practical emphasis because it causes more conflict between works in a
shared exhibition than any other factor, and it is far cheaper to address in the floor plan than
afterward. Low frequencies travel through structure and around barriers and cannot be contained by
anything a gallery will install, so a piece with substantial bass content will be heard
throughout the space. Discussing the acoustic requirements at the layout stage — which works need
quiet, which produce sound, and where they can be separated — is the intervention, and it requires
raising the question before the layout is fixed.

## Exhibition Practice

An installation must run unattended, and this requirement shapes the engineering more than any
aesthetic consideration. The piece will be switched on by a gallery attendant who knows nothing
about it, will run for eight to twelve hours, will be switched off, and will repeat for weeks or
months. Anything requiring intervention will not receive it.

Automatic startup is therefore the first requirement: the piece must reach its running state from
a cold power-on with no login, no dialogue box, no manual launch, and no dependence on a network
that may be down. Automatic recovery is the second: a crash must be detected and the piece
restarted, which means a watchdog and a supervisor process rather than trusting the software not
to crash. And a scheduled restart during closed hours resolves accumulated memory leaks and drift
without anyone noticing.

Physical robustness is the requirement that separates a gallery piece from a studio prototype.
Cables will be walked on and pulled. Visitors will touch, lean on, and occasionally strike the
work. Children will do things nobody anticipated. Anything reachable will be reached. Strain
relief, secured connections, enclosed electronics, and rounded edges are not polish but
functional requirements.

Exhibition practice:
  automatic startup    from cold power-on with no interaction
  no dialogue boxes    disable updates, notifications, screensavers, and power
                       management
  watchdog             detect a hang and restart; a supervisor process restarts a
                       crash
  scheduled restart    during closed hours; resolves leaks and drift
  remote monitoring    know it has failed before the gallery tells you
  logging              enough to diagnose remotely; a failure you cannot
                       reconstruct will recur
  physical robustness  cables walked on, works leaned on, everything touched
  strain relief        on every cable; the most common physical failure
  secured connections  locking connectors or mechanical retention; friction fits
                       work loose
  spare parts          on site, with instructions a non-specialist can follow
  documentation        a one-page start and stop procedure for gallery staff
  attendant briefing   what it does, what normal looks like, what to do if it
                       stops, and whom to call
  power sequencing     if components must start in an order, enforce it in
                       hardware rather than in a procedure
  fail visible or fail dark  decide which; a piece failing into a diagnostic
                       screen is worse than one failing into darkness
  environmental        temperature, humidity, dust, and continuous operation
                       degrade equipment that a studio never stresses

The last consideration deserves the closing word: decide what failure looks like. A piece that
crashes to a desktop, a driver error, or a diagnostic overlay is worse than one that goes dark,
because the failure becomes visible as a technical failure rather than as an absence. Configuring
the system so that any unhandled failure results in a black screen, and building an explicit
attract or idle state that is visually complete, means that the audience encounters either the
work or nothing — which preserves the piece's integrity when the inevitable failure occurs
unattended on a Sunday afternoon.
