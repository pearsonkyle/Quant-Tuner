## Letterforms and Type Anatomy

A typeface is a designed system of letterforms, and its consistency comes from shared proportions
and shared construction rather than from letters resembling each other. The relevant measurements
are the x-height, the height of lowercase letters without ascenders; the cap height; the ascender and
descender extents; and the relationship among them, which determines how large a typeface appears at
a given nominal size far more than the nominal size does.

This is why two typefaces set at the same point size can look very different in scale. Point size
measures the em, an abstract box, not any visible feature, so a typeface with a large x-height
relative to its em appears larger and generally reads better at small sizes. Comparing typefaces
requires setting them to matching x-heights rather than to matching point sizes, which is a
practical adjustment that most software does not make automatically.

Stroke construction distinguishes the broad categories. Serif faces have terminating strokes whose
form and contrast reflect their historical origins in written and engraved letterforms. Sans serif
faces omit them and vary in whether their skeleton is geometric, humanist, or neutral. Contrast —
the difference between thick and thin strokes — is the single most consequential characteristic for
legibility at small sizes and for behaviour at large ones, since high contrast is elegant in display
and disappears in body text at low resolution.

Q: Two typefaces are set at the same point size and one appears noticeably larger. Why?
A. One file is scaled incorrectly
B. Point size measures the em box rather than any drawn feature, so a typeface whose x-height is a
   larger fraction of its em appears larger; matching apparent size requires matching x-heights, not
   point sizes
C. The rendering differs
D. One has a larger cap height
Reasoning: The em is an abstract unit that the designer fills as they choose. X-height relative to
em varies widely across typefaces, and since most of the text a reader sees is lowercase, x-height
dominates perceived size. This is why substituting one typeface for another at the same size
frequently requires a size adjustment. Answer: B.

## Font Technology and Rendering

A font file contains outlines, metrics, and tables describing behaviour. The outlines are described
by curves — quadratic or cubic depending on the format — that a rasterizer fills. Metrics give each
glyph's advance width and the spacing adjustments between specific pairs. Feature tables describe
substitutions and positioning that the layout engine applies, which is what makes ligatures,
contextual alternates, and complex script shaping possible.

Rendering converts outlines to pixels, and at typical text sizes the stem widths are on the order of
one or two pixels, so the fit between the outline and the pixel grid dominates the result. Hinting
is instruction embedded in the font that adjusts the outline at small sizes to align features with
the grid, producing crisp consistent stems at the cost of distorting the design. Modern
high-resolution displays reduce the need for it substantially, and rendering stacks have moved
toward respecting the outline and relying on antialiasing.

Antialiasing uses intermediate grey values to represent partial coverage, which the eye integrates
into a smoother shape. Subpixel rendering goes further by addressing the individual colour elements
of an LCD, tripling the effective horizontal resolution at the cost of colour fringing and
dependence on the display's subpixel arrangement, which is why it is applied less than it once was.
Gamma handling determines whether antialiased text appears the correct weight, and incorrect gamma
is why the same text can look too thin on one system and too heavy on another.

## Text Layout and Typesetting

Layout arranges glyphs into lines and lines into blocks, and a small number of parameters carry most
of the readability. Line length is the most important: too short and the eye jumps back constantly,
too long and it loses the return position, with the comfortable range being roughly a alphabet and a
half to two and a half alphabets of characters. Line spacing must increase with line length, since a
longer line needs more vertical separation for the eye to find the next one reliably.

Justification distributes extra space to make lines flush at both edges, and doing it well requires
more than adding space between words. A greedy line-breaking algorithm that fills each line as much
as possible produces uneven spacing and rivers of white running down the block. A paragraph-level
algorithm that minimizes total badness across all lines produces markedly better results by
accepting a worse line here to enable a better one there, and it is available in typesetting systems
and generally not in web browsers.

Hyphenation reduces the spacing variation that justification requires, and it needs language-specific
rules or dictionaries since hyphenation points depend on morphology. Without it, justified text in a
narrow column has spacing so uneven that ragged-right setting is usually better. The practical
guidance is that justification without good hyphenation and good line breaking is worse than not
justifying at all.

## Internationalization and Complex Scripts

Text rendering beyond simple left-to-right alphabetic scripts requires capability that many systems
lack. Bidirectional text mixes right-to-left and left-to-right runs in one paragraph, and resolving
the display order requires an algorithm operating on character properties and embedding levels
rather than on simple reversal. Getting it wrong produces text that is not merely ugly but reordered
into a different meaning.

Complex scripts require shaping: the glyph drawn depends on context. Arabic letters have different
forms initially, medially, finally, and in isolation. Indic scripts form conjunct consonants and
reposition vowel marks around a cluster in ways that do not correspond to the storage order.
Handling these correctly requires a shaping engine and a font with the relevant feature tables, and
substituting a font without them produces text that is readable to nobody.

The unit distinctions matter for any text processing. A code point is one entry in the character
encoding. A grapheme cluster is what a user perceives as a character, which may be several code
points combined. A glyph is what is drawn, which may be one for several code points or several for
one. Cursor movement, selection, and character counting must operate on grapheme clusters, and code
that operates on code points or on encoded units produces cursors that land inside a character and
counts that surprise users.

## Variable Fonts and Practical Selection

A variable font contains a design space rather than a fixed set of instances, with axes along which
the outlines interpolate continuously. Weight and width are standard axes; optical size adjusts the
design for the intended rendering size, which restores a practice that predates digital type; and
custom axes can control anything the designer parameterizes. A single file replaces a family of
static instances, and any point in the space is available rather than only the named ones.

The practical benefits are file size for a full family, fine-grained control, and the ability to
animate between states. The costs are that some rendering paths handle variation poorly, that the
design space's corners may not be places the designer intended anyone to use, and that arbitrary
interpolated instances lack the individual attention that named weights receive. The optical size
axis is the one with the clearest quality benefit and it is the least used.

Selecting type for a project comes down to a few practical questions rather than to aesthetic
preference alone. What sizes and what rendering environment — a face designed for print at large
sizes fails as small screen text. What character coverage is needed — a beautiful face lacking the
scripts, diacritics, or numerals a project requires is unusable. What weights and widths are needed,
and does the family actually have them. And what the licensing permits, since font licences vary
widely in whether they cover web embedding, application bundling, and the volume of use anticipated.
