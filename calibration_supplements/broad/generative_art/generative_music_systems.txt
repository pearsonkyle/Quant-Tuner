## Rule-Based and Deterministic Composition

Algorithmic composition specifies a process that produces music rather than specifying the music
itself, and the composer's work moves from writing notes to designing the process and choosing its
parameters. This shift is older than computers — canon, isorhythm, and serial technique are all
processes producing music from rules — and computation made the space of expressible processes much
larger.

Deterministic processes produce the same output every run, which makes them repeatable and
inspectable. Cellular automata evolve a grid of states by local rules and map the resulting patterns
to pitch and rhythm, producing structures that are neither random nor predictable. L-systems rewrite
symbols recursively according to production rules and generate self-similar structures well suited to
hierarchical musical form. Iterated function systems and fractal mappings produce material with
statistical self-similarity across timescales, which resembles a property that has been measured in
composed music.

The central design problem in all of these is the mapping from the generative structure to musical
parameters. The process produces numbers; the mapping decides whether those numbers become pitches
in a scale, rhythmic durations, dynamics, timbral parameters, or structural boundaries. Two composers
using an identical generator and different mappings produce entirely unrelated music, which means
the mapping rather than the algorithm is where most of the aesthetic decisions live.

  determinism          repeatable and inspectable output

  L-systems            recursive symbol rewriting; hierarchical self-similar form
  fractal mappings     statistical self-similarity across timescales

                       approach that expresses voice-leading rules directly

Q: Two composers implement the same cellular automaton and produce completely different-sounding
pieces. This is because:
A. One implementation is incorrect
B. The generator produces abstract state sequences; the mapping from those states to pitch, rhythm,
   dynamics, and timbre is a separate set of decisions and is where most of the musical character
   is determined
C. Cellular automata are random
D. The parameters differ
Reasoning: An algorithmic generator is a source of structured sequences with no inherent musical
meaning. The mapping layer assigns that meaning, and it involves scale choice, rhythmic
interpretation, register, orchestration, and formal segmentation. Identical generators with
different mappings are effectively different instruments. Answer: B.

## Stochastic Methods and Grammars

Introducing randomness makes each realization different while preserving statistical character, and
the design question becomes what to constrain and what to leave free. Uniform random choice produces
music that sounds arbitrary because it has no structure at any timescale. Weighted choice from a
distribution shaped by musical considerations produces material with recognizable character while
remaining unpredictable in detail.

Markov models are the standard tool. A first-order model chooses the next note based on the current
one; higher orders condition on more history. The tradeoff is sharp and instructive: low orders
produce material that is locally plausible and globally aimless, while high orders trained on a
corpus increasingly reproduce fragments of the training material verbatim. The interesting range is
narrow, and the model's order is effectively a dial between incoherence and plagiarism.

Grammar-based approaches address the structural weakness directly by generating hierarchically. A
grammar expands a start symbol into a phrase structure, then into subphrases, then into notes, so
the output has organized structure at every level by construction rather than by chance. This is
what Markov chains fundamentally cannot do, since their memory extends only as far as their order,
and combining a grammar for structure with a stochastic model for surface detail is a common and
effective architecture.

Stochastic and grammatical methods:
  uniform randomness   arbitrary; no structure at any scale
  weighted distributions  character preserved, detail unpredictable
  Markov models        next event conditioned on recent history
  first order          locally plausible, globally aimless
  high order           increasingly verbatim reproduction of training material
  the narrow useful range  order as a dial between incoherence and copying
  variable order models  adapting context length to the available statistics
  the structural weakness  no memory beyond the order; no long-range form
  generative grammars  hierarchical expansion from a start symbol
  structure by construction  organized at every level rather than by chance
  the effective combination  grammar for form, stochastic model for surface
  probabilistic grammars  weighted production rules
  constrained randomness  random within a set of hard rules; the practical
                       compromise most systems use
  seeding              a reproducible random sequence, so a good realization can
                       be recovered rather than lost, which matters more in
                       practice than it sounds

## Live Coding and Real-Time Systems

Live coding performs music by writing and modifying code while it runs, with the audience typically
seeing the code projected. It requires a system where the program can be changed without stopping the
sound, which is a specific technical demand: state must persist across redefinitions, and changes
must take effect at musically sensible moments rather than immediately.

Temporal recursion is the pattern most live coding environments are built on. A function schedules
its own next invocation at a future time, so the schedule is expressed in the code itself and
redefining the function changes what happens from the next invocation onward. Combined with a
quantization scheme that aligns changes to the beat or the bar, this produces a system where edits
take effect musically rather than abruptly.

Pattern languages provide a higher-level layer, expressing repeating structures as concise notation
that can be transformed by operators — reversal, rotation, stretching, layering, conditional
substitution. This is what makes live coding practical as a performance activity, since typing note
lists in real time is not, and the expressiveness of the pattern language substantially determines
what a performer can reach for under time pressure.

Live coding and real time:
  code as the instrument  the program modified while it runs
  the technical demand  state persists across redefinition

  pattern languages    concise notation for repeating structure

  the performance constraint  what can be reached for under time pressure
  latency              scheduling ahead of the audio output to absorb jitter
  the scheduling principle  compute early, sound on time
  audio thread discipline  no allocation, no locks, no unbounded work in the

  the projected screen  the code as part of the performance; a convention with

  failure in performance  a syntax error silences nothing if the previous

                       property rather than an accident

## Machine Learning for Music

Learned models generate music by fitting a distribution to a corpus and sampling from it, and the
representation choice determines what the model can learn. Symbolic representations — note events
with pitch, timing, duration, and velocity — are compact and let the model learn musical structure
directly, at the cost of discarding everything about performance and timbre. Audio representations
capture everything and require the model to learn signal structure before it can learn musical
structure, which is a far larger task.

Sequence models over symbolic music learn local style convincingly and struggle with long-range
structure for the same reason Markov models do, though at a much longer horizon. A generated passage
sounds stylistically right phrase by phrase and frequently lacks the sense of an arc — a beginning,
a development, a destination — that composed music has. Conditioning on an explicit structural plan,
or generating hierarchically, are the approaches that address this.

Control is the practical requirement that distinguishes a useful tool from a demonstration. A
generator producing plausible music with no way to steer it is not usable in a creative workflow.
Conditioning on chord progressions, on a melody to harmonize, on a style descriptor, on a target
instrumentation, or on a partially completed score is what makes these systems tools, and
infilling — generating material to fit between fixed surrounding context — is the interaction that
composers reach for most.

  symbolic representation  note events; compact, structurally learnable
  what it discards     performance nuance and timbre
  audio representation  everything, and signal structure must be learned first
  piano roll and event sequences  the two common symbolic encodings
  sequence models      convincing local style
  the long-range weakness  stylistically right phrases without an overall arc
  hierarchical generation  structure planned before surface
  conditioning         chords, melody, style, instrumentation, partial score

## Interactive Systems and Practice

An interactive music system responds to a performer, and the design question is what relationship it
embodies. An instrument responds deterministically to control, so the performer is fully
responsible. A partner responds with its own agency, contributing material the performer did not
determine. A mirror analyses and reflects the performer's material transformed. Each demands a
different interaction design and a different rehearsal practice, and confusion about which is
intended produces systems that satisfy nobody.

The analysis side determines what the system can respond to. Onset detection finds note beginnings.
Pitch tracking follows a monophonic line reliably and a polyphonic texture unreliably. Beat tracking
locates the pulse and is difficult for rubato and for music without a steady beat. Higher-level
analysis — key, harmony, phrase boundaries, gesture — is progressively less reliable, and a system
depending on a fragile analysis fails in performance in ways that are hard to recover from.

The practical wisdom in this area is that latency and reliability matter far more than
sophistication. A simple system responding in a few milliseconds feels alive; a sophisticated one
responding in two hundred feels dead, regardless of the quality of its response. Musicians detect
timing deviations at scales far below conscious perception, and any system in a performance chain
must treat its latency budget the way a real-time engineer would rather than the way an application
developer would.
