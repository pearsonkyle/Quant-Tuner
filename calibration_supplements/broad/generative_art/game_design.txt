## Mechanics, Dynamics, and Systems

A game is a system of rules producing experiences, and design is the practice of choosing rules
whose emergent behaviour is worth engaging with. The useful decomposition separates mechanics — the
rules as written — from dynamics, the behaviour that emerges when players interact with those
rules, from the aesthetic experience that results. Designers author mechanics directly and the
other two only indirectly, which is why playtesting is not optional: the dynamics of a rule set are
not reliably predictable from reading it.

A core loop is the repeated activity a game is built around, and identifying it is the first design
decision. Explore, fight, loot, upgrade. Observe, hypothesize, test. Plant, wait, harvest, expand.
The loop must be satisfying at the scale of seconds to minutes because it is what the player does
thousands of times, and no amount of content quality compensates for a core loop that is not
enjoyable in isolation.

Systems earn their place by interacting. A mechanic that combines with others multiplies the design
space; a mechanic that stands alone adds only itself. This is why designers favour a small number of
deeply interacting systems over a large number of isolated ones — the former generates situations
the designer never explicitly authored, which is the source of the moments players remember and
describe to others.

Mechanics and systems:
  mechanics            the rules as written; what the designer authors directly
  dynamics             emergent behaviour from play; authored only indirectly
  aesthetics           the resulting experience
  core loop            the repeated activity; must be satisfying in isolation
  loop scales          seconds, minutes, sessions, whole playthroughs; each needs
                       its own reward
  interacting systems  combinations multiply the design space
  isolated mechanics   add only themselves; a cost with limited return
  emergence            situations the designer did not author
  meaningful choice    options with different, knowable, consequential outcomes
  false choice         options that differ cosmetically or where one dominates
  dominant strategy    a single best option; collapses the decision space and is
                       the most common systemic design failure
  the verb list        what the player can actually do; a short list executed well
                       beats a long one

Q: A strategy game has twelve unit types but experienced players use three. This indicates:
A. The players are unimaginative
B. The other nine are dominated — worse than an alternative in essentially all situations — so
   the effective decision space is much smaller than the nominal one
C. The game needs more units
D. The tutorial is inadequate
Reasoning: When options go unused by skilled players, the cause is almost always that they are
strictly or near-strictly dominated. The fix is to give each unit a situation where it is the best
answer, which usually means differentiating along more axes than raw effectiveness. Adding more
units to a dominated set makes the problem worse. Answer: B.

## Feedback, Difficulty, and Flow

Every player action needs a response, and the quality of that response is most of what makes a game
feel good independently of its systems. Immediate feedback confirms the input registered — the
animation, the sound, the screen shake, the particle. Delayed feedback communicates the
consequence. A game with excellent systems and poor feedback feels unresponsive and imprecise; a
game with mediocre systems and superb feedback often feels better than it has any right to.

Difficulty is a relationship between challenge and player skill, and the target is the band where
challenge slightly exceeds current competence. Below it the player is bored, above it frustrated,
and within it engaged. Since skill grows during play, the challenge must grow too, which is what a
difficulty curve is. The curve should not be monotonic: periods of comfortable mastery after a
difficult section let the player feel the growth, and a curve that only ever climbs is exhausting
regardless of its slope.

Failure design deserves as much attention as success design, because players fail far more often
than they succeed at the moments that matter. The failure should be legible — the player must
understand what went wrong and what to do differently — and the cost of retrying should be
proportionate. A long unskippable sequence between attempts converts a challenging encounter into a
tedious one without changing its difficulty at all, and that distinction is one designers
frequently miss.

Feedback and difficulty:
  immediate feedback   confirms the input; sound, animation, impact
  consequence feedback  communicates the outcome
  game feel            responsiveness, weight, impact; largely independent of
                       systems
  input latency        the invisible determinant; a few frames is perceptible
  coyote time          brief input forgiveness; makes controls feel fair without
                       being noticed
  input buffering      accepting an input slightly early; the same purpose
  difficulty curve     rising with skill, with plateaus for mastery
  flow band            challenge slightly above current skill
  legible failure      the player understands the cause
  retry cost           should be proportionate; long resets create tedium not
                       difficulty
  checkpoint placement  before the challenge, not before the corridor leading to
                       it
  difficulty options   accessibility and audience breadth; separate the challenge
                       from the experience where possible
  dynamic difficulty   adjusts silently to performance; effective and resented
                       when detected

## Level, Encounter, and Economy Design

A level teaches, tests, and varies. The standard structure introduces a mechanic in a safe
context, combines it with a previously learned one, then presents a situation requiring both under
pressure. This progression is visible in well-designed games once you look for it, and its absence
produces levels that feel arbitrary — a sequence of challenges rather than a developing argument.

Spatial design guides without instructing. Sightlines direct attention toward objectives; lighting
and colour draw the eye to interactive elements; the width of a path signals whether it is the main
route or a detour. These techniques work because they operate below deliberate attention, and their
failure mode is a player who is lost while the designer cannot understand why, since the designer
knows where everything is.

Economies govern the flow of resources and they are the systems most likely to break in ways that
are invisible until late. The relevant quantities are sources, sinks, and the rate of each. An
economy with insufficient sinks inflates until currency is meaningless; one with insufficient
sources starves and creates grinding. Progression systems are economies where the currency is
capability, and the same analysis applies: a progression that grants power faster than the
challenge grows trivializes the game, and the reverse creates a wall.

Level and economy:
  teach, test, vary    the standard level progression
  safe introduction    a mechanic first presented without failure cost
  combination          old mechanic plus new; where the design earns its depth
  environmental storytelling  the space communicating history without text
  sightlines           directing attention spatially
  affordance signalling  what is climbable, breakable, interactive; consistency
                       matters more than realism
  the designer's blind spot  you know the space; you cannot experience being lost
                       in it
  pacing               alternating intensity; sustained peak reads as flat
  encounter design     enemy composition creating a problem, not a quantity
  sources and sinks    the two halves of any economy; measure both rates
  inflation            insufficient sinks; currency loses meaning
  grinding             insufficient sources relative to costs
  progression as economy  capability is the currency; the same analysis applies
  power versus challenge  the ratio determines whether growth feels earned
  the numbers spreadsheet  economies are designed in a spreadsheet before they
                       are implemented, and simulating a thousand hypothetical
                       players through the curve catches problems that months of
                       playtesting would not reach

## Narrative and Player Agency

Narrative in games differs from narrative in linear media because the audience acts. The tension is
structural: a story has an authored shape, and player agency threatens that shape. Every approach to
game narrative is a position on how to resolve that tension, and there is no resolution that is
correct in general.

Environmental and systemic storytelling avoid the tension by embedding narrative in the world and
in the mechanics rather than in scripted sequences. A room's arrangement tells what happened there.
A resource scarcity mechanic communicates desperation more effectively than dialogue about
desperation. This approach scales well and it constrains what can be told — precise plot events are
difficult to convey this way.

Branching narrative grants agency at a cost that grows combinatorially. Every meaningful branch
doubles the content required, which is why most branching structures rejoin quickly and why the
apparent breadth of choice usually exceeds the actual. The honest designs make the branches short
and the consequences local, or they let choices affect state rather than plot — the story reaches
the same place, and who is present and how they regard you has changed.

Narrative approaches:
  the core tension     authored shape versus player agency
  environmental storytelling  narrative in the space
  systemic storytelling  narrative in the mechanics
  ludonarrative dissonance  mechanics contradicting story; a character described
                       as reluctant who kills hundreds
  branching            agency at combinatorial content cost
  the rejoin           branches converging; the standard economic compromise
  state versus plot    choices changing who and how rather than what happens
  the illusion of choice  presenting options with converging outcomes; effective
                       until noticed, and corrosive afterward
  emergent narrative   stories arising from systems; unrepeatable and the most
                       shared
  pacing control       cutscenes control pacing precisely and remove agency; the
                       tradeoff should be deliberate
  player character     a blank vessel invites projection, a defined character
                       enables specific storytelling; pick one and commit

## Playtesting and Technical Reality

Playtesting is the only reliable source of information about dynamics, and the discipline is in how
it is conducted rather than in doing it at all. Watch without helping. The instinct to explain is
strong and it destroys the data, because the question is what happens without you there. Record
where players hesitate, what they try, what they say aloud, and where they stop. Their explanations
of their own behaviour are less reliable than the behaviour itself.

Test with the right people at the right stage. Early tests need players who tolerate rough builds
and can articulate structural problems. Later tests need players resembling the actual audience,
seeing the game fresh, and the single most valuable resource in later development is a player who
has never seen it — a resource that is consumed on first use and cannot be regenerated.

Technical constraints shape design more than designers usually admit. The frame budget determines
how many entities can be simulated, which determines encounter scale. Memory determines level size
and streaming strategy. Network latency determines what can be authoritative on the client, which
determines how responsive a competitive game can feel. A design that ignores these constraints gets
rebuilt around them later, at a cost far higher than designing within them from the start.

Playtesting and constraints:
  watch, do not help   the instinct to explain destroys the data
  observed behaviour   more reliable than reported reasoning
  where they hesitate  the signal for a comprehension failure
  where they stop      the signal for a motivation failure
  fresh players        a consumable, non-renewable resource
  audience matching    later tests need the actual audience
  the frame budget     determines simulated entity count and encounter scale
  memory and streaming  determines level size and structure
  network authority    server-authoritative is fair, client-prediction is
                       responsive; competitive games need both and reconcile them
  latency compensation  rollback and prediction; the design must tolerate
                       corrections
  determinism          required for replays, lockstep networking, and reliable
                       reproduction of bugs; floating-point differences across
                       platforms break it in ways that are painful to diagnose
  scope                the constraint that ends more projects than any technical
                       one; cutting features is a design skill, and a smaller game
                       finished well is categorically better than a larger one
                       abandoned
