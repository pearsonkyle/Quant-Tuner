## Generative Practice

Generative art is work whose form is determined in part by an autonomous system the
artist designs but does not fully specify. The artist writes rules; the rules produce
outputs; the artist judges, revises, and re-runs. What distinguishes it from procedural
production is that the artist cedes real authority to the system — if every output could
have been predicted, the system is a drawing tool rather than a collaborator.

The central practical skill is not writing the algorithm but tuning the space of outputs.
A generative system defines a distribution over possible pieces, and the work of making
it good is narrowing that distribution so that samples are reliably interesting. A system
whose outputs are all nearly identical has too little variance and reads as a template
with noise applied. A system whose outputs range from striking to incoherent has too
much, and hand-picking the good ones means the artist rather than the system is doing the
composition. Between those failures lies the craft.

Parameter ranges are where most of that tuning happens, and the discipline is to
constrain rather than to filter. If a colour parameter sometimes produces an ugly
combination, the answer is to restrict the range, or to sample from a curated palette,
or to enforce a relationship between hues — not to generate freely and discard. Every
constraint encoded in the system is a decision made once that applies to every future
output; every rejection made by hand is a decision that must be remade forever and that
does not transfer when the piece becomes a long-form series.

Practice principles:
  constrain, do not filter    encode taste into the sampling, not into rejection
  seed everything             a piece you cannot reproduce is a piece you cannot
                              refine; log the seed with every output
  vary one axis at a time     grid renders across a single parameter reveal what
                              that parameter actually controls
  build a contact sheet       judge a system by a hundred outputs at thumbnail
                              size, never by one at full size
  separate composition        structure, palette, and texture as independent
                              layers so each can be tuned without disturbing the
                              others
  keep the failures           the interesting bugs are the main source of new
                              directions; screenshot before fixing
  fix the output size late    resolution independence forces you to express
                              everything in relative units, which is good hygiene

The contact sheet is the single most useful habit. A generative system is a distribution,
and judging a distribution from one sample is a category error. Rendering a hundred
outputs at small size shows immediately whether the variety is real, whether some region
of parameter space produces failures, and whether the pieces are recognizably from one
family — which is what makes a series cohere. Most systems look far better in a single
cherry-picked render than they do across a hundred, and the gap between those two
impressions is the work remaining.

## Randomness and Its Control

Randomness in generative work is a material with texture, and different sources have
different character. Uniform random is flat and structureless, producing clumps and gaps
that read as noise rather than as distribution. Gaussian random clusters around a mean
and is what you want for natural variation around an intended value. Perlin and simplex
noise are spatially coherent, so nearby points get similar values, which is what makes
them read as organic rather than as static. Blue noise is uniformly distributed but with
no two points close together, producing the even scattering that looks deliberately
placed — the reason it is used for stippling, sampling, and dithering.

The distinction between white noise and blue noise is worth dwelling on because it
explains a common disappointment. Scattering points with uniform random placement
produces visible clumps and voids; the eye reads them as clustering with meaning that is
not there. Poisson disc sampling, or a relaxation process like Lloyd's algorithm applied
to a Voronoi diagram, enforces a minimum separation and produces the even-but-irregular
distribution that reads as natural. Almost every time "random placement" looks wrong, the
fix is a blue-noise distribution rather than more randomness.

Seeded pseudorandom generation is non-negotiable for serious practice. A piece produced
by an unseeded generator cannot be reproduced, refined, or printed at a different
resolution, and in long-form generative work — where a collector mints a token and the
piece is derived from it — determinism from the seed is the entire contract. This
requires care: the built-in random function in most environments cannot be seeded, so a
small explicit PRNG must be used, and every consumer of randomness must draw from that
one stream in a fixed order. Adding a new random draw in the middle of the code changes
every subsequent value and therefore every previously generated piece, which is why
mature systems draw all parameters up front from a fixed sequence.

Randomness techniques:
  seeded PRNG           sfc32, mulberry32, xorshift; small, fast, deterministic
  uniform               structureless; use for choices, not for placement
  gaussian              natural variation around an intended value
  weighted choice       curated options with probabilities; how rarity tiers work
  Perlin / simplex      spatially coherent; the basis of organic form
  blue noise            even irregular spacing; Poisson disc or Lloyd relaxation
  Levy flight           mostly small steps with rare large jumps; produces natural
                        foraging and exploration patterns
  Markov chain          state-dependent transitions; good for sequences and grammars
  jitter                small random offset applied to a regular structure; the
                        cheapest way to make a grid feel hand-made

Controlled randomness — where the random value is constrained by context — produces far
better results than free randomness. Sampling a colour from a palette rather than from
the full gamut, choosing an angle from multiples of fifteen degrees rather than from a
continuum, selecting a length from a small set of related values: each constraint makes
the output more coherent while retaining variety. This is the generative equivalent of a
design system, and systems that use it look designed while systems that sample freely
look computed.

## Algorithms of Form

A relatively small set of algorithms generates most of the vocabulary of the medium, and
knowing them as a repertoire matters more than knowing any one deeply.

Flow fields assign a direction to every point in space, typically from noise, and trace
particles along them. The result is the characteristic flowing filament imagery that
defines much of the field. Variations come from how the field is constructed — noise,
mathematical functions, image-derived gradients — from how particles are seeded and
terminated, and from how the traces are rendered. Curl noise produces a divergence-free
field, so particles neither converge nor spread, which reads as incompressible fluid.

Reaction-diffusion simulates two chemicals that react and spread at different rates,
producing spots, stripes, and labyrinthine patterns from a homogeneous start. The
Gray-Scott model is the standard formulation, and its parameter space contains sharply
distinct regimes — a small change in the feed and kill rates moves from stable spots to
growing mitosis to chaotic turbulence. It is a good example of a system whose interest
lies almost entirely in exploring a two-dimensional parameter space rather than in the
algorithm itself.

Cellular automata evolve a grid by local rules applied simultaneously. Conway's Game of
Life is the famous instance, but the more useful generative property is that
extraordinarily simple rules produce unbounded complexity — Rule 30 generates sequences
passing statistical randomness tests from a single seed cell. For visual work, continuous
and multi-state variants (SmoothLife, Lenia) produce organic forms that discrete
automata cannot.

Generative algorithm repertoire:
  flow field            trace particles along a noise-derived vector field
  reaction-diffusion    Gray-Scott; spots, stripes, labyrinths
  cellular automata     local rules, global complexity
  L-systems             string rewriting interpreted as turtle graphics; plants,
                        branching structures, space-filling curves
  differential growth   a curve that lengthens and repels itself; coral, cortex,
                        wrinkle patterns
  Voronoi / Delaunay    space partition from points; cells, cracking, adjacency
  circle packing        fill a region with non-overlapping circles of varying size
  physarum              agent-based slime mould simulation; emergent networks
  strange attractors    Lorenz, Clifford, De Jong; dense parametric point clouds
  Wave Function Collapse constraint propagation over tile adjacency; coherent
                        structured layouts from a small example
  Truchet tiles         a small tile set placed with random rotation; continuous
                        curves emerge from discrete pieces

Differential growth deserves particular mention because it produces forms that look
biological in a way few algorithms do. A closed polyline repeatedly inserts new points
where segments grow too long, and each point is pushed away from its near neighbours
while being pulled toward its immediate ones along the curve. The curve lengthens faster
than the space allows, so it buckles and folds, producing exactly the convoluted
boundaries seen in coral, in brain cortex, and in leaf margins — for the same underlying
reason, which is growth constrained by space.

Layering algorithms is where distinctive work usually comes from. A flow field whose
directions are derived from a reaction-diffusion pattern; a Voronoi partition whose seed
points come from circle packing; an L-system whose growth is deflected by a noise field.
The individual algorithms are widely known and produce recognizable results on their own;
composition is where an individual voice appears.

The field itself. Perlin noise mapped directly to angle is where everyone starts.
Alternatives that change the character completely: derive the field from the gradient of
an image, so the flow follows real structure; use curl noise for incompressible motion;
build the field from a sum of point sources and sinks so it has singularities that
particles spiral into; or make the field time-varying so traces from different moments
disagree.

Seeding. Uniform random seeding produces even coverage, which is why the output reads as
a texture rather than as a composition. Seed along a shape, seed with blue noise so
spacing is controlled, seed densely in one region and sparsely elsewhere, or seed from
the bright areas of a source image.

Termination. Constant lifetime is why every line is the same length. Terminate when a
particle enters a region, when it comes too close to an existing trace (which produces
the packed non-overlapping look), when its speed drops below a threshold, or
probabilistically so lengths vary.

Rendering. This is where the largest gain usually is and where the least attention
usually goes. Constant-width lines are the generic marker. Vary width with speed or with
distance travelled; render as tapering strokes; accumulate density into a buffer and map
it to colour so overlap is visible; draw with many short transparent segments so
crossings build up; or do not draw lines at all and instead deposit particles as points
or as textured stamps.

Pick two of those four to change, not all four — changing everything at once gives you a
different system you understand no better than this one. My suggestion for the largest
distinctiveness gain per unit effort: proximity-based termination plus speed-varying
stroke width. That combination alone produces the packed, organic, varied-weight look
that reads as considered rather than as generated.

## Physical Output and Plotters

Pen plotters have become central to the medium because they impose constraints that
improve the work. A plotter draws lines with a physical pen, so there is no fill, no
transparency, no anti-aliasing, and no colour blending — everything must be expressed as
strokes. That constraint forces decisions about mark-making that screen work allows you
to avoid, and it connects generative practice to the whole history of drawing.

Designing for a plotter means thinking in paths rather than pixels. Shading becomes
hatching, and the hatching angle, spacing, and variation become expressive choices.
Texture becomes stroke quality. Overlap becomes visible layering, since a pen crossing
its own line deposits more ink. Colour becomes discrete pen changes, so a palette is
literally a set of physical objects and the number of colours is bounded by patience.

The practical pipeline is SVG-centric. The generative program outputs SVG paths, those
paths are optimized — merged, ordered to minimize travel, deduplicated where lines
overlap — and then converted to G-code or sent to the plotter. Path optimization matters
more than it sounds: a naively ordered drawing spends most of its time with the pen up,
moving between unrelated segments, and reordering can cut plot time by more than half.

Plotter workflow considerations:
  output format        SVG with paths in real-world units; set width/height and
                       viewBox consistently or scaling surprises you
  line merging         join segments sharing endpoints into continuous polylines;
                       fewer pen lifts, cleaner joins
  travel optimization  greedy nearest-neighbour reordering is enough; 2-opt if the
                       drawing is large
  overdraw             repeated strokes darken; use deliberately, avoid accidentally
  pen choice           fineliners for detail, gel and rollerball for saturation,
                       fountain pens for line variation, brush pens for expression
  paper                heavier stock resists bleed and cockling; texture affects
                       line quality substantially
  registration         multi-colour work needs the paper to return to the same
                       position; tape or a jig, and plot all of one colour first
  bleed and dry time   dense hatching floods the paper; sequence regions so wet ink
                       is not revisited
  plot time            estimate before committing; complex pieces run for hours

The failure that catches everyone once is generating a drawing whose complexity is
invisible on screen and ruinous on paper. Ten thousand short segments look like a smooth
gradient at screen resolution and take six hours to plot while shredding the paper under
a repeatedly lifting pen. Estimating total path length and pen-lift count before plotting,
and previewing at true physical scale rather than fit-to-window, prevents this.

Beyond plotters, the same generative output feeds laser cutters, CNC routers, embroidery
machines, and 3D printers, each with its own constraint set that shapes the work.
Embroidery imposes stitch direction and density limits; laser cutting requires closed
paths and kerf compensation; 3D printing requires manifold geometry and overhang limits.
In every case the constraint is generative in itself — the requirement that geometry be
manifold, for instance, rules out whole families of form and pushes toward others.

## Animation, Timing, and Tooling

Motion in generative work is governed by the same principles as in traditional animation,
and easing is the most important. Linear interpolation between states reads as mechanical
because nothing in the physical world starts and stops instantaneously. Ease-in-out
functions — smoothstep being the simplest — give acceleration and deceleration that read
as physical. Overshoot and settle read as elastic. The choice of easing curve carries more
expressive weight than the motion path in most cases.

Frame-rate independence is a correctness requirement, not an optimization. Animation
driven by "add 2 to x each frame" runs at different speeds on different machines and
stutters when a frame is dropped. Driving motion by elapsed time — multiplying velocity
by delta time — makes speed consistent, and for deterministic output (essential for
rendering to video or for reproducible generative work) driving by a fixed logical
timestep independent of real time is better still.

Loops that read as seamless require the state at the end to match the state at the
beginning exactly. The reliable technique is to parameterize motion by an angle that
completes a full revolution over the loop, so every quantity is a periodic function of
that angle. For noise-driven animation, sampling noise along a circular path in a
higher-dimensional noise space gives a value that returns exactly to its start — a
four-dimensional noise sampled around a circle in two of its dimensions produces
seamlessly looping two-dimensional noise, which is the standard trick.

Tools and where each fits:
  p5.js / Processing   the teaching standard; immediate mode, huge community, ideal
                       for sketching and for learning
  openFrameworks       C++; performance, hardware access, installation work
  three.js             WebGL scene graph; 3D in the browser
  GLSL / shadertoy     pure fragment-shader work; maximum GPU throughput
  TouchDesigner        node-based, real-time, projection and installation
  Houdini              procedural 3D; the deepest node-based toolkit
  Blender + geometry nodes  procedural 3D, free, increasingly capable
  vsvg / vpype         SVG optimization for plotting
  Cairo / skia         high-quality 2D rasterization from any language
  numpy + PIL          when the piece is fundamentally array manipulation

Choosing a tool by what the piece needs rather than by familiarity is worth some
discomfort. Work that is fundamentally about per-pixel computation belongs in a fragment
shader and will be a hundred times faster there than in a CPU loop. Work that is about
manipulating a few thousand geometric primitives belongs in a vector environment. Work
that must run in a browser for an audience has different constraints from work that
renders overnight to a print file.

Finally, the curatorial layer is part of the practice. Long-form generative work — where
a single system produces hundreds of outputs that are all released — makes this explicit,
because the artist cannot select and every output must stand. The discipline this imposes
is valuable regardless of whether the work is released that way: build the system so that
its worst output is acceptable, rather than so that its best output is impressive.
Systems tuned to the best case have a long tail of failures; systems tuned to the worst
case tend to have a coherent identity across every sample, which is what makes a body of
work rather than a collection of images.
