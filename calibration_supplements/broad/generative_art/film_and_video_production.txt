## Capture, Codecs, and the Recording Chain

Video is a sequence of images with a sound track, and every technical decision in production is
some tradeoff among image quality, data rate, and workflow cost. The recording format sets the
terms of that tradeoff at the moment of capture, and it is the one decision that cannot be revisited
later — everything downstream can be redone, and the recorded file cannot.

Codecs divide into intraframe and interframe families with very different properties. An intraframe
codec compresses each frame independently, producing larger files that edit efficiently because any
frame can be decoded on its own. An interframe codec stores occasional complete frames and encodes
the rest as differences, which is far more efficient for a given quality and requires decoding a
sequence to reach any given frame. Delivery formats are interframe; acquisition and editing formats
should be intraframe, and using a delivery codec as an editing format is the most common cause of
sluggish editing and generational quality loss.

Chroma subsampling exploits the eye's lower spatial acuity for colour than for brightness by
recording colour information at reduced resolution. Full colour resolution is what compositing and
keying require, because a green screen key operates on colour edges that subsampling has blurred.
Half colour resolution is adequate for most direct delivery. Quarter is common in consumer formats
and produces visible artifacts on saturated edges and makes keying substantially harder.

Capture and codecs:
  the irreversible decision  the recorded file; everything downstream can be
                       redone
  intraframe codec     each frame independent; large files, efficient editing
  interframe codec     differences between frames; efficient delivery, awkward
                       editing
  the common mistake   editing directly in a delivery codec
  proxies              low-resolution stand-ins for editing, relinked to the
                       originals for finishing
  bit depth            levels per channel; eight bits banding in gradients, ten
                       or more for grading headroom
  chroma subsampling   colour recorded at reduced resolution
  full colour          required for keying and compositing
  the keying failure   subsampled colour edges make a clean key impossible
  bit rate             data per second; constant or variable
  log encoding         a curve preserving dynamic range for grading; looks flat
                       until graded and is not a finished image

Q: Footage shot in a log profile looks washed out and low contrast straight from the camera. This
means:
A. The camera was set up incorrectly
B. Log encoding deliberately compresses the dynamic range into the recording's code values to
   preserve highlight and shadow detail for grading; it is an intermediate representation, not a
   finished image, and applying the appropriate transform produces the intended look
C. The exposure was wrong
D. The sensor is faulty
Reasoning: Log curves trade apparent contrast for retained range. The flat appearance is the format
working as designed. It must be graded, or at minimum have a display transform applied, before it
looks correct. Answer: B.

## Colour Pipelines and Grading

Colour management in production means knowing, at every point in the chain, what the numbers in a
file mean. A pixel value has no inherent colour; it acquires one through a colour space definition
specifying primaries, white point, and transfer function. Files that travel between systems without
that information carried alongside them get interpreted with the receiving system's assumptions,
and mismatched assumptions are the cause of nearly every unexplained colour shift.

A managed pipeline transforms everything into a common working space, performs the creative work
there, and transforms out to each delivery target. The working space is chosen to be large enough
to contain all the source gamuts and to behave predictably under grading operations. The output
transform maps that working space to the specific display, and there is a different one for each
delivery target, which is why a grade approved on one display needs verification on the others
rather than assumption.

Grading proceeds in a conventional order for good reasons. Primary correction sets exposure,
contrast, and white balance for the whole image and establishes the baseline. Secondary correction
isolates regions or colour ranges for local adjustment. Shot matching makes cuts within a scene
consistent, which is the unglamorous majority of the work. The creative look is applied last, over a
foundation that is already neutral and consistent, because applying it first means every subsequent
correction fights it.

Colour pipelines:
  a pixel value has no inherent colour  meaning comes from a colour space
                       definition
  primaries, white point, transfer function  what defines a colour space
  metadata             what carries the interpretation between systems
  the mismatch symptom  unexplained shifts when files move between tools
  working space        large, predictable under grading operations
  input transform      source camera encoding into the working space
  output transform     working space to a specific display
  per-target transforms  a grade verified on one display is not verified on all
  primary correction   exposure, contrast, white balance for the whole image
  secondary correction  isolated regions or colour ranges
  shot matching        consistency across cuts; the bulk of the work
  the creative look    applied last, over a neutral foundation
  scopes               waveform and vectorscope; the eye adapts and the scopes do
                       not
  reference monitoring  a calibrated display in a controlled environment; grading
                       on an uncalibrated screen produces decisions that do not
                       transfer
  high dynamic range delivery  a wider range and a different transfer function;
                       a separate grading pass, not a conversion

## Camera, Lens, and Lighting Craft

Lens choice affects the image more than camera choice at any professional tier, because the camera
records what the lens delivers. Focal length determines field of view and, through the shooting
distance it implies, the apparent relationship between foreground and background. Maximum aperture
determines available depth of field and low-light capability. And the character of a lens — its
rendering of out-of-focus areas, its flare behaviour, its contrast, its distortion — is a creative
choice that no post-production process reproduces convincingly.

Lighting is where most of the visual quality is created, and the principles transfer directly from
still photography with the addition of continuity requirements. Motivated lighting places sources
where the scene's logic suggests them, which makes the image read as natural regardless of how
constructed it is. The key light establishes the form, fill controls the shadow density and
therefore the contrast, and separation from the background prevents the subject merging into it.

The practical constraints of moving-image lighting are different from still work in ways that
dominate the process. Sources must be positioned outside the frame for shots from multiple angles,
which is why grip equipment — stands, arms, flags, diffusion frames — represents most of the setup
effort. Continuity across a scene shot over hours requires that the lighting not change, which
conflicts with any use of daylight. And the actors move, so the lighting must work across their
whole path rather than at one position.

  focal length         field of view and implied shooting distance

  matched sets         lenses that share character across focal lengths
  focus pulling        maintaining focus on a moving subject; a skilled role
  motivated lighting   sources placed where the scene's logic suggests
  key, fill, separation  the functional decomposition
  contrast ratio       key to fill; the primary mood control
  colour temperature   mixing sources of different temperature is a deliberate
                       choice or an error, never an accident to ignore
  grip equipment       stands, arms, flags, frames; most of the setup effort
  the off-frame constraint  sources must be hidden from every angle to be shot

  the coverage question  what shots are needed to cut the scene, decided before
                       lighting rather than discovered during it

## Sound and Post-Production Workflow

Sound is half the experience and receives a small fraction of the attention on most productions. The
practical asymmetry is that audiences tolerate mediocre images with good sound far better than the
reverse, and that bad production sound is expensive or impossible to fix while a mediocre image can
often be improved in grading.

Production recording aims for clean dialogue with adequate level and minimal room reflection, which
means getting the microphone close, controlling the acoustics of the space, and recording room tone
for the editor to use in filling gaps. A boom overhead is the primary source because it sounds
natural; lavalier microphones provide backup and coverage where a boom cannot reach, and their
sound is closer and less natural. Recording separate tracks rather than a mix preserves the
editor's options.

Post-production sound builds the final track in layers: dialogue edited and cleaned, replacement
dialogue where production audio is unusable, sound effects both from libraries and recorded to
picture, ambience establishing the space, and music. The mix balances these for the intended
playback environment, and mixes made for a cinema and for a phone are genuinely different, which is
why delivery requires separate versions rather than one mix played louder or quieter.

Sound and workflow:
  the asymmetry        bad sound is less tolerated and harder to fix than a
                       mediocre image
  microphone proximity  the single largest factor in dialogue quality
  boom                 overhead and natural; the primary source
  lavalier             close, less natural; backup and coverage
  room tone            recorded on location for filling edits
  separate tracks      preserves the editor's options
  timecode sync        keeps separately recorded audio aligned with picture
  dialogue editing     cleaning, levelling, and removing distractions
  replacement dialogue  re-recorded to picture when production audio fails
  sound effects        library and recorded to picture
  ambience             establishes the space and covers edits
  the mix              balanced for a specific playback environment
  separate deliverables  a cinema mix and a mobile mix are different mixes
  loudness standards   broadcast and streaming specify integrated loudness; a mix
                       that ignores them is normalized by the platform in ways
                       the mixer did not choose
  the edit-first principle  picture is locked before the detailed sound work,
                       because every picture change invalidates sound editing
                       downstream of it

## Delivery, Archiving, and Practical Discipline

Delivery specifications differ by destination and they are exacting. Broadcast, theatrical,
streaming, and web each specify resolution, frame rate, codec, bit rate, colour space, audio
configuration, loudness, and often the exact structure of the file including leader, slates, and
caption formats. Reading the specification before finishing rather than after is the difference
between one delivery pass and several.

Archiving is where most productions fail, and the failure is only discovered years later when the
material is needed. Camera originals and the final master should be preserved in a form that does
not depend on a specific software version; project files preserve editorial decisions and are
useless without the media and the exact software version, which is why the archive should include
rendered self-contained masters as well as project files.

The practical discipline that most improves outcomes is verification at each transfer. Card offloads
are checksummed and verified against the original before the card is reformatted, because a
corrupted transfer discovered after reformatting is unrecoverable material. Backups are made in
duplicate before shooting continues. Every step in this chain is boring and each one has been
skipped by someone whose production then lost a day's work permanently.

Delivery and archiving:
  specifications differ  by destination and they are exacting
  read before finishing  the difference between one pass and several
  resolution and frame rate  the first constraints
  codec and bit rate   the delivery format, not the working format
  colour space         with the correct metadata attached
  audio configuration  channel layout, loudness, and stems if required
  captions and subtitles  burned in or as a sidecar; specified per platform
  camera originals     the irreplaceable asset
  self-contained masters  playable without a project file or specific software
  project files        preserve decisions and require exact software versions
  file naming          consistent and descriptive; the archive's only index if
                       the database is lost
  checksummed offloads  verify before reformatting the card
  duplicate backups    before shooting continues
  the media hierarchy  working copies on fast storage, archives on redundant
                       storage, one copy off site
  format obsolescence  archives need periodic migration to current formats; a
                       tape or drive nobody has read in a decade is a hypothesis
  the recurring lesson  every one of these steps is tedious, each has been
                       skipped by someone who lost material permanently, and the
                       cost of doing them is trivial compared with the cost of
                       the one time it matters
