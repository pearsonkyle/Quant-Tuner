## Exposure, Optics, and the Triangle

A photograph is a measurement of light integrated over time onto a sensor, and three parameters
determine how much light arrives and what the image looks like. Aperture is the opening's diameter
relative to focal length; shutter speed is the integration time; sensitivity is the gain applied to
the sensor's output. Each also has a side effect independent of brightness, and those side effects
are the reason the choice among equivalent exposures is a creative decision rather than an
arithmetic one.

Aperture controls depth of field, the range of distances rendered acceptably sharp. A wide aperture
gives a shallow depth of field that isolates a subject from its background; a narrow one extends the
range in focus and, past a point, begins to soften the whole image through diffraction. The
diffraction limit is a physical bound rather than a lens defect, and on small sensors it becomes
significant at apertures that would be unremarkable on large ones.

Shutter speed controls motion rendering. Fast shutters freeze motion, slow ones accumulate it into
blur, and the choice communicates something about the subject that no other parameter can. The
reciprocal rule of thumb for handholding — shutter speed at least the reciprocal of the focal length
— is a rough guide that stabilization has substantially relaxed for camera motion while leaving
subject motion entirely unaffected, a distinction that catches people out constantly.

Exposure and optics:
  aperture             opening relative to focal length; controls depth of field
  f-number             the ratio; smaller number means wider opening
  depth of field       the acceptably sharp range; narrows with wider apertures,
                       longer focal lengths, and closer subjects
  diffraction          softening at narrow apertures; a physical limit
  the sensor size interaction  small sensors hit diffraction at wider apertures
  shutter speed        integration time; controls motion rendering
  motion blur          subject motion; not addressed by stabilization
  camera shake         addressed by stabilization
  sensitivity          gain; increases noise rather than collecting more light
  the equivalent exposures  many combinations give the same brightness with
                       different rendering
  focal length         angle of view and perspective compression
  perspective          determined by camera position, not focal length; the
                       compression effect is a consequence of standing further
                       back
  the common misconception  a long lens does not compress perspective by itself;
                       the distance it lets you shoot from does

Q: Two photographs of the same scene are taken at f/2.8 with 1/500 s and at f/8 with 1/60 s, both
at the same sensitivity. They will differ in:
A. Brightness
B. Depth of field and motion rendering; the total light is the same, so overall brightness matches,
   but the wide aperture gives shallower focus and the fast shutter freezes motion the slow one
   would blur
C. Colour balance
D. Nothing
Reasoning: Three stops wider aperture and three stops faster shutter cancel for exposure. What does
not cancel is the side effects: depth of field from aperture and motion rendering from shutter
time. Choosing between equivalent exposures is choosing between these. Answer: B.

## Sensors, Noise, and Dynamic Range

A digital sensor counts photons in wells, and the fundamental noise source is that photon arrival is
a Poisson process — the count fluctuates with a standard deviation equal to the square root of the
mean, regardless of how good the sensor is. This shot noise dominates in all but the darkest parts
of an image, and it is why the signal-to-noise ratio improves with the square root of exposure and
why there is no way to photograph in dim light without either more time, a wider aperture, or a
larger sensor.

Raising sensitivity does not collect more light. It amplifies the signal already collected, along
with its noise, and the reason high-sensitivity images look noisy is that they were made from few
photons, not that the amplification added noise. This distinction has a practical consequence:
underexposing and brightening in processing is, on many modern sensors, nearly equivalent to
raising sensitivity in camera, and the choice depends on where the remaining read noise sits in the
chain.

Dynamic range is the ratio between the largest signal a sensor can record without clipping and the
noise floor. It determines how much of a high-contrast scene can be captured in one exposure, and
it is the parameter that most distinguishes raw capture from processed output — a raw file
routinely holds several stops of highlight and shadow information that a rendered image has
discarded. Exposing to preserve highlights and recovering shadows in processing exploits this,
because clipped highlights are unrecoverable while shadows contain noisy but real data.

Sensors and noise:
  photon counting      the sensor measures arrivals in wells
  shot noise           Poisson; standard deviation is the square root of the count
  the fundamental limit  no sensor design removes it
  signal-to-noise      improves as the square root of exposure
  read noise           added by the electronics; matters most in deep shadows
  sensitivity is gain  it amplifies, it does not collect
  the practical equivalence  underexpose and lift, or raise gain; similar on
                       modern sensors
  dynamic range        clipping level over noise floor
  clipped highlights   unrecoverable; no information remains
  lifted shadows       noisy but real; recoverable within limits
  expose to the right  maximize signal without clipping
  sensor size          larger area collects more photons for the same scene
                       brightness; the main reason larger sensors perform better
                       in low light
  colour filter array  a mosaic of colour filters; each site records one channel
  demosaicing          reconstructing full colour from the mosaic; the source of
                       several characteristic artifacts
  aliasing and moiré   fine repeating detail beating against the sensor grid

## Colour, Raw Processing, and Rendering

A raw file is sensor data, not an image. It holds per-site values in the sensor's native response
with no white balance applied, no tone curve, no colour transformation, and no sharpening. Turning
it into a viewable image is a chain of decisions, and understanding that the chain is a set of
choices rather than a recovery of truth is what separates deliberate processing from guesswork.

White balance is the first decision, and it exists because human vision adapts to the colour of the
illumination while a sensor does not. Setting it correctly means choosing what should be neutral,
which is a judgement about the scene rather than a measurement, and the correct answer is
frequently not neutral — the warmth of late light is a property of the scene that a technically
correct white balance removes.

Tone mapping compresses the captured dynamic range into the display's much smaller range, and every
image has undergone it whether or not the photographer chose the curve. The tradeoffs are between
global contrast, which makes the image feel punchy, and local contrast, which preserves detail in
shadows and highlights. Excessive local contrast produces the characteristic over-processed look —
halos at edges and a flat, ungraded feel — because it has removed the global luminance relationships
that communicate the light in the scene.

Colour and processing:
  raw is data          not an image; it requires a rendering chain
  the chain            white balance, demosaic, colour transform, tone curve,
                       sharpen, output transform
  white balance        choosing what is neutral; a judgement, not a measurement
  colour temperature   the illuminant's spectrum described by a blackbody
                       equivalent
  tint                 the perpendicular axis; needed for non-blackbody sources
  colour space         the set of representable colours; sRGB is small, wider
                       spaces hold more and require managed workflows
  gamut mapping        what happens to colours outside the destination space
  colour profile       the interpretation of the numbers; an untagged file is
                       ambiguous
  tone mapping         compressing captured range into display range
  global contrast      the overall curve; communicates the light in the scene
  local contrast       preserves detail; excessive amounts read as over-processed
  halos                the visible artifact of aggressive local contrast
  sharpening           edge contrast enhancement; capture, creative, and output
                       stages have different purposes
  noise reduction      trades detail for smoothness; the tradeoff is unavoidable
                       and applying it last is usually wrong
  the general principle  every step discards information, so order the chain to
                       make the destructive steps last

## Composition, Light, and Seeing

Technical control produces a correct photograph; composition and light produce a good one. Light is
the more important of the two and the less taught. Its qualities are direction, which creates form
through shadow; hardness, which is set by the apparent size of the source and determines how
abruptly shadows transition; colour, which sets mood and depends on the source; and intensity ratio
between key and fill, which sets contrast.

The single most useful practical fact about light is that softness depends on the source's size
relative to the subject, not on its absolute size or its distance alone. A window is a soft source
for a face nearby and a hard one for a landscape. Moving a subject closer to a source makes the
light softer, which is counterintuitive until the geometry is clear, and it is the lever that most
improves portrait lighting with no equipment at all.

Composition organizes the frame so the viewer's attention goes where intended. The conventional
guidelines — placing subjects off centre, using leading lines, framing with foreground elements,
balancing visual weight — are descriptions of what tends to work rather than rules, and they are
most useful as diagnostics for an image that is not working. The more fundamental skills are
noticing what is in the frame that should not be, and understanding that what is excluded shapes the
image as much as what is included.

Light and composition:
  direction            creates form through shadow
  hardness             set by apparent source size; the key practical variable
  the softness lever   move the subject closer to the source
  colour of light      mood; and it mixes badly when sources differ
  contrast ratio       key to fill; sets how dramatic the rendering is
  golden hour          low sun, warm and directional, long shadows
  blue hour            after sunset, soft and cool, low contrast
  overcast             a very large soft source; flattering and flat
  backlighting         separation and rim light; requires exposure judgement
  the frame edges      what is excluded shapes the image
  negative space       emptiness as a compositional element
  visual weight        what draws the eye; brightness, contrast, faces, sharpness
  leading lines        directing attention through the frame
  layering             foreground, subject, background as depth
  the diagnostic use   guidelines are most useful for fixing an image that is not
                       working, not for constructing one
  simplification       the most reliable improvement to most photographs is
                       removing something

## Computational Photography and Workflow

Computational photography produces images that no single exposure could, by combining multiple
captures or by using models of the scene. Exposure bracketing and merging extends dynamic range
beyond the sensor's. Burst capture and alignment reduces noise by averaging, which is why phone
cameras with tiny sensors produce usable low-light images. Focus stacking extends depth of field
beyond the diffraction limit by combining exposures focused at different distances.

Depth estimation, whether from multiple cameras, phase-detection sites, or a learned model,
enables synthetic aperture effects — the background blur that a small sensor cannot produce
optically. The characteristic failure is at the boundaries of the depth estimate, where hair, glass,
and fine structures are misclassified, and recognizing these artifacts is how synthetic depth is
distinguished from optical.

Workflow and archiving are the least glamorous and most consequential part of the practice. Files
accumulate faster than any manual organization scheme survives, and the strategies that work are
the ones requiring no discipline: capture-time metadata, a consistent folder structure derived from
the date, and a rating pass done immediately rather than eventually. Backup follows the standard
rule of multiple copies on multiple media with one off-site, and the specific failure that destroys
archives is a single copy on a single drive that is discovered to be corrupt only when it is needed.

Computational photography and workflow:
  exposure bracketing  multiple exposures merged for extended range
  burst averaging      noise reduced by combining aligned frames; the basis of
                       small-sensor low-light performance
  alignment            required before combining; motion is the difficulty
  focus stacking       extended depth of field beyond the diffraction limit
  panorama stitching   extended field of view; projection choice matters
  depth estimation     from stereo, phase detection, or a learned model
  synthetic aperture   background blur computed rather than optical
  the boundary artifact  hair, glass, and fine structure at depth
                       discontinuities
  semantic processing  region-aware adjustment; skies, skin, foliage treated
                       differently
  the aesthetic question  computational processing has a house style, and
                       recognizing it is part of evaluating an image
