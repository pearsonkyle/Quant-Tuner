## Displays, Optics, and the Perceptual Target

A head-mounted display presents a separate image to each eye through optics that place the virtual
image at a comfortable focal distance. The design targets are field of view, angular resolution,
persistence, and weight, and they conflict with each other in ways that make every headset a
particular set of compromises rather than a point on a single quality axis.

Angular resolution is the meaningful resolution measure, expressed in pixels per degree, because a
panel's pixel count means nothing without knowing across how wide a field it is spread. Human foveal
acuity corresponds to roughly sixty pixels per degree, and reaching that across a wide field
requires pixel counts far beyond current panels, which is why foveated rendering — full resolution
only where the eye is looking, tracked in real time — is the standard approach to closing the gap.

Persistence is the fraction of each frame during which the display actually emits light, and low
persistence is essential rather than optional. If a pixel stays lit while the head rotates, the
image smears across the retina, producing motion blur that is perceived as unsteadiness. Displays
therefore flash briefly each frame, which trades brightness for clarity and introduces a flicker
sensitivity that constrains the minimum acceptable refresh rate.

Displays and optics:
  stereoscopic display  a separate image per eye
  field of view        how much of the visual field is covered
  angular resolution   pixels per degree; the meaningful measure
  foveal acuity        roughly sixty pixels per degree
  foveated rendering   full detail only where the eye looks
  eye tracking         the enabling technology for foveation and for interaction
  persistence          the emitting fraction of each frame
  low persistence      required; otherwise head motion smears the image
  refresh rate         ninety hertz is a common floor; higher reduces both smear
                       and flicker
  optical distortion   lens distortion corrected by pre-warping the render
  chromatic aberration  corrected per colour channel in the same pass
  the vergence-accommodation conflict  the eyes converge at the virtual depth and
                       focus at the fixed optical distance; the mismatch causes
                       fatigue and is the central unsolved optical problem
  varifocal and light field  approaches to resolving it; not yet common
  passthrough          camera-based view of the real world; latency and
                       distortion are the difficulties
  optical see-through  transparent combiners; bright, and unable to render black

Q: A headset with a high panel resolution appears less sharp than one with fewer pixels. The most
likely reason is:
A. The panel is defective
B. It spreads those pixels across a wider field of view, so its angular resolution in pixels per
   degree is lower; total pixel count is meaningless without the field it covers
C. The refresh rate is lower
D. The lenses are dirty
Reasoning: Perceived sharpness depends on pixels per degree of visual angle, not on panel pixel
count. A wide field of view spreads the same pixels thinner. This is why field of view and
sharpness are a direct tradeoff at any given panel specification. Answer: B.

## Tracking, Registration, and Latency

Tracking determines where the headset and controllers are in space, and its quality determines
whether the experience feels solid or unstable. Rotational tracking from inertial sensors is fast
and drifts; positional tracking from cameras is accurate and slower. Fusing them gives the low
latency of the inertial path with the drift correction of the visual one, and essentially every
modern system does this.

Inside-out tracking uses cameras on the headset to observe the environment and compute the headset's
pose within it, building a map as it goes. It requires no external hardware and depends on the
environment having enough visual texture, which is why blank walls, low light, and reflective
surfaces degrade it. Outside-in tracking uses fixed external sensors and is more robust in exchange
for setup and a bounded play area.

End-to-end latency — from head motion to the corresponding photons — is the number that determines
comfort, and the target is around twenty milliseconds. Since a full pipeline cannot reliably meet
that, the standard technique is late reprojection: render the frame, then just before display,
re-sample the most recent head pose and warp the rendered image to match. This decouples perceived
latency from render time and is what allows a heavy scene to remain comfortable, though it cannot
correct for objects that moved within the frame.

Tracking and latency:
  inertial sensing     fast, drifts
  visual tracking      accurate, slower
  sensor fusion        low latency with drift correction
  inside-out           headset cameras mapping the environment; no external setup
  the texture requirement  blank walls, low light, and reflections degrade it
  outside-in           fixed external sensors; robust, bounded, requires setup
  simultaneous localization and mapping  building the map while locating within
                       it
  relocalization       recovering pose after tracking loss; the recovery
                       experience matters as much as the loss rate
  hand tracking        camera-based; no controller, less precise, no haptics
  controller tracking  precise pose with buttons and haptics
  end-to-end latency   head motion to photons; target around twenty milliseconds
  late reprojection    re-sample the pose and warp just before display
  what it cannot fix   objects that moved within the frame; they show artifacts
  registration         alignment of virtual content with the real world in
                       augmented reality
  drift in registration  a virtual object sliding relative to its intended
                       physical anchor; the most noticeable augmented reality
                       failure and the hardest to eliminate

## Rendering Constraints

Rendering for a headset means rendering twice, at high resolution, at a high refresh rate, with a
hard deadline. Missing the deadline is not a dropped frame that goes unnoticed — it is a visible
judder that immediately breaks the experience and contributes to discomfort. The frame budget is
therefore treated as a hard constraint from which everything else is derived, which is a different
engineering culture from flat-screen rendering where an occasional dropped frame is tolerable.

The techniques for meeting it exploit properties specific to the medium. Stereo rendering shares
most work between eyes through single-pass instanced rendering rather than rendering the scene
twice. Foveated rendering reduces shading rate in the periphery, where acuity is low, and eye
tracking makes this aggressive. Fixed foveation without eye tracking shades the edges of the display
less, which works because the optics are worse there anyway.

Some standard flat-screen techniques transfer badly. Screen-space effects that assume a single
viewpoint produce stereo inconsistencies that read as depth errors. Post-processing that adds motion
blur or depth of field conflicts with the viewer's own eye movement and focus. Billboarded impostors
that look correct from one angle are immediately obvious in stereo. The general rule is that
anything faking depth is much more likely to be detected when the viewer has real binocular depth
cues and can move their head.

Rendering:
  double rendering     one view per eye
  the hard deadline    a missed frame is visible judder, not a minor artifact
  single-pass stereo   sharing work between eyes
  foveated rendering   reduced shading rate in the periphery
  fixed foveation      without eye tracking; exploits the optics being worse at
                       the edges
  variable rate shading  hardware support for spatially varying shading cost
  the transfer problem  flat-screen techniques that fake depth are detected
  screen-space effects  stereo inconsistency read as depth error
  artificial blur      conflicts with the viewer's own focus and eye motion
  impostors and billboards  obvious under binocular vision and head motion
  level of detail      aggressive, and transitions must be invisible since the
                       viewer can approach anything
  lighting             baked lighting is cheap and static; the tradeoff is
                       sharper here because the budget is tighter
  scale accuracy       objects must be modelled at true scale or they read as
                       wrong immediately; this is a modelling discipline that
                       flat-screen work does not require

## Interaction, Comfort, and Presence

Interaction in three dimensions has no established conventions comparable to the desktop's, so most
designs are still exploring. Direct manipulation — reaching out and grabbing — is the most
immediately learnable and is limited by arm reach and by fatigue. Ray-based pointing extends reach
and is precise at distance and imprecise for small targets because small angular errors amplify.
Gaze plus a confirming action is low-effort and suffers from the difficulty of distinguishing
looking from selecting.

Arm fatigue is a real constraint with a memorable name and it is routinely underestimated. Holding
the arms extended is tiring within a couple of minutes, which rules out interfaces that require
sustained mid-air interaction. The mitigations are keeping interaction near the body, allowing rest
positions, using brief gestures rather than sustained ones, and providing physical support where the
context permits.

Locomotion is the largest single source of discomfort. Motion sickness in these systems is
attributed to the conflict between visual motion and the vestibular system's report that the body is
stationary, and any smooth artificial movement produces it in a substantial fraction of people.
Teleportation avoids it entirely by removing continuous motion. Comfort options — vignetting the
periphery during movement, snap turning rather than smooth rotation, and a stable reference frame
such as a visible cockpit — reduce it substantially and should be provided rather than debated.

Interaction and comfort:
  direct manipulation  reach and grab; learnable, limited by reach and fatigue
  ray pointing         extends reach; angular error amplifies at distance
  gaze plus confirm    low effort; separating looking from selecting is the
                       problem
  hand versus controller  precision and haptics against nothing to hold
  arm fatigue          within minutes; rules out sustained mid-air interfaces
  the mitigations      interaction near the body, rest positions, brief gestures
  affordances          virtual objects must signal what can be grabbed
  haptic feedback      even simple vibration substantially improves confidence in
                       a grab
  locomotion sickness  visual-vestibular conflict
  teleportation        removes continuous motion; the most comfortable option
  vignetting           narrowing the field during movement; measurably effective
  snap turning         discrete rotation instead of smooth
  stable reference frame  a visible cockpit or horizon reduces conflict
  presence             the sense of being there; broken instantly by tracking
                       failure, judder, or an object behaving wrongly
  the fragility        presence takes minutes to build and one artifact to
                       destroy, which is why reliability outranks fidelity

## Spatial Audio, Authoring, and Evaluation

Audio contributes more to presence than its share of development effort suggests. Spatial audio
renders sound from a position in the world, using interaural time and level differences for
horizontal localization and spectral filtering by the outer ear for elevation and front-back
disambiguation. The filtering is individual, so generic models localize adequately in azimuth and
poorly in elevation, and personalization measurably improves it.

Room acoustics matter as much as direction. Early reflections and reverberation tell the listener
the size and material of the space, and a sound rendered without them is perceived as being inside
the head rather than in the world. Occlusion — muffling sound that passes through a virtual wall —
is a strong cue and is comparatively cheap to approximate.

Authoring and evaluation for this medium have their own discipline. Testing in the headset
frequently is the only reliable way to judge scale, comfort, and readability, because none of them
can be assessed on a monitor. New users are the essential test population since they have not
adapted, and adaptation is real — a developer who has spent months in a build no longer experiences
what a first-time user does. Session length should be part of the test protocol, since discomfort
accumulates and a demo that feels fine for three minutes may not for thirty.
