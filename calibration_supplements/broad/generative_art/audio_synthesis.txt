## Acoustics and Hearing

Sound is a pressure wave, and its perceptual attributes map onto physical ones
imperfectly enough that the mismatches drive most of audio engineering. Frequency maps
roughly to pitch, amplitude to loudness, and spectral content to timbre, but each mapping is
nonlinear and context-dependent in ways that matter practically.

Pitch perception is logarithmic. A doubling of frequency is an octave regardless of where it
occurs, so 100 to 200 Hz and 1000 to 2000 Hz are the same musical interval and vastly
different absolute spans. This is why frequency axes in audio are drawn logarithmically, why
equalizer bands are specified by centre frequency and Q rather than by absolute width, and why
a filter sweep sounds musically even only when swept exponentially.

Loudness perception is also logarithmic, which is what the decibel expresses, and it is
frequency-dependent. The equal-loudness contours show that human hearing is most sensitive
around 2 to 5 kHz — the range of speech consonants — and much less sensitive at low and very
high frequencies, with the disparity greatest at low listening levels. This is why a mix
balanced at high volume sounds bass-light when played quietly, and why checking a mix at
several levels is standard practice rather than fussiness.

Acoustic and perceptual quantities:
  audible range         nominally 20 Hz to 20 kHz; the top declines with age
  octave                a 2:1 frequency ratio
  semitone              a ratio of 2^(1/12) ≈ 1.0595 in equal temperament
  cents                 1/100 semitone; ~5 cents is the discrimination threshold
  decibel               20 log10 amplitude ratio, or 10 log10 power ratio
  +6 dB                 double the amplitude;  +10 dB is roughly twice as loud
  equal loudness        sensitivity peaks 2-5 kHz; bass needs more level to
                        sound equally loud, especially when quiet
  masking               a loud tone hides a quieter nearby one; the basis of
                        perceptual audio compression
  critical bands        the ear's effective filter widths; masking acts within them
  Haas effect           a delayed copy under ~30 ms is perceived as one sound with
                        direction from the first arrival
  speed of sound        ~343 m/s in air; ~34 cm per millisecond

Masking is the perceptual phenomenon with the most practical consequences. A loud sound
renders a quieter sound at a nearby frequency inaudible, and this is why mixes become muddy:
several instruments occupying the same frequency range mask each other, so raising the level
of one does not make it clearer, it merely masks the others more. The remedy is spectral
separation — carving space with equalization, choosing sounds that occupy different ranges,
or arranging so that competing elements do not sound simultaneously. Masking is also what
perceptual codecs exploit, discarding content that would be masked and therefore inaudible.

Q: Two sine tones are played at 220 Hz and 440 Hz. The musical interval between them is:
A. A perfect fifth   B. One octave   C. Two octaves   D. A major third
Reasoning: An octave is a frequency ratio of exactly 2:1, and 440/220 = 2. Musical intervals
correspond to frequency ratios rather than differences, which is why the same interval spans
different absolute frequency ranges at different pitches. Answer: B.

## Synthesis Methods

Subtractive synthesis begins with a harmonically rich waveform and removes content with
filters. It is the classic analog approach and remains the most intuitive, because the
mapping from control to result is direct: a sawtooth contains all harmonics with amplitude
falling as one over harmonic number, a square contains only odd harmonics, and a low-pass
filter sweeping down progressively removes the upper ones, producing the characteristic
sweeping timbre.

Additive synthesis builds sound by summing sine waves, each with its own frequency,
amplitude, and envelope. It is completely general — any sound can in principle be constructed
this way — and it is unwieldy, since a realistic timbre may need dozens of partials each with
a time-varying envelope, giving hundreds of parameters. It comes into its own for sounds with
inharmonic partials such as bells and metallic tones, where subtractive methods struggle
because the source waveforms are harmonic.

Frequency modulation synthesis modulates one oscillator's frequency with another at audio
rate, generating sidebands at sums and differences of the carrier and modulator frequencies.
Small numbers of operators produce enormously complex spectra, which is why it was
computationally attractive and why it defined a generation of digital instrument sound. Its
weakness is control: the relationship between parameters and result is not intuitive, since
changing the modulation index redistributes energy across sidebands in a way described by
Bessel functions, and the harmonicity of the result depends on the ratio of modulator to
carrier frequency — integer ratios give harmonic tones, non-integer ratios give clangorous
inharmonic ones.

Synthesis methods:
  subtractive      rich source plus filters; intuitive, the analog paradigm
  additive         sum of sines; fully general, parameter-heavy, good for
                   inharmonic timbres
  FM / phase mod   sidebands from audio-rate modulation; complex spectra cheaply,
                   unintuitive control
  wavetable        interpolate through a table of single-cycle waveforms; evolving
                   timbres with modest computation
  granular         overlapping short grains; textures, time-stretching, clouds
  physical model   simulate the mechanism — string, tube, membrane; expressive and
                   computationally expensive
  Karplus-Strong   a delay line with a filter in the feedback; plucked string from
                   almost nothing
  sample-based     recorded sources with pitch shifting and layering; realistic
                   and storage-hungry
  concatenative    assemble from a corpus of analyzed fragments; corpus-based
                   sound design
  neural           learned generative models; increasingly capable, opaque control

Physical modelling deserves attention because it produces the most expressive instruments.
Rather than reproducing a sound's spectrum, it simulates the mechanism that produces it — a
string's wave equation, a tube's air column, a membrane's modes — and the result responds to
performance gestures the way the physical instrument does. Playing harder does not simply
increase amplitude, it changes the spectrum in the way the real instrument does, because the
same nonlinearity is present in the model. The cost is computation and the difficulty of
tuning models that can become unstable or produce unphysical results.

## Filters, Envelopes, and Modulation

Filters shape the spectrum and are the primary timbral tool. A low-pass passes frequencies
below its cutoff and attenuates above, a high-pass the reverse, a band-pass a range around a
centre, and a notch rejects a narrow band. Slope, expressed in decibels per octave,
determines how sharply the transition occurs, with 12 and 24 dB per octave the common analog
designs. Resonance emphasizes frequencies near the cutoff by feeding output back to the
input, and at high settings the filter self-oscillates, becoming a sine oscillator at the
cutoff frequency.

The musical importance of resonance is that it creates a formant — a peak in the spectrum at
a fixed frequency — and formants are how the ear identifies vowel sounds and instrument
bodies. Sweeping a resonant filter produces the characteristic sound of the medium precisely
because it moves a formant, which the auditory system interprets as a change in the shape of
the resonating body rather than as a change in the source.

Envelopes control how a parameter changes over the duration of a note, and the
attack-decay-sustain-release shape is the standard. Attack time is perceptually the most
important parameter in the whole synthesizer for instrument identity: the onset transient is
what the ear uses to identify a sound source, and a recording of a piano with its attack
removed is difficult to identify. Sustain is a level rather than a time, which is the detail
that confuses newcomers, and release begins when the note is released rather than at any
fixed time.

Filters and modulation:
  low-pass          the primary timbral control; brightness
  high-pass         removes rumble and mud; often the most useful mix filter
  band-pass         isolates a range; formants and telephone effects
  notch             surgical removal; hum and resonance elimination
  slope             6, 12, 18, 24 dB/octave; steeper is more surgical
  resonance / Q     emphasis at the cutoff; self-oscillates at extremes
  ADSR              attack, decay, sustain level, release
  attack transient  the key cue for source identification
  LFO               sub-audio modulation; vibrato, tremolo, filter sweeps
  modulation matrix routing any source to any destination; where character comes
                    from
  velocity          per-note dynamic; should modulate brightness as well as level,
                    since real instruments get brighter when played harder
  keytracking       scale filter cutoff with pitch so timbre stays consistent
                    across the keyboard

The advice about velocity modulating brightness rather than only volume is worth
emphasizing because it is the single most effective change for making a synthesized
instrument sound played rather than triggered. Real instruments excited harder produce more
high-frequency content, not merely more amplitude — a hard-struck string has more upper
partials, a loudly blown reed has more harmonics. Routing velocity to filter cutoff alongside
amplitude reproduces this, and its absence is a large part of why unmodulated sample playback
sounds mechanical.

The fix exploits a useful property of hearing called the missing fundamental: if the
harmonics of a note are present, the ear reconstructs the pitch even when the fundamental
itself is absent. A small speaker cannot reproduce 45 Hz, and it can reproduce 90, 135, and
180 Hz, and the listener will hear the bass at its correct pitch.

Three ways to add that harmonic content, roughly in order of how natural they sound:

Saturation or soft clipping on the bass. Distortion generates harmonics at integer multiples
of the fundamental, so a modest drive adds exactly the series you need. Use a parallel or
band-limited version — saturate a high-passed copy and blend it under the clean low end — so
you keep the weight on systems that can reproduce it without making the sub-bass gritty.

A parallel octave-up layer. Duplicate the bass part an octave higher with a simple timbre and
mix it well below the main part. On big systems it sits under the fundamental and is barely
noticed; on small ones it carries the line.

Reduce the reliance on the sub range at the source. If the patch is a pure sine or a heavily
filtered saw, open the filter or change the waveform so the note has natural harmonic content
to begin with. This is usually the cleanest solution and the one to try first.

Two process notes. Check on the worst system you expect people to use, not just on
monitors — a single mono phone speaker is the standard reference for exactly this reason. And
high-pass everything else in the mix above the bass's range so it is not competing with
kick and pad energy in the same octave, which makes the bass audible without adding level.

## Sampling, Granular, and Time Manipulation

Sample-based instruments play back recordings, and their realism depends on how the
inevitable gaps are covered. Multi-sampling records the source at many pitches and dynamic
levels so that playback interpolates between close neighbours rather than stretching a single
recording far from its origin. Pitch shifting by playback rate changes duration and shifts
formants, which is why a voice pitched up sounds unnaturally small — the resonances of the
body scaled with the pitch, which does not happen when a real singer sings higher. Formant
preservation corrects this by separating the spectral envelope from the excitation and
shifting only the latter.

Granular synthesis decomposes audio into short grains, typically 10 to 100 milliseconds, and
reassembles them with independent control of position, density, pitch, and envelope. Its
power is that time and pitch become independent: grains can be read from a fixed position
while playback continues, freezing a sound indefinitely, or advanced at any rate relative to
their pitch. Grain size determines character — very short grains produce a pitched buzz at
the grain rate, medium grains produce texture, long grains preserve recognizable fragments.

Time and pitch manipulation:
  playback rate       changes pitch and duration together; the tape paradigm
  granular            independent time and pitch; textural artifacts at extremes
  phase vocoder       STFT-based; excellent for tonal material, smears transients
  transient-aware     detect and preserve onsets, stretch only between them
  formant preserve    keep the spectral envelope fixed when shifting pitch
  loop points         must match in phase and amplitude or produce a click
  crossfade loops     the practical fix for imperfect loop points
  round robin         alternate among several recordings of the same note to avoid
                      the machine-gun effect of identical repeats
  velocity layers     separate recordings per dynamic level; the main cost of
                      realistic sample libraries

The machine-gun effect is worth naming because the fix is simple and the problem is
pervasive. Repeated identical samples are immediately recognizable as artificial, because no
real performer produces two identical notes. Round robin cycling among several recordings,
combined with small randomizations of pitch, timing, and level, removes the artifact almost
entirely. The randomization amounts are small — a few cents, a few milliseconds, a decibel —
and their absence is more audible than their presence.

## Composition, Space, and Practice

Algorithmic composition generates musical material by rule. The approaches parallel those in
visual generative work: grammars that rewrite musical phrases, Markov chains that model
transition probabilities between pitches or chords, constraint systems that enforce voice
leading rules, cellular automata mapped to pitch and rhythm, and generative models trained on
corpora. The perennial difficulty is long-range structure: local rules produce plausible
phrases and rarely produce a piece with direction, because musical form operates over minutes
and local rules see only seconds.

Hierarchical approaches address this by generating structure first — form, key areas,
dynamic arc — and filling in detail within that scaffold, which is how human composers
typically work and which reliably produces more coherent results than generating note by
note. The same principle appears throughout generative work: decide global structure
explicitly rather than hoping it emerges from local rules.

Spatial audio places sound in three dimensions, and the cues the auditory system uses are
well characterized. Interaural time difference dominates below about 1.5 kHz, since the
wavelength exceeds the head's width and phase comparison is unambiguous. Interaural level
difference dominates above, where the head shadows the far ear. Spectral cues from the outer
ear's shape resolve elevation and front-back ambiguity, and these are individual, which is why
generic head-related transfer functions localize imperfectly and personalized ones work
markedly better.

Spatial and production practice:
  panning              amplitude difference between channels; a simple ILD cue
  binaural / HRTF      filter with head-related transfer functions; convincing over
                       headphones, individual variation limits generic sets
  ambisonics           a channel-agnostic spherical harmonic representation;
                       decodable to any speaker layout, rotatable for head tracking
  reverberation        early reflections convey room size and distance; the tail
                       conveys space
  pre-delay            gap before reverb; separates source from space and preserves
                       clarity
  distance cues        level, high-frequency loss, and the ratio of direct to
                       reverberant sound; the ratio is the strongest cue
  gain staging         keep levels healthy through the chain; in floating point,
                       headroom is cheap and clipping is still permanent
  dynamic range        compression controls it; over-compression removes the
                       contrast that makes loud passages feel loud
  reference tracks     compare against known material on the same system
  mono compatibility   check; phase-cancelling stereo effects can vanish

The direct-to-reverberant ratio deserves the last word because it is the strongest distance
cue and the most commonly mishandled. A sound close to the listener is mostly direct with
little room; a distant sound is mostly reflected. Changing the level of a source without
changing this ratio makes it louder or quieter rather than nearer or farther, which is why
simply turning something down does not push it back in a mix. Increasing the reverb send
while reducing the direct level moves a source convincingly into the distance, and doing the
opposite brings it forward, and this pair of controls does more for depth than any amount of
equalization.
