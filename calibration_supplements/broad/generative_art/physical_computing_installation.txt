## Light Sources and Control

Light is the primary medium of most installation work, and the properties determining how it
behaves are frequently different from the ones specified in a catalogue. Luminous flux measured in
lumens is the total light emitted, which describes the source. Illuminance in lux is the light
falling on a surface, which depends on distance and falls with its square. Luminance in candelas
per square metre is the brightness of a surface as seen, which is what the viewer perceives. A
source specified only in lumens says nothing about how bright anything will appear without knowing
the geometry.

Colour rendering describes how faithfully a source reveals surface colours, and it matters
whenever the light is illuminating something rather than being the subject. The colour rendering
index compares against a reference across a set of sample colours, and its known weakness is that
a source can score well while rendering saturated reds poorly, which is why the newer metrics
report fidelity and gamut separately. Sources with spiky spectra — some LEDs and most discharge
lamps — render some colours badly regardless of their index.

Colour temperature describes the appearance of white light on a scale from warm to cool, and it
is not a measure of quality. Two sources at the same colour temperature can have entirely
different spectra and render colours differently, which is why matching colour temperature between
fixtures does not guarantee they will look the same on a surface.

Q: An installation lit by PWM-dimmed LEDs looks fine to the eye and shows severe banding when
filmed. The cause is:
A. The camera is faulty
B. The PWM frequency interacts with the camera's rolling shutter, so different rows of the sensor
   are exposed during different parts of the PWM cycle
C. The LEDs are the wrong colour temperature
D. The exposure is wrong
Reasoning: A rolling shutter exposes sensor rows sequentially rather than simultaneously, so a
light switching on and off during the frame produces bright and dark bands corresponding to the
PWM state when each row was exposed. The eye integrates over time and does not see it. Raising the
PWM frequency well above the row rate, or using analogue dimming, eliminates it. Answer: B.

## LED Systems

Addressable LED strips have become the default medium for light-based installation work because
they permit per-pixel control at low cost. The two families differ in ways that determine which
suits an application. Single-wire protocols embed the clock in the data timing, which requires only
one signal wire and makes the timing critical — an interrupt during transmission corrupts the
frame. Two-wire protocols with a separate clock are timing-insensitive, work over longer distances,
and support far higher refresh rates.

Power is the constraint that most installations underestimate. A common strip specification is
sixty LEDs per metre at sixty milliamps each at full white, which is 3.6 amps per metre — so a
ten-metre run at full brightness draws thirty-six amps, which is a substantial supply and a
conductor sizing problem. Voltage drop along the strip is the associated failure: at five volts, a
run of more than a couple of metres shows visible dimming and colour shift toward red at the far
end, because the red channel's forward voltage is lowest and it survives the drop best.

Power injection addresses this by feeding supply at intervals along the strip rather than only at
one end, and the spacing follows from the acceptable drop. Twelve-volt and twenty-four-volt strips
tolerate longer runs because the same absolute drop is a smaller fraction of the supply, which is
why higher voltages are preferred for large installations despite the lower per-pixel density.

  power calculation    LEDs times current times voltage; a ten-metre run at full

The missing common ground deserves specific emphasis because it produces symptoms that appear to
be data corruption and is fixed by a wire. If the controller and the LED strip are powered from
separate supplies with no ground connection between them, the data signal has no defined reference
at the receiving end, and the behaviour ranges from erratic flickering to apparently random colours
to intermittent failure that appears when someone touches the installation. Connecting the grounds
resolves it, and checking for it first saves hours of investigating the data.

## Projection

Projection places light on surfaces, and the fundamental constraint is that a projector adds light
and cannot remove it — so black in a projected image is whatever the ambient light makes the
surface, and contrast depends entirely on controlling that ambient. An installation in a bright
space will have washed-out projection regardless of the projector's specification, and darkening
the space is more effective than any increase in brightness.

Throw ratio relates the projection distance to the image width and determines whether a projector
fits a space. A standard lens might have a ratio around 1.5, so a three-metre-wide image requires
4.5 metres of throw. Short-throw and ultra-short-throw lenses reduce this substantially, which
matters when the space is small or when people would otherwise walk through the beam and cast
shadows.

Keystone correction compensates for projecting at an angle, and it should be understood as a
compromise rather than a solution. Correcting geometrically in software means discarding pixels —
the projected rectangle becomes a trapezoid within the available resolution — so the image loses
brightness and resolution. Physical alignment, lens shift, and mounting the projector on the
optical axis are preferable where possible.

Projection mapping projects onto non-flat surfaces so that the imagery aligns with the geometry,
and the technique is what permits projection onto buildings, sculptures, and irregular objects.
The alignment is achieved by warping the source image to match the surface, either by manually
adjusting a mesh or by structured-light scanning that determines the correspondence
automatically.

Edge blending deserves note because it is how large projected surfaces are built and because its
difficulty is in matching rather than in blending. Overlapping two projectors and applying a
gradient in the overlap region so their contributions sum to the correct level is geometrically
straightforward. Making the seam invisible requires the two projectors to match in brightness,
colour, and black level — and projectors of the same model differ, drift over time, and age
differently, so the matching requires measurement and periodic adjustment rather than being a
one-time setup.

[assistant] The arithmetic is stark: your ambient went from 20 lux to 300, and your projector puts
about 444 lux on the surface. In the studio the contrast ratio was 23 to 1, which reads as
properly black. In the gallery it is 2.5 to 1, which reads as grey with slightly brighter grey on
it.

You cannot fix this with brightness alone. Matching the studio contrast at 300 lux ambient would
require about 6,700 lux on the surface, which is fifteen times your current projector — a
sixty-thousand-lumen machine, which is a different order of installation.

Four approaches, and the first two are the realistic ones.

Reduce the ambient locally rather than globally. The gallery cannot darken the whole space and can
frequently accommodate a local intervention: a partial enclosure, a light-blocking baffle above and
around the projection surface, redirecting or shading the specific fixtures illuminating that wall,
or a fabric surround. Reducing the ambient on the surface from 300 to 60 lux restores most of the
contrast and is a fraction of the cost of a larger projector.

Change the surface. An ambient-light-rejecting screen material accepts light from the projector's
direction and rejects light from other angles, which improves the contrast ratio by a factor of
several in exactly your situation. It is directional, so the viewing positions must be considered,
and it is substantially cheaper than the projector upgrade.

Increase brightness, which helps proportionally and does not solve it alone. Going to 10,000
lumens takes your contrast from 2.5 to about 4.7, which is better and still not what the studio
looked like.

Redesign the content. Imagery designed for a low-contrast environment — bright content on a
mid-tone base rather than bright elements on black — reads well where black is unattainable. This
is a legitimate response rather than a compromise, and content designed for the actual condition
will look better than content designed for a dark room and shown in a bright one.

The general principle to carry forward: measure the ambient illuminance at the installation site
before finalizing the design. It is a two-minute measurement with a meter or a phone app, and it
determines what is achievable in a way that no projector specification does.

## Kinetic Elements and Fabrication

Kinetic work adds mechanical motion, and the engineering requirements are unforgiving because the
piece runs continuously and is accessible to the public. The duty cycle question dominates: a
mechanism running eight hours a day for three months completes an enormous number of cycles, and
components adequate for a demonstration wear out or overheat within days.

Actuator selection follows from the motion required and from the duty cycle. Hobby servos hold
position by drawing current continuously and overheat when loaded; they suit intermittent motion
and not sustained holding. Steppers hold position with current and also run hot, and they lose
steps silently under overload. Geared DC motors with position feedback suit continuous rotation.
Solenoids must be duty-limited or they burn out. And for slow silent motion, shape memory alloys
and pneumatics have their niches.

Noise is a design parameter in a gallery and is frequently discovered late. A stepper motor's
characteristic whine, a servo's chatter as it hunts around a position, a fan, a relay click, and a
mechanism's vibration transmitted into a plinth are all audible in a quiet space and all read as
technical noise rather than as part of the work. Compliant mounting, quieter actuators,
microstepping, and mechanical damping address them, and considering them at the design stage
avoids a rebuild.

The anisotropy of 3D printed parts deserves emphasis because it produces failures that seem
inexplicable. A part printed by fused deposition is strong within each layer and weak between
layers, so the strength depends entirely on the print orientation relative to the load. A bracket
that would carry the load if printed one way snaps at a layer boundary if printed another, and the
part looks identical. Designing with the load path in mind and orienting the print accordingly is
what makes printed parts structural rather than decorative.

## Power, Safety, and Production

Electrical safety in installation work is a legal and ethical requirement rather than a matter of
diligence, because the public will touch the work and the installer is not present. Anything
connected to mains voltage requires appropriate isolation, enclosure, earthing, and protection, and
in most jurisdictions requires appropriately qualified work and testing.

Power calculation should be done for the simultaneous worst case rather than for typical
operation. An LED installation at full white draws several times its average, a motor's inrush
current is many times its running current, and a projector's startup draw exceeds its steady
consumption. Sizing for the average produces a supply that trips or sags at the moment everything
runs together, which is typically the opening.

Cable management is both a safety requirement and a visual one. Cables on the floor are a trip
hazard and are also visible, and both are addressed by the same interventions — routing within
structure, under covers, or overhead. Strain relief at every connection prevents the most common
physical failure, which is a cable pulled until the conductor breaks inside the insulation where
it cannot be seen.

The rigging point deserves the closing emphasis because it is the one where a mistake is
catastrophic rather than merely inconvenient. Anything suspended above people is a structural
element requiring calculation, appropriate rated hardware, secondary retention in case the primary
fails, and in most venues sign-off by someone qualified. The consequences of a failure are entirely
different in kind from a piece that stops working, and the temptation to improvise under time
pressure at install is exactly when the risk is greatest. Determining the rigging approach and the
hardware early, and treating it as a fixed constraint rather than as something to resolve on site,
is the practice that prevents the situation arising.
