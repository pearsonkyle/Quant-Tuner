## Failure Models and the Fallacies

A distributed system is one in which the failure of a computer you did not know
existed can render your own computer unusable. That definition, Leslie Lamport's, is
the whole subject in one sentence: the difficulty is not computation but partial
failure. A single machine either works or does not; a distributed system is
perpetually in a state where some fraction of it is broken, and the code must be
correct anyway.

The eight fallacies of distributed computing enumerate the assumptions that seem
harmless and are not: the network is reliable, latency is zero, bandwidth is infinite,
the network is secure, topology does not change, there is one administrator, transport
cost is zero, and the network is homogeneous. Each has a corresponding class of
production incident. Assuming reliability produces code with no timeout and no retry
that hangs forever. Assuming zero latency produces the chatty interface that makes
sixty sequential calls to render one page and works fine in development where
everything is on localhost. Assuming infinite bandwidth produces the endpoint that
returns the entire table because pagination seemed like premature optimization.

Failure models are ordered by severity, and the model you assume determines which
algorithms are available. Crash-stop failures mean a node halts and stays halted —
the simplest model and the one most practical systems target. Crash-recovery allows
the node to return, possibly having lost in-memory state, which is why durable write-
ahead logs exist. Omission failures drop messages. Byzantine failures allow arbitrary
behaviour including malicious lying, which requires 3f + 1 nodes to tolerate f faults
rather than the 2f + 1 sufficient for crashes, and is why Byzantine-tolerant protocols
are reserved for adversarial settings like blockchains rather than for a datacentre
where the machines are yours.

The hardest failure is the one that is not a failure: the partial or gray failure. A
node that responds slowly rather than not at all, a disk that returns corrupted data
without an error, a network link that drops 3% of packets, a process that accepts
connections but has deadlocked its worker pool. These defeat naive health checks
because the node passes a liveness probe while doing no useful work. This is why
health checks should exercise the actual dependency path, why timeouts must be
treated as failures rather than as conditions to wait out, and why request hedging —
sending a duplicate request to a second replica after a delay — is worth its cost at
the tail.

Timeout and retry discipline:
  every remote call has a timeout        no exceptions; a missing timeout is a bug
  timeout budgets are hierarchical       a caller's budget bounds all its callees
  retry only idempotent operations       or carry an idempotency key
  exponential backoff with jitter        without jitter, retries synchronize into
                                         a thundering herd that prevents recovery
  cap total retries and total time       infinite retry converts a blip into an
                                         outage by saturating the recovering node
  circuit breaker on sustained failure   stop calling a dead dependency; fail fast
  distinguish retryable from terminal    a 400 will fail identically forever

Jitter deserves its own emphasis because its absence causes a specific and severe
failure mode. When a service becomes briefly unavailable, every client fails at
approximately the same moment. With deterministic backoff they all retry at the same
moment, producing a synchronized load spike that knocks the service down again just as
it recovers, repeating indefinitely. Adding uniform random jitter — full jitter, where
the delay is uniform on [0, backoff], is the standard recommendation — spreads the
retries and lets the service recover. This is a one-line change that repeatedly
determines whether an incident lasts two minutes or two hours.

Q: A service adds retries with exponential backoff to improve reliability, and its
availability during dependency blips gets worse. The most likely cause is:
A. The backoff multiplier is too large
B. Retries without jitter synchronize clients into a thundering herd
C. The timeout is too long
D. Retries are not idempotent
Reasoning: Synchronized retries concentrate load at the exact moments the dependency
is attempting to recover, amplifying rather than absorbing the failure. Full jitter
spreads them. Option D is a real hazard but produces incorrect data rather than reduced
availability. Answer: B.

## Consistency Models

The CAP theorem states that a system experiencing a network partition must choose
between consistency and availability. It is widely cited and widely misread. The
choice only applies *during a partition*: a system is not obliged to sacrifice
anything when the network is healthy, so "CP" and "AP" describe partition behaviour
rather than steady-state properties. Nor is availability binary — real systems
degrade specific operations rather than going wholly down. PACELC extends the
statement usefully: during a partition, choose availability or consistency; else, in
normal operation, choose latency or consistency. That second clause is the one that
governs day-to-day design, because the cost of strong consistency is paid on every
request, not only during rare partitions.

Consistency models form a hierarchy from strongest to weakest. Linearizability, the
strongest, means every operation appears to take effect instantaneously at some point
between its invocation and its response, and all clients observe a single consistent
order consistent with real time. Sequential consistency drops the real-time constraint:
all clients see the same order, but it need not match wall-clock ordering. Causal
consistency guarantees only that causally related operations are seen in order, while
concurrent operations may be seen differently by different observers. Eventual
consistency promises only that replicas converge if updates stop, saying nothing about
what is observed meanwhile.

The weaker models are not merely compromises; they buy availability and latency that
strong models cannot offer, and many applications genuinely do not need more. A social
feed, a view counter, a DNS record, a product catalogue — all tolerate stale reads.
Account balances, inventory decrements, and unique-username registration do not. The
design skill is identifying which operations in a system need which guarantee, since
applying the strictest requirement uniformly is expensive and applying the loosest
uniformly is incorrect.

Consistency guarantees and typical mechanisms:
  linearizable            consensus (Raft/Paxos), single leader, quorum reads
  sequential              total order broadcast
  causal                  vector clocks, version vectors, dependency tracking
  read-your-writes        session stickiness, or read from the primary after write
  monotonic reads         pin a session to one replica
  bounded staleness       replica lag threshold with fallback to the primary
  eventual                asynchronous replication, anti-entropy, CRDTs

Session guarantees are the pragmatic middle ground and are underused. Read-your-writes
means a user who posts a comment sees it immediately, even if other users do not for a
second — which is almost always what "consistency" means to a product manager.
Implementing it by routing a user's reads to the primary for a few seconds after their
write, or by passing a version token the replica waits for, delivers the perceptible
benefit of strong consistency at a small fraction of the cost.

Conflict-free replicated data types sidestep coordination entirely by constraining the
data structure so that concurrent updates merge deterministically. A grow-only counter,
an add-only set, a last-writer-wins register, an observed-remove set: each defines a
merge operation that is commutative, associative, and idempotent, so replicas
converge regardless of message order or duplication. The cost is expressiveness — not
every operation can be phrased this way, and metadata can grow — but for collaborative
editing, presence, and distributed counters they eliminate coordination completely.

## Consensus and Replication

Consensus is the problem of getting a set of nodes to agree on a single value despite
failures, and it is the foundation on which linearizable systems are built. The FLP
impossibility result proves that in a fully asynchronous system with even one faulty
process, no deterministic algorithm guarantees consensus — there is always a schedule
that prevents termination. Practical systems evade this with partial synchrony
assumptions and randomized timeouts: they guarantee safety always and liveness only
during periods when the network behaves, which is the right trade because an
occasional extra election is tolerable and disagreement is not.

Raft is the consensus algorithm most commonly implemented because it was explicitly
designed for understandability. It elects a single leader by randomized timeout, and
the leader accepts all writes, appends them to a replicated log, and commits an entry
once a majority have persisted it. Terms provide logical time, so a stale leader
returning after a partition discovers a higher term and steps down. Safety rests on the
majority-overlap property: any two majorities of an odd-sized cluster share at least
one member, so a newly elected leader necessarily has every committed entry.

Cluster sizing follows directly from majority quorums. A cluster of 2f + 1 nodes
tolerates f failures, so three nodes tolerate one and five tolerate two. Even-sized
clusters are strictly worse than the odd size below them: four nodes still need a
majority of three, tolerating only one failure while adding a machine that can fail.
Larger clusters improve fault tolerance and worsen write latency, since every commit
waits for a majority, which is why production clusters are almost always three or five
and scale is achieved by partitioning across many such groups rather than by growing
one.

Replication topologies:
  single leader        all writes to one node, reads from any; simple, the default
                       for relational databases; failover is the hard part
  multi-leader         writes accepted at several nodes; needed for multi-region
                       write locality and offline clients; conflicts are inevitable
                       and must be resolved explicitly
  leaderless (Dynamo)  clients write to W and read from R of N replicas; strong
                       consistency when R + W > N; sloppy quorums and hinted
                       handoff trade consistency for availability
  chain replication    writes at the head, reads at the tail; strong consistency
                       with high throughput, poor tail latency during failures

Replication lag is where correctness bugs concentrate in single-leader systems. A user
writes to the leader and immediately reads from a replica that has not yet applied the
change, and their own update appears to have vanished. The fixes are read-your-writes
routing, monotonic-read session pinning so a user never moves to a more stale replica,
and explicit lag monitoring with automatic removal of replicas beyond a threshold. The
failure is intermittent and load-dependent, which makes it hard to reproduce and easy
to dismiss as a client bug.

Split-brain is the corresponding hazard in failover. A leader that is merely slow, not
dead, may be declared failed and replaced, leaving two nodes believing they are the
leader and accepting conflicting writes. Fencing tokens are the standard remedy: every
leader receives a monotonically increasing token, and the storage layer rejects any
write bearing a token lower than the highest it has seen, so the deposed leader's
writes are refused even if it never learns it was deposed. Systems that rely on a lease
timing out without a fencing mechanism are relying on clocks, which is exactly what
distributed systems cannot do.

[user] Our Postgres read replicas are 4-8 seconds behind under load and users report
their profile edits disappearing on refresh.
[assistant] tool_call db.replication_status({"cluster": "prod-primary",
"replicas": 3, "metric_window": "1h"})
[tool] {"max_lag_seconds": 8.4, "mean_lag_seconds": 4.1, "wal_generation_mb_s": 42,
"replica_apply_mb_s": 38, "longest_query_on_replica_s": 61,
"conflicts": 0, "verdict": "apply_bound_plus_long_queries"}
[assistant] Two separate problems, and the user-visible one has a fast fix.

The disappearing edits are a read-your-writes violation, not data loss. The user
writes to the primary, is redirected to a replica for the subsequent read, and the
replica has not applied the change yet. Nothing is lost — it appears a few seconds
later. Fix this at the routing layer rather than by chasing the lag: after a write,
pin that user's session to the primary for a window longer than your worst observed
lag, say 15 seconds. The more precise version is to capture the primary's WAL LSN at
write time, pass it back to the client, and have the replica wait until it has applied
that LSN before serving the read — Postgres exposes this well and it gives correctness
rather than a heuristic timeout.

Separately, the lag itself is real and worth fixing. Your primary generates 42 MB/s of
WAL and replicas apply at 38 MB/s, so they cannot keep up and lag grows monotonically
under sustained load. Compounding it, you have a 61-second query running on a replica;
recovery conflicts with long read queries either pause apply or cancel the query
depending on your `max_standby_streaming_delay` setting, and pausing apply is what
turns a 4-second lag into an 8-second one.

In priority order: move that long analytical query off the serving replicas onto a
dedicated one with a permissive delay setting, then reduce WAL volume at the source —
the usual culprits are unbatched small writes, an over-indexed hot table, and full-page
writes after checkpoints, so check `pg_stat_statements` for write amplification. If WAL
volume is genuinely required, you need to partition the write load rather than tune it.

## Time, Ordering, and Idempotence

Physical clocks cannot be trusted for ordering. NTP-synchronized clocks in a datacentre
typically agree within a few milliseconds but can diverge by far more during network
disruption, and clocks jump backward when corrected — which is why comparing wall-clock
timestamps to determine which of two events happened first is unsound, and why
measuring elapsed time requires a monotonic clock rather than a wall clock. Last-writer-
wins conflict resolution based on wall-clock timestamps silently discards writes when
clocks are skewed, and the discarded write is the one from the node whose clock is
behind, which is not a property anyone intended.

Logical clocks order events by causality instead. A Lamport clock is a counter
incremented on each local event and advanced to max(local, received) + 1 on message
receipt; it guarantees that if A causally precedes B then clock(A) < clock(B), but not
the converse, so it cannot distinguish causality from coincidence. Vector clocks carry
one counter per node and can distinguish causally related from concurrent events
exactly, at the cost of size linear in the number of nodes. Hybrid logical clocks
combine a physical timestamp with a logical counter, giving causally consistent
ordering that also stays close to wall-clock time, which makes them practical for
systems that need both.

Google's Spanner takes the opposite approach with TrueTime: rather than avoiding
physical clocks, it bounds their uncertainty using GPS and atomic clocks, exposing an
interval rather than an instant. A transaction commits by waiting out the uncertainty
window, typically a few milliseconds, guaranteeing that its timestamp is genuinely in
the past for every observer. This buys externally consistent distributed transactions
at the cost of specialized hardware and a deliberate commit-wait latency — an explicit
purchase of consistency with latency, exactly as PACELC describes.

Exactly-once delivery does not exist at the transport layer; exactly-once *processing*
is achievable and is what systems actually mean. The construction is at-least-once
delivery plus idempotent handling. Idempotence is obtained by carrying a unique key
with each request and having the receiver record processed keys, rejecting duplicates;
by making the operation naturally idempotent (set balance to X rather than add Y); or
by transactionally coupling the side effect with the record of having performed it.

Messaging delivery semantics:
  at-most-once      fire and forget; fast, loses messages on failure
  at-least-once     retry until acknowledged; duplicates are guaranteed eventually
  exactly-once      at-least-once plus consumer-side idempotence or a transactional
                    write; not a transport property
  ordered           per-partition ordering is achievable; global ordering across
                    partitions is not, and demanding it eliminates parallelism
  dead-letter queue where poison messages go after N failures, so one bad message
                    cannot block a partition indefinitely

The dual-write problem is the most common source of inconsistency in service
architectures: a handler writes to its database and then publishes an event, and the
process crashes between the two. The database is updated and no event exists, or the
event is published and the transaction rolls back. There is no ordering of the two
writes that fixes this. The correct solutions couple them atomically — the transactional
outbox, where the event is written to a table in the same transaction and a separate
relay publishes it, or change data capture, where the event stream is derived from the
database's replication log so it cannot diverge by construction.

Q: A payment service uses at-least-once delivery and processes each message by charging
a card. To achieve exactly-once semantics it should:
A. Switch the broker to exactly-once mode
B. Deduplicate by an idempotency key recorded transactionally with the charge
C. Use at-most-once delivery instead
D. Add a longer visibility timeout
Reasoning: No broker configuration makes end-to-end exactly-once possible, because the
acknowledgement itself can be lost after the side effect. The receiver must make
processing idempotent, and the deduplication record must commit atomically with the
charge — recording it separately reintroduces the same crash window. Answer: B.

Q: Two nodes' wall clocks differ by 300 ms. A last-writer-wins store resolves a
conflict between concurrent writes. The outcome is:
A. The genuinely later write is kept
B. The write from the node with the faster clock is kept, regardless of real order
C. Both writes are preserved
D. The write is rejected
Reasoning: LWW compares timestamps, so it selects whichever node reported a larger
number. With 300 ms of skew, a write that really happened later on the slow-clocked
node loses and is silently discarded. This is why LWW needs either bounded clock
uncertainty or logical clocks. Answer: B.

## Partitioning, Caching, and Resilience

Partitioning splits data across nodes so that capacity scales horizontally. Range
partitioning keeps keys in sorted order, which makes range scans efficient and creates
hotspots when the access pattern is skewed toward recent keys — timestamps as the
leading component of a partition key is the classic mistake, funnelling all current
writes to one partition. Hash partitioning distributes uniformly and destroys range
locality. Consistent hashing places nodes and keys on a ring so that adding or
removing a node relocates only 1/n of keys rather than remapping everything, and
virtual nodes smooth the imbalance that arises from placing few nodes on the ring.

Hot keys defeat any partitioning scheme, because a single key cannot be split by
definition. A celebrity account, a viral product, a global configuration row: all
concentrate load on one partition regardless of how well the rest is distributed. The
mitigations are read replicas for that key, key salting where the key is split into N
sub-keys that are aggregated on read (which trades write simplicity for read
complexity), or a dedicated cache layer in front of the hot subset.

Caching is the highest-leverage and most bug-prone optimization in the field. The core
tension is that a cache is a second copy of data, and two copies can disagree. Cache
invalidation strategies each fail differently: TTL-only is simple and serves stale data
for up to the TTL; write-through updates the cache on write and can leave it
inconsistent if the two writes are not atomic; write-behind buffers writes and risks
loss; explicit invalidation is precise and misses cases that the code did not
anticipate. There is no strategy without a failure mode, so the design question is
which staleness is acceptable rather than how to eliminate it.

Three cache failure patterns worth naming:
  cache stampede      an expired hot key causes every concurrent request to miss and
                      recompute simultaneously. Fix with a mutex or single-flight so
                      one request recomputes while others wait, or probabilistic early
                      expiry so refreshes spread out before the TTL.
  cache penetration   requests for keys that do not exist bypass the cache every time
                      and hit the database. Fix by caching negative results, or with a
                      Bloom filter over existing keys.
  cache avalanche     many keys expire at once, typically because they were populated
                      together at deploy or restart. Fix with randomized TTL jitter.

Resilience patterns compose into a defensive posture. The circuit breaker tracks the
failure rate of a dependency and, above a threshold, stops issuing calls entirely for a
cooldown period, then admits a trial trickle before closing — converting a slow cascade
into fast local failures and giving the dependency room to recover. Bulkheads isolate
resources per dependency, so a thread pool exhausted by one slow downstream cannot
starve calls to healthy ones. Load shedding rejects excess requests at the edge with a
cheap error rather than accepting work that will time out anyway, which keeps latency
bounded for the requests that are served. Backpressure propagates capacity limits
upstream instead of allowing unbounded queues, since an unbounded queue converts an
overload into an unbounded latency increase and eventually an out-of-memory failure.

Observability is what makes all of this operable. The three signals are complementary:
metrics are cheap aggregates good for alerting and trends but cannot explain a single
request; logs are detailed and expensive and answer questions about specific events;
distributed traces follow one request across service boundaries and are the only signal
that answers "where did the latency go" in a system of thirty services. Percentiles
matter far more than averages, because the mean is dominated by the common case while
users experience the tail — and in a request that fans out to a hundred backends, the
overall latency is governed by the slowest of the hundred, so a p99 backend latency
becomes a near-certainty at the fan-out level. Tail tolerance, not average
optimization, is what makes large systems feel fast.
