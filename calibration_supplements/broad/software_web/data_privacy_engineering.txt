## Inventory, Classification, and Data Flow

Privacy engineering starts with knowing what data exists, where it lives, where it goes, and why it
is held. Most organizations do not know this, and the discovery exercise consistently finds personal
data in places nobody expected — log files, analytics events, error reports, backups, test fixtures
copied from production, and third-party services receiving more than anyone intended.

Classification assigns sensitivity levels and determines what handling each requires. The categories
that matter operationally are direct identifiers, which name a person; indirect identifiers, which
identify in combination; sensitive categories with elevated legal protection such as health,
biometrics, and precise location; and derived data, which is inferred rather than collected and
which people are frequently more surprised by than by anything they provided.

Data flow mapping traces each category from collection through every system that processes it to
every destination. It is tedious and it is the artifact everything else depends on: a retention
policy cannot be enforced on data whose locations are unknown, a deletion request cannot be
fulfilled, and a breach cannot be scoped. The mapping should be maintained as systems change rather
than produced once for an audit, which requires it to be embedded in the development process rather
than performed by a separate function.

Inventory and classification:
  the starting point   what exists, where, where it goes, and why
  the discovery finding  personal data in logs, analytics, errors, backups, and
                       test fixtures
  third-party leakage  services receiving more than intended
  direct identifiers   name a person
  indirect identifiers  identify in combination
  sensitive categories  health, biometrics, precise location, and others with
                       elevated protection
  derived data         inferred rather than collected; often the most surprising
                       to people
  data flow mapping    collection through processing to destinations
  the dependency       retention, deletion, and breach scoping all require it
  maintenance          embedded in development, not produced for audits
  processing purpose   recorded per category; the basis of every other decision
  the legal basis      why processing is permitted; distinct from the purpose and
                       required in several regimes
  data controller and processor  who decides purposes and who acts on
                       instructions; the distinction determines obligations

Q: An organization receives a request to delete a user's data and finds the data in production, but
also in backups, analytics, a third-party service, and several exports. What does this reveal?
A. Poor engineering practice generally
B. That data flow mapping was incomplete — deletion can only be executed against known locations, so
   the inventory is the prerequisite capability rather than the deletion mechanism itself
C. That deletion is impossible
D. A legal problem rather than a technical one
Reasoning: Deletion is a straightforward operation once locations are known. The difficulty is
knowing them, which is why inventory and flow mapping are the foundational work and why building
deletion capability without them produces a system that deletes some copies and leaves others.
Answer: B.

## Minimization, Retention, and Purpose

Data minimization is the principle that collection should be limited to what is necessary for a
stated purpose, and it is the single most effective privacy control because data not collected
cannot be breached, misused, subpoenaed, or requested. It conflicts directly with the engineering
instinct to log everything in case it is useful later, and resolving that conflict requires the
purpose to be stated before the collection rather than reconstructed afterward.

Retention limits apply the same logic over time. Data held indefinitely accumulates risk without
accumulating value, since the utility of most operational data declines sharply within months while
its liability does not. Setting a retention period per data category, implementing automatic deletion
rather than relying on manual cleanup, and applying it to backups and derived copies is the practical
programme, and the automation is essential because manual retention enforcement does not happen.

Purpose limitation constrains use to the purposes for which data was collected, and it is the
principle most often violated inadvertently. Data collected for one function is convenient for
another, and the new use is proposed by someone with no visibility into the original basis. The
control is procedural: a review that asks whether a proposed use is within the stated purpose, and a
mechanism to obtain a new basis when it is not. Systems that make the original purpose visible at
the point of access make this far easier than systems that do not.

Minimization and retention:
  minimization         collect only what the purpose requires
  the effectiveness    uncollected data cannot be breached or misused
  the conflict         the instinct to log everything for later
  the resolution       state the purpose before collecting
  field-level review   each collected field justified individually
  aggregation at collection  storing a count rather than the events, where the
                       count is what is used
  retention periods    per category, with automatic enforcement
  the liability curve  utility declines faster than risk does
  automated deletion   manual cleanup does not happen
  backups              subject to the same policy and frequently exempted in
                       practice
  derived copies       exports, caches, and analytics stores
  purpose limitation   use constrained to the collection purpose
  the inadvertent violation  a convenient secondary use proposed without
                       visibility into the basis
  the procedural control  review, and a mechanism to obtain a new basis
  visible purpose      systems exposing the original purpose at access time make
                       compliance substantially easier

## Identifiers and De-identification

Identifier design has large downstream consequences and is decided early. A persistent global
identifier used across all contexts makes correlation trivial for anyone with access to more than
one dataset, including adversaries. Per-context identifiers, derived from a master identifier and a
context salt, allow linkage where it is needed and prevent it elsewhere, and the cost is that
cross-context linkage requires deliberate mechanism rather than being free.

De-identification removes or obscures identifying information, and the persistent finding of the
research literature is that it is much harder than it appears. Removing direct identifiers is
insufficient because combinations of quasi-identifiers — birth date, postal code, and gender being
the classic triple — uniquely identify a large fraction of a population. Re-identification attacks
against supposedly anonymized datasets have succeeded repeatedly, including against medical records,
movie ratings, mobility traces, and browsing histories.

The formal frameworks address this with varying strength. Generalization and suppression approaches
guarantee that each record is indistinguishable from a number of others on the quasi-identifiers,
which prevents unique identification and not attribute disclosure when the group shares a sensitive
value. Differential privacy provides a fundamentally stronger guarantee that holds regardless of what
auxiliary information an adversary has, which is the property the earlier approaches lack, and it
applies to computed outputs rather than to released records.

Identifiers and de-identification:
  persistent global identifiers  make correlation trivial
  per-context identifiers  derived from a master and a context salt
  the tradeoff         cross-context linkage requires deliberate mechanism
  pseudonymization     identifiers replaced with tokens; reversible with the map
  the legal position   pseudonymized data is generally still personal data
  quasi-identifiers    combinations identifying uniquely
  the classic triple   birth date, postal code, and gender
  re-identification attacks  successful against many released datasets
  generalization and suppression  coarsening values to create indistinguishable
                       groups
  the residual weakness  attribute disclosure when a group shares a sensitive
                       value
  differential privacy  a guarantee independent of auxiliary information
  the mechanism        calibrated noise on computed outputs
  the scope difference  it protects computations, not released record sets
  the utility cost     real, and concentrated on rare values and small groups
  the practical position  aggregate release with formal guarantees is defensible;
                       record-level release described as anonymous usually is not,
                       and the burden should be on demonstrating the claim rather
                       than asserting it

## Access Control, Consent, and Rights

Access control for personal data goes beyond the usual authorization model because the relevant
question is not only whether an identity may access a resource but whether this access, for this
purpose, at this time, is appropriate. Role-based access is the baseline; purpose-based access adds
the requirement that the accessor's task justifies it; and just-in-time access with expiry replaces
standing privileges that accumulate and are never revoked.

Access logging is the control that makes the others meaningful. A log recording who accessed which
records, when, and under what justification supports both detection of inappropriate access and the
investigation that follows a report. Its effectiveness depends on someone reviewing it, which is why
automated anomaly detection — an accessor viewing records outside their normal pattern — is what
converts a log from a forensic artifact into a control.

Consent, where it is the basis for processing, must be specific, informed, freely given, and as easy
to withdraw as to give. The engineering consequences are concrete: consent state must be recorded
with its scope and timestamp, processing must check it, withdrawal must propagate to every system
including downstream recipients, and the absence of consent must be a functioning code path rather
than an untested branch. Subject rights — access, correction, deletion, portability, objection —
similarly require implemented capability, and a rights process that depends on engineers running
manual queries does not scale and does not reliably complete.

Access, consent, and rights:
  the extended question  whether this access for this purpose is appropriate
  role-based access    the baseline
  purpose-based access  the task justifying the access
  just-in-time access  granted for a period and expiring
  standing privilege accumulation  the default failure of role-based systems
  access logging       who, what, when, and why
  review               what makes logging a control rather than an artifact
  anomaly detection    access outside an accessor's normal pattern
  consent requirements  specific, informed, freely given, easily withdrawn
  consent state        recorded with scope and timestamp
  enforcement          processing checks it
  withdrawal propagation  to every system including downstream recipients
  the untested branch  the no-consent path must actually work
  subject rights       access, correction, deletion, portability, objection
  implemented capability  a manual process does not scale or reliably complete
  identity verification  for rights requests, and it must not itself become a
                       data collection or an attack vector

## Cryptographic Techniques and Practice

Encryption protects data in specific states and it is worth being precise about which. Encryption in
transit protects against network interception. Encryption at rest protects against physical media
compromise and is largely irrelevant to an attacker with application-level access, which is the more
common threat. Application-level encryption, where the application encrypts specific fields before
storage, protects against database compromise and against overly broad internal access, and it is
the layer that actually addresses most realistic threat models.

Key management determines whether encryption provides anything. Keys stored beside the data they
protect provide nothing; keys in a dedicated management service with access control, rotation, and
audit provide real separation. Envelope encryption — encrypting data with a data key and the data key
with a master key — is the standard pattern, allowing key rotation without re-encrypting the data.
And the availability question matters as much as the confidentiality one: a lost key is
indistinguishable from destroyed data.

Privacy-enhancing computation techniques allow useful work on data without exposing it. Secure
multi-party computation lets parties jointly compute a function on their combined inputs without
revealing them. Homomorphic encryption permits computation on encrypted data directly. Private set
intersection finds common elements between two sets without revealing the rest. These are real and
deployed for specific applications, and they carry computational costs from substantial to enormous,
so they are chosen when the alternative is not computing at all rather than as general replacements
for ordinary processing.

Cryptography and practice:
  in transit           against network interception
  at rest              against physical media compromise
  the limitation       at-rest encryption does not stop application-level access
  application-level encryption  specific fields encrypted before storage
  what it addresses    database compromise and overly broad internal access
  key management       the determinant of whether encryption means anything
  co-located keys      provide nothing
  envelope encryption  a data key encrypted by a master key
  rotation             without re-encrypting the underlying data
  the availability risk  a lost key is destroyed data
  secure multi-party computation  joint computation without revealing inputs
  homomorphic encryption  computation on encrypted data
  private set intersection  common elements without revealing the rest
  the cost              substantial to enormous; chosen when the alternative is
                       not computing at all
  privacy by design    controls built in rather than added; the difference is
                       usually an order of magnitude in cost
  privacy impact assessment  a structured review before building; its value is in
                       surfacing decisions early enough to change them, which is
                       precisely why it loses value when performed as a
                       compliance step after the design is fixed
