## Measurement and Methodology

Performance work that begins without measurement is guessing, and the consistent experience
of the field is that intuition about where time goes is unreliable even for people who wrote
the code. The bottleneck is regularly somewhere nobody suspected, and the parts that look
expensive are frequently not on the critical path at all.

Amdahl's law bounds what optimization can achieve. If a fraction of the work is
unparallelizable or unoptimizable, the total speedup is limited by that fraction regardless of
how much the rest improves. Optimizing a component that consumes ten percent of runtime to
take zero time yields an eleven percent improvement at most, which is why identifying the
dominant cost before optimizing anything is not merely good practice but the difference
between a meaningful gain and a wasted week.

The distinction between latency and throughput is fundamental and frequently conflated.
Latency is the time for one operation; throughput is operations per unit time. They are not
reciprocals in any system with parallelism or pipelining, and optimizing one often degrades
the other — batching improves throughput by amortizing overhead and increases latency by
making requests wait for a batch to fill. Deciding which matters for the workload is a
prerequisite to optimizing either.

Measurement principles:
  measure first          intuition about bottlenecks is unreliable
  Amdahl's law           speedup bounded by the unoptimized fraction
  latency vs throughput  not reciprocals; frequently in tension
  percentiles            report p50, p95, p99; the mean hides the tail users feel
  the tail matters       in a request fanning out to N backends, the slowest of N
                         dominates, so p99 backend latency becomes typical at the
                         aggregate level
  warm-up                JIT compilation, cache filling, and connection
                         establishment mean early iterations are unrepresentative
  variance               report distributions, not single runs; performance
                         measurements are noisy
  representative load    benchmark with realistic data sizes and access patterns;
                         synthetic uniform data hides cache and branch behaviour
  isolate the change     one variable at a time, or attribution is impossible
  regression testing     automated benchmarks in CI catch gradual degradation that
                         nobody notices in any single change

The tail-at-scale effect deserves particular emphasis because it is counterintuitive and
governs the behaviour of distributed systems. If a request must contact one hundred backends
and each has a 99th-percentile latency of one second, the probability that at least one of the
hundred is slow approaches certainty, so the aggregate request's typical latency is set by
that backend p99 rather than by its median. This is why reducing tail latency is far more
valuable than reducing median latency in fanned-out architectures, and why techniques
targeting the tail specifically — hedged requests, tied requests, micro-partitioning — exist.

Q: A service's mean response time is 40 ms and its p99 is 2.5 seconds. Each user request fans
out to 50 such calls. The typical user-perceived latency will be closest to:
A. 40 ms   B. 2 seconds   C. 200 ms   D. 2.5 seconds times 50
Reasoning: With 50 independent calls each having a 1% chance of exceeding 2.5 seconds, the
probability that at least one does is 1 - 0.99^50, about 39%. Combined with the tail's shape,
the typical aggregate is dominated by the slowest call rather than the mean, landing on the
order of a second or two. The mean of the individual calls is almost irrelevant to the user
experience. Answer: B.

## Complexity, Data Structures, and Algorithms

Asymptotic complexity describes how cost grows with input size and is the first filter on
algorithmic choice, with the important caveat that constants and lower-order terms dominate at
practical sizes. An O(n log n) algorithm with a large constant loses to an O(n^2) algorithm for
small n, which is why production sorting implementations switch to insertion sort below a
threshold of a few dozen elements.

The gap between asymptotic analysis and measured performance is largely explained by memory
behaviour, which the standard model ignores. Big-O counts operations and treats all memory
access as equal cost, and on real hardware a cache-resident access is a hundred times faster
than a main-memory one. This is why an array frequently outperforms a linked list for
traversal despite identical asymptotic complexity, why a flat array of structures may
outperform a tree for small collections, and why algorithms designed for cache behaviour beat
theoretically superior ones.

Data structure selection follows from the operation mix, and the practical guidance is to
identify which operations dominate before choosing. A structure with excellent insertion and
poor iteration is wrong for a workload that builds once and reads many times, and the reverse
holds for a workload that mutates constantly.

Structures and their operation costs:
  array / vector       O(1) index, O(n) insert in middle, excellent locality
  linked list          O(1) insert given a node, O(n) search, terrible locality;
                       rarely the right choice on modern hardware
  hash table           O(1) average lookup, O(n) worst case, unordered, poor
                       locality on collision-heavy loads
  balanced tree        O(log n) for everything, ordered traversal, decent locality
  B-tree               O(log n) with a high branching factor; designed for
                       block-oriented storage, and equally beneficial for cache
  heap                 O(1) peek, O(log n) insert and extract; priority queues
  trie                 prefix operations; memory-hungry
  bloom filter         probabilistic membership; no false negatives, tunable false
                       positive rate, very compact
  ring buffer          fixed capacity, O(1) both ends, excellent locality
  sorted array         better than a tree for read-heavy workloads; binary search
                       with perfect locality

Amortized analysis explains why a dynamic array's append is considered constant time despite
occasionally requiring a full reallocation and copy. Doubling the capacity on each growth means
the total copying work across n appends is bounded by 2n, so the average per append is
constant even though individual operations occasionally are not. The distinction matters when
worst-case latency is a requirement rather than average throughput — a real-time system cannot
tolerate the occasional expensive operation, which is why such systems preallocate.

## Memory Hierarchy

The memory hierarchy is the dominant factor in single-threaded performance on modern hardware,
and the reason is a widening gap: processor speed improved far faster than memory latency for
decades, so a main-memory access now costs on the order of two hundred cycles during which the
processor could have executed hundreds of instructions.

Caches exploit locality. Temporal locality means recently accessed data is likely to be
accessed again; spatial locality means data near recently accessed data is likely to be
accessed. Caches are organized in lines of typically 64 bytes, so accessing one byte loads its
64-byte neighbourhood, which is why sequential access is dramatically faster than random —
sequential access uses every byte of each loaded line while random access uses one.

The practical consequences reshape data structure design. An array of structures places all
fields of one element together, which suits code accessing whole elements. A structure of
arrays places each field contiguously, which suits code accessing one field across many
elements — and for that access pattern it can be several times faster, because the cache lines
contain only useful data rather than being mostly the fields being skipped. This is why
columnar layouts dominate analytical processing and why data-oriented design is a recurring
theme in high-performance code.

Memory hierarchy characteristics:
  register             sub-nanosecond; a few hundred bytes
  L1 cache             ~1 ns, ~4 cycles; 32-64 KB
  L2 cache             ~4 ns, ~14 cycles; 256 KB - 2 MB
  L3 cache             ~15 ns, ~40 cycles; shared, 8-64 MB
  main memory          ~80-100 ns, ~200-300 cycles
  SSD                  ~50-100 us
  disk                 ~5-10 ms
  cache line           64 bytes typically; the unit of transfer
  sequential access    prefetchers detect the pattern; near peak bandwidth
  random access        one useful byte per line; an order of magnitude slower
  false sharing        two threads writing different variables on the same line
                       cause the line to bounce between cores; a severe and
                       invisible scalability killer
  alignment            unaligned access crosses lines; pad hot structures
  working set          if it fits in cache, everything changes; the largest single
                       performance cliff in most programs
  TLB                  virtual-to-physical translation cache; large working sets
                       thrash it, and huge pages are the remedy

False sharing deserves specific attention because it is invisible in the source code and can
eliminate the benefit of parallelism entirely. Two threads updating adjacent variables that
happen to share a cache line cause that line to be invalidated and transferred between cores on
every write, so a program that should scale linearly instead slows down as threads are added.
The fix is padding so that per-thread data occupies separate cache lines, and the diagnostic is
that adding threads makes the program slower rather than faster while each thread's work is
genuinely independent.

[user] My data processing loop takes 4 seconds for 10 million records and I expected under
one. It's a simple sum over one field.
[assistant] tool_call perf.profile({"records": 10000000, "runtime_s": 4.1,
"layout": "array_of_structs", "struct_bytes": 192, "field_bytes": 8,
"access": "sequential", "cache_miss_rate": 0.94, "ipc": 0.31})
[tool] {"bytes_touched_gb": 1.92, "bytes_useful_gb": 0.08, "efficiency": 0.042,
"bound": "memory_bandwidth", "recommendation": "structure_of_arrays",
"expected_runtime_s": 0.28}
[assistant] You are reading 1.92 GB of memory to use 80 MB of it. The loop is
memory-bandwidth-bound, and 96% of the bandwidth is spent on fields you never touch.

Your records are 192 bytes each, and you are summing one 8-byte field. Because the cache line
is 64 bytes and your access stride is 192, every record requires loading at least one full
line to extract 8 useful bytes. The 94% cache miss rate and the instructions-per-cycle of 0.31
both confirm this — the processor is stalled waiting for memory nearly all the time.

Restructure to a structure of arrays: store each field in its own contiguous array rather than
storing whole records contiguously. Summing one field then reads a dense 80 MB array with
perfect spatial locality, every byte of every cache line is used, and the hardware prefetcher
recognizes the sequential pattern and stays ahead of the loop. Expect roughly 0.3 seconds,
which is about a fourteenfold improvement from a layout change with no algorithmic difference
at all.

If restructuring the whole record type is impractical because other code needs whole records,
two intermediate options exist. Extract the hot field into a parallel array maintained
alongside, accepting the bookkeeping cost of keeping them synchronized. Or split the struct
into a hot portion accessed in tight loops and a cold portion accessed rarely, which preserves
the record abstraction while improving locality for the common path.

Once memory-bound behaviour is fixed, vectorization becomes worthwhile — the compiler can
process four or eight elements per instruction on a dense array and cannot do so on a strided
one. That may give another factor of two to four on top. Doing it before fixing the layout
gains nothing, because the bottleneck is bandwidth rather than arithmetic.

## Concurrency and Parallelism

Concurrency and parallelism are distinct: concurrency is dealing with multiple things at
once, which is a structuring concern, and parallelism is doing multiple things at once, which
is an execution concern. A single-threaded event loop is concurrent and not parallel. The
distinction matters because the right technique depends on whether the workload waits or
computes.

I/O-bound work waits, and the goal is to overlap the waiting. Asynchronous programming or
lightweight threads let a single core manage thousands of in-flight operations, because a
waiting operation costs a small structure rather than a thread stack. Adding more threads
beyond a modest number provides nothing, since they would all be waiting too.

CPU-bound work computes, and the goal is to use more cores. The parallelism should be sized
near the core count, since more threads than cores merely add context-switching overhead. The
practical limits are Amdahl's law bounding speedup by the sequential fraction, and
synchronization overhead which can exceed the work being parallelized for fine-grained tasks.

Concurrency mechanisms and hazards:
  mutex                 mutual exclusion; simple, correct, serializes access
  read-write lock       concurrent readers; helps read-heavy workloads and costs
                        more than a mutex when contention is low
  atomic operations     lock-free single-word updates; the building block
  compare-and-swap      the primitive underlying most lock-free structures
  memory ordering       relaxed, acquire-release, sequentially consistent; the
                        weaker orderings are faster and much harder to reason about
  lock-free             progress guaranteed for some thread; difficult to write
                        correctly and often slower than a good mutex under low
                        contention
  contention            the dominant cost; reduce by sharding, by per-thread data,
                        or by shrinking critical sections
  deadlock              cyclic waiting; prevent by consistent lock ordering
  livelock              threads active and making no progress
  priority inversion    a high-priority thread blocked by a low-priority lock
                        holder; fixed by priority inheritance
  false sharing         padding is the remedy
  work stealing         idle threads take tasks from busy ones; the standard
                        scheduler for irregular parallel workloads

The recurring practical advice is that the fastest synchronization is the synchronization not
performed. Partitioning data so that each thread owns its portion exclusively eliminates
contention entirely and scales linearly, where any shared mutable state introduces a
serialization point that bounds scalability. Where sharing is unavoidable, reducing the
critical section to the minimum and moving computation outside it is worth more than choosing a
cleverer lock. Lock-free structures are appropriate when contention is genuinely high and
measured, and they are difficult enough to get right that reaching for them first is usually a
mistake.

## Profiling and Systems Tuning

Profilers come in two families that answer different questions. Sampling profilers interrupt
periodically and record the stack, producing a statistical picture with low overhead and
without distorting the program's behaviour — which is what makes them appropriate for
production. Instrumenting profilers record every call, giving exact counts and substantial
overhead that changes the behaviour being measured, particularly for small frequently called
functions where the instrumentation cost exceeds the function's own.

Flame graphs visualize sampled stacks with width proportional to time, which makes the
dominant paths immediately visible in a way that a sorted function list does not, because they
show the calling context. A function appearing expensive in a flat profile may be cheap per
call and invoked from one hot path, and the flame graph shows which caller to address.

Beyond the application, systems-level tools identify whether the bottleneck is even in the
program. A process that is blocked on I/O, starved of memory bandwidth, throttled by the
scheduler, or waiting on a lock in a library shows low CPU utilization and slow progress
simultaneously, and the application profiler will show it waiting without saying why.

Profiling and system tools:
  sampling profiler     low overhead, statistical, production-safe
  instrumenting         exact counts, high overhead, distorts small functions
  flame graph           stacks with width by time; shows calling context
  differential profile  compare two profiles to attribute a regression
  hardware counters     cache misses, branch mispredictions, IPC, stalls; identify
                        the microarchitectural cause
  IPC below ~1          usually memory-bound
  high branch misses    unpredictable control flow; consider branchless
                        formulations
  system tracing        syscalls, scheduling, I/O; find waiting rather than
                        computing
  allocation profiling  allocation is frequently the hidden dominant cost in
                        managed languages
  GC pressure           reduce allocation rate rather than tuning the collector
  I/O patterns          sequential versus random; readahead and buffering
  huge pages            reduce TLB pressure for large working sets
  NUMA                  memory attached to a remote socket costs substantially
                        more; pin threads near their data

The optimization sequence that reliably works is to establish a benchmark, profile to find the
dominant cost, address that cost, and re-measure — repeating until the remaining costs are
distributed rather than concentrated. Each step should be validated by measurement, because
changes that should help sometimes do not, for reasons ranging from the compiler already having
performed the optimization to the change having disturbed some other property such as inlining
or cache layout.

The final consideration is when to stop. Performance work has costs — in complexity, in
readability, in the risk of introducing bugs, and in the engineering time not spent on
something else — and those costs are paid permanently while the benefit may be marginal. The
right stopping point is when the performance meets a stated requirement, and having that
requirement stated in advance is what prevents optimization from becoming an unbounded
activity. Code that is fast enough and clear is a better outcome than code that is faster and
that nobody can safely modify.
