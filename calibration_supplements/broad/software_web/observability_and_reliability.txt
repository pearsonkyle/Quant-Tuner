## Signals and Instrumentation

Observability is the property that a system's internal state can be inferred from its outputs, and
it is distinguished from monitoring by the questions it supports. Monitoring answers questions
prepared in advance — is the error rate above threshold, is the queue growing. Observability answers
questions not anticipated, which requires that the emitted data carry enough dimensionality to slice
along attributes nobody thought to create a dashboard for.

The three primary signal types have different costs and different uses. Metrics are numeric
aggregates over time, cheap to store and query at any retention, and limited by cardinality — every
distinct combination of label values is a separate series, so a label containing user identifiers
will destroy the metric system. Logs are discrete records with arbitrary content, expensive at
volume, and irreplaceable for the specific detail of a single event. Traces record the path of a
request across services with timing at each step, and they are what answers the question of where the
latency actually went.

Structured logging is the practice that makes logs usable at scale. A log line that is a formatted
sentence must be parsed with regular expressions and breaks whenever the message changes; a log
record with named fields can be queried, filtered, and aggregated directly. The cost is a small
discipline at the call site, and the benefit is the difference between a searchable dataset and a
haystack.

  dimensionality       enough attributes to slice along

  logs                 discrete records; expensive at volume, irreplaceable in
                       detail

Q: An engineer adds a metric label containing the customer identifier to make per-customer
dashboards possible. What happens?
A. Nothing; labels are free
B. Cardinality explosion — each distinct label combination becomes a separate stored series, so a
   high-uniqueness label multiplies storage and query cost by the number of customers and commonly
   overwhelms the metrics backend
C. The metric becomes more accurate
D. Queries become faster
Reasoning: Metrics systems store one time series per unique label combination. Unbounded label
values are the standard way to take down a metrics backend. Per-entity detail belongs in logs or
traces, which are queried differently and priced differently. Answer: B.

## Service Level Objectives and Error Budgets

A service level indicator is a measurement of some aspect of service quality — the fraction of
requests served successfully, the fraction served within a latency threshold, the freshness of data.
A service level objective is a target for that indicator over a window. Together they convert
reliability from an aspiration into a number that can be measured, reasoned about, and traded
against other goals.

The objective should be set below one hundred percent deliberately, and the reasoning is important.
Perfect reliability is not achievable and pursuing it consumes resources with diminishing returns
while blocking every change that carries risk. Setting a target reflects a judgement about what
users actually need, and the gap between the target and perfection is the error budget: the amount of
unreliability the service is permitted to spend.

The error budget makes the tension between velocity and stability explicit and resolvable. A team
with budget remaining can ship risky changes; a team that has exhausted it stops feature work and
addresses reliability until the budget recovers. This converts an argument between two groups with
different incentives into a policy driven by measurement, which is its principal value — the numbers
do not settle what the right target is, and they do settle what follows once it is agreed.

Objectives and budgets:
  indicator            a measurement of service quality
  objective            a target for that indicator over a window
  the good indicators  success rate, latency threshold fraction, freshness
  user-centric measurement  measured where the user experiences it, not at the
                       server
  the target below perfection  deliberate, and the reasoning matters
  diminishing returns  each additional nine costs disproportionately more
  error budget         the permitted unreliability
  budget policy        remaining budget permits risk, exhausted budget stops
                       feature work
  the value            an argument converted into a measured policy
  what it does not settle  what the target should be; that is a product decision
  latency objectives   stated as a percentile at a threshold, never as an average
  the averages problem  a good average with a terrible tail is a bad service
  multi-window alerting  fast burn and slow burn detected separately
  dependency budgets   a service cannot be more reliable than its dependencies
                       unless it is designed to degrade, which is an architectural
                       requirement flowing directly from the target

## Alerting and On-Call

Alerting exists to bring a human's attention to something requiring it, and the failure mode is not
missing alerts but too many. An alerting system that pages frequently for conditions that resolve
themselves trains its recipients to ignore it, at which point it has negative value — the noise
conceals the signal and the responders are exhausted.

The discipline is that every page must be urgent, actionable, and important. Urgent means it cannot
wait until business hours. Actionable means there is something the recipient can do. Important means
the consequences of not acting are real. A condition failing any of these belongs in a ticket, a
dashboard, or a report, not in a page, and applying this test to an existing alert set typically
eliminates most of it.

Symptom-based alerting is more robust than cause-based alerting. Alerting on high error rates or
slow responses catches every cause including the ones not anticipated; alerting on a specific
condition like disk usage catches only that cause and fires when the condition is present without
consequences. The practical guidance is to page on symptoms the user experiences, and to use
cause-level signals for diagnosis after the page rather than as page triggers themselves.

Alerting practice:
  the failure mode     too many alerts, not too few
  alert fatigue        recipients trained to ignore; negative value
  the three tests      urgent, actionable, important
  where non-pages go   tickets, dashboards, reports
  symptom-based        catches unanticipated causes
  cause-based          catches only the anticipated one and fires without
                       consequence
  the guidance         page on symptoms, diagnose with causes
  runbooks             what to check and what to do; linked from the alert
  the runbook test     if the responder needs knowledge not in the runbook, the
                       runbook is incomplete
  alert review         periodic examination of what fired and whether it should
                       have
  on-call sustainability  the load must be humane or people leave and quality
                       falls
  handover             what is ongoing, what is degraded, what is being watched
  escalation           clear paths and clear expectations
  the follow-the-sun option  distributing on-call across time zones to avoid
                       night pages, which is the single largest improvement to
                       on-call quality of life where the organization is
                       distributed enough to support it

## Incident Response and Learning

An incident is an unplanned disruption, and the response has two distinct goals in a strict order:
restore service, then understand the cause. Conflating them lengthens outages, because diagnosis is
slower than mitigation and the pressure to explain what is happening competes with the work of
making it stop. Rolling back a recent change, failing over, shedding load, or disabling a feature
restores service without understanding, and understanding can follow.

Roles should be explicit during an incident. An incident commander coordinates and decides, without
performing the technical work themselves; responders investigate and act; a communications role
keeps stakeholders informed so that the responders are not interrupted. On a small incident one
person holds several roles, and naming them prevents the common failure where everyone investigates
and nobody coordinates or communicates.

The post-incident review is where the value is captured, and it is worth only as much as its
blamelessness. A review that identifies a person as the cause produces defensiveness and hiding; a
review that treats human error as a symptom of a system that permitted it produces changes that
prevent recurrence. The relevant question is never why someone did something but why the action
seemed reasonable at the time and why the system allowed it to have that consequence.

Incident response:
  the strict ordering  restore first, understand second
  mitigation options   roll back, fail over, shed load, disable a feature
  the rollback default  the fastest mitigation for change-induced incidents,
                       which are the majority
  incident commander   coordinates and decides; does not investigate
  responders           investigate and act
  communications       stakeholders informed without interrupting responders
  role naming          prevents everyone investigating and nobody coordinating
  the timeline         recorded during, not reconstructed after
  blameless review     human error as a symptom of the system
  the right question   why the action seemed reasonable at the time
  contributing factors  multiple, and the search for a single root cause is
                       usually a search for a place to stop looking
  action items         specific, owned, and tracked; a review producing none is a
                       retelling
  the prevention hierarchy  eliminate the possibility, detect it automatically,
                       or document the manual step; they differ by an order of
                       magnitude in effectiveness
  near misses          incidents that did not happen; the cheapest learning
                       available and almost never reviewed

## Failure Modes, Capacity, and Resilience

Distributed systems fail in characteristic ways worth recognizing on sight. Cascading failure occurs
when one component's failure increases load on others until they fail too, and retries are the usual
amplifier — a client retrying a failing service multiplies the load exactly when the service can
least handle it. Exponential backoff with jitter, circuit breakers that stop calling a failing
dependency, and bounded retry budgets are the standard defences.

Thundering herds occur when many clients act simultaneously — a cache expiring, a scheduled job
firing, a service restarting and every client reconnecting at once. Jitter is the general remedy:
spreading actions over a window converts a spike into a manageable rate. Metastable failure is the
subtler cousin, where a system enters a state that sustains itself after the trigger is removed,
which is why simply restoring the original conditions does not always restore the system and why
load shedding is sometimes necessary to break out.

Capacity planning and resilience testing are the proactive counterparts. Load testing establishes
where the limits are; the more informative test is finding the failure mode rather than the number,
since how a system fails at its limit determines whether the failure is a graceful degradation or a
collapse. Deliberately injecting failures in controlled conditions verifies that the redundancy and
fallback mechanisms work, which is the only way to know, because an untested failover path has a
high probability of not working when it is finally needed.

Failure modes and resilience:
  cascading failure    one failure increasing load until others fail
  retry amplification  clients multiplying load on a struggling service
  exponential backoff with jitter  the standard client-side defence
  circuit breaker      stop calling a failing dependency; fail fast
  retry budget         a bound on total retry load
  thundering herd      many clients acting simultaneously
  jitter               spreading actions over a window
  metastable failure   a self-sustaining bad state after the trigger is gone
  load shedding        dropping work deliberately to recover
  graceful degradation  reduced function rather than none
  backpressure         slowing intake rather than accepting unbounded work
  timeouts             on every call; and they must be shorter at each layer
                       going outward or the outer layer gives up while the inner
                       one is still trying
  load testing         the limit and, more usefully, the failure mode at it
  failure injection    verifying redundancy in controlled conditions
  the untested failover  has a high probability of not working when needed, which
                       is why the exercise is the point rather than the report
